# D3. "Indigenous and traditional peoples eat animals"

**Thesis:** the argument uses those with no alternative to justify those who have one. Veganism has never demanded the impossible: its classic definition speaks of avoiding the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable". What holds for an isolated people in the Arctic or the forest doesn't hold for someone choosing at the supermarket.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this chapter also answers "What about the Inuit, who have no alternative?", which was merged into it.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "What about Indigenous peoples? Are you going to tell them they're wrong? What about the Inuit, who have no alternative?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Indigenous and traditional peoples have eaten animals for thousands of years, often in ways integrated with their environment.
2. **P2.** It would be unjust and colonialist to condemn these peoples.
3. **P3.** If eating animals isn't wrong for them, it can't be wrong in itself.
4. **C.** So eating animals isn't wrong.

P1 and P2 are largely true. The problem lies in P3: what makes these peoples' situation acceptable is exactly what's missing for those who use the argument.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which generalizes from exceptional cases.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people live in very cold or remote places, where hardly any plants grow and there are no stores. For them, hunting may be the only way to survive. But most of us live near stores full of food. The question isn't about those who have no choice; it's about what we do when we have one.

## 4. Short answer

Veganism has never asked for the impossible: its classic definition speaks of avoiding the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable". An isolated people who depend on hunting to survive are in a completely different situation from someone buying meat at the supermarket. Using these peoples to justify one's own plate is using those with no choice to excuse those who have one.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you live in these peoples' situation, or is there a supermarket near your home?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument compares very different situations:

| Situation | Real alternatives | Responsibility |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An isolated people who depend on hunting or fishing to survive | Few or none | Greatly reduced |
| A city dweller with access to stores | Many | Full |

The argument uses the first case to draw conclusions about the second. It's a **hasty generalization from an exception**: an exception becomes the rule.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Veganism already allows for the exception.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". Someone with no alternative isn't violating that definition.

**2. Those who use the argument usually aren't in that situation.** It's generally used by city dwellers, with supermarkets and alternatives, who don't live like the peoples they cite. For them, those peoples' situation doesn't change the question.

**3. These peoples don't practice industrial animal farming.** Even when they eat animals, traditional peoples generally don't confine billions of animals, breed them to grow until they suffer or slaughter them on production lines. The comparison can't defend the system supermarket meat comes from.

**4. Many traditional peoples have practices of respect for animals.** Indigenous traditions vary widely, and several include limits, rituals and recognition of animal life. Treating all these peoples as proof that "eating animals is normal" is also a simplification.

**5. The point isn't to condemn these peoples.** Veganism is addressed to those who can choose. There's no contradiction between respecting those who live in extreme conditions and asking for change from those who don't.

**6. Using Indigenous peoples as an argument is a form of tokenization.** Often the person saying "vegans want to impose veganism on Indigenous peoples" is not Indigenous and does not usually defend those peoples' rights on any other issue. Veganism does not target specific groups: the focus is on large-scale industrial animal exploitation, not on Indigenous peoples or on groups in poor material conditions. The harm that really matters to Indigenous peoples, such as the loss of land, rivers and forests, comes precisely from cattle ranching and agribusiness.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: then veganism should be relative

The most serious version says that if veganism allows exceptions for some peoples, it isn't a universal moral obligation but a choice that depends on context, and so everyone can decide where their own exception lies.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Every ethics allows exceptions out of necessity.** Killing in self-defense doesn't turn homicide into a personal choice.
2. **The exception depends on facts, not preferences.** Either there's a reasonable alternative or there isn't. For someone with a supermarket, there is.
3. **Being sensitive to context isn't being relative.** The principle is the same for everyone; what changes is what's possible.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization from an exception | An exception becomes the rule for everyone |
| Logical | False analogy | Compares those with no choice to those with one |
| Epistemological | Simplifying cultures | Treats diverse peoples as a single block |
| Moral | Borrowed shield | Uses others' situation to justify one's own choice |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't live like the peoples they cite.** Not in diet, not in anything else.
2. **Doesn't defend these peoples' rights on other issues.** The same argument often comes from people who don't support Indigenous land rights.
3. **Buys meat from industry, not from hunting.** The comparison doesn't describe what the person does.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if something is acceptable for those with no alternative, it's acceptable for everyone".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stealing out of hunger | Stealing would be acceptable for anyone | No one accepts this |
| Self-defense | Any violence would be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Clearing land to survive | Large companies could clear forests freely | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Concern for Indigenous and traditional peoples is legitimate. Veganism shouldn't be used to judge those living in conditions of scarcity, and many of these peoples have relationships with nature that are more respectful than those of industrial society. Ignoring that would be arrogant.

But respecting these peoples isn't using them as a shield. What makes their situation understandable is the lack of an alternative, and that's exactly what isn't lacking for whoever asks the question.
