# J3. "Taxing meat or cutting subsidies hurts the poor"

**Thesis:** concern for people on low incomes is legitimate: taxes on food weigh more heavily on poor families' budgets. But meat is already artificially cheap. It receives subsidies and tax exemptions, and its price doesn't include the costs production passes on to society, such as pollution, emissions, antibiotic resistance and disease risk, nor the gravest cost, the suffering and death of animals. Protecting the poor doesn't require keeping this system: subsidy money can make beans, lentils and other plant proteins cheaper, and there are mechanisms to return taxes to low-income families.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "They want to raise taxes on meat? The rich will keep eating steak, and the poor will have no meat on their plate."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Taxes on food weigh proportionally more on poor families' budgets.
2. **P2.** Meat is an important source of protein and has strong cultural value.
3. **P3.** Taxing meat or cutting its subsidies would raise its price.
4. **C.** So taxing meat or cutting its subsidies would hurt the poor.

P1 is true, and it's the serious part of the argument. P2 is true as to cultural value, but protein can come from cheaper plant foods. P3 is true. The conclusion, however, assumes the policy amounts only to making meat more expensive, and ignores that the money can be redirected and that there are ways to compensate people on low incomes.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails in assuming there are no alternatives or compensations.

## 3. Answer for children

Some foods seem cheap because the government helps pay for them, and because nobody charges for the damage they do, to nature and to animals. That help could be used to make beans, lentils and other plant foods cheaper, so everyone eats well without animals suffering.

## 4. Short answer

Concern for the poor is fair. But meat is already artificially cheap: it receives subsidies and tax exemptions, and its price doesn't include pollution, emissions, health risks, or the suffering and death of animals. Worldwide, about 540 billion dollars a year go to supporting agricultural producers, and most of it distorts prices or causes harm. That money could make plant proteins cheaper, and there are ways to return taxes to low-income families. Protecting the poor and ending subsidies for exploitation aren't opposite choices.

**Question to ask back:** "If the goal is to feed the poor well, why should public money make meat cheaper, and not beans?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food taxes weigh more on the poor | Consumption taxes tend to be regressive | There are compensation mechanisms |
| Meat would get more expensive | The price would rise | Today's price is artificially low |
| The poor would lose protein | Meat is a source of protein | Beans and other legumes are cheaper sources |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Meat is already artificially cheap.** According to a 2021 report by FAO, UNDP and UNEP, support to agricultural producers worldwide amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and 87% of it distorts prices or causes environmental and social harm. Much of it sustains animal farming and feed grains.

**2. The price doesn't include what production costs.** Pollution, emissions, deforestation, antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases from animals are paid by society, not by the buyer. These are what economists call externalities.

**3. The gravest cost isn't even counted.** The suffering and death of animals appear in no price calculation, because animals are treated as the product, not as someone who bears a cost.

**4. The money can be redirected.** Cutting harmful subsidies doesn't require making food more expensive in general: the same resources can make legumes, grains, fruit and vegetables cheaper, and support new proteins, such as plant-based, fermented and cultivated ones.

**5. There are ways to protect people on low incomes.** Brazil's tax reform, for example, provides for returning part of the taxes to low-income families registered in the government's social registry. Policies like this make it possible to change prices without penalizing the poor.

**6. A purely environmental tax can make things worse for animals.** If the tax were proportional only to emissions, chicken and fish would become relatively cheaper, and the number of animals killed could rise (see J9). A policy that takes animals seriously has to include them in the calculation.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: meat is part of culture and food dignity

The most serious version says that, for many poor families, being able to eat meat is a sign of dignity after years of deprivation, and that making it more expensive would take away an achievement.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Food dignity is real and important.** It calls for enough, healthy and affordable food, not for one specific kind of food.
2. **The achievement can be expanded, not taken away.** Making plant proteins cheaper and securing income broadens access to good food.
3. **Animals pay too.** Some people's dignity can't depend on the suffering and death of others when there are alternatives.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question isn't whether the poor should pay more for food, because they shouldn't. It's whether public money should keep making cheap a product that harms society and requires the suffering and death of animals, when it can feed people in other ways.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either meat stays cheap or the poor go hungry |
| Empirical | Ignoring hidden costs | Treats today's price as the real cost |
| Moral | Invisible cost | Animals don't enter the calculation |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Defends the poor.** And defends public money making meat cheaper, not beans.
2. **Talks about food security.** And ignores the health and environmental costs of animal farming, which hit the poor hardest.
3. **Rejects a tax on meat.** And accepts that society pays its costs in other ways.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a product is consumed by the poor, the state should keep it cheap, whatever the harm".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cigarettes | They shouldn't carry high taxes | Many countries tax them for health reasons |
| Cheap pesticides | They should stay subsidized | The harm also falls on the poor |
| Plant proteins | They deserve public support | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that food taxes weigh more on the poor, that meat has cultural value, and that any change must protect people on low incomes.

But meat is already artificially cheap, because it doesn't pay what it costs, to society or to animals. Protecting the poor and ending subsidies for exploitation can go together.
