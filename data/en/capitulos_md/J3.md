# J3. "Farmers and slaughterhouse workers would lose their jobs"

**Thesis:** this argument has been used against almost every moral and environmental reform: against the end of slavery, child labor, asbestos and coal. Jobs matter, which is why transitions should be planned, with support for those who depend on the sector. But changes in consumption are gradual, and demand for other foods creates new jobs. Besides, slaughterhouse work is among the most dangerous and grueling there is.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "What about the farmers? You want to put millions out of work."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Millions of people depend on livestock farming and the meat industry.
2. **P2.** Ending or shrinking that industry would cause unemployment and poverty.
3. **P3.** Causing mass unemployment is a serious harm.
4. **C.** So veganism is harmful to society.

P1 and P3 are true. P2 is true if the change were sudden and unplanned. The problem lies in the conclusion: change is gradual, and the argument would serve to block any reform.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it assumes an abrupt transition with no alternatives.

## 3. Answer for children

Lots of people work raising animals or in slaughterhouses, and they need jobs. That's why the change has to happen little by little, helping those people find other work, such as growing beans and fruit or making plant foods. Many jobs have changed before, and people have adapted.

## 4. Short answer

Jobs matter, and that's why the transition should be planned. But this argument was used against the end of slavery, child labor and asbestos. Changes in consumption are gradual, and demand for plant foods creates new jobs in farming, industry and retail. And slaughterhouse work is among the most dangerous and grueling there is: change could be good for the workers too.

**Question to ask back:** "If a sector causes harm, is the solution to keep it forever or to help those who work in it to change?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument imagines an abrupt change with no alternatives:

| How the argument imagines it | How transitions usually happen |
| --- | --- |
| Everyone stops eating meat at once | Consumption changes gradually |
| Jobs disappear with no replacement | New sectors grow with new demand |
| No one helps the workers | Just transition policies can support those who depend on the sector |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The argument works against any reform.** It was used against abolition, against child labor laws, against banning asbestos. If it held, no harmful activity could ever be changed.

**2. Change is gradual.** Consumption shifts over years and decades, which allows adaptation.

**3. New jobs appear.** Demand for legumes, fruit, vegetables, plant proteins and other foods creates work in farming and industry.

**4. Slaughterhouse work is dangerous.** Meat and poultry workers face high rates of repetitive strain injuries, accidents with knives and machinery, and exposure to cold and to animal suffering.

**5. Just transitions can be planned.** Public policy can support retraining, credit and alternatives for dependent regions.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: rural communities would be hit hardest

The most serious version points out that entire rural regions depend on livestock farming, and the transition could concentrate losses in vulnerable communities.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That calls for transition policies.** Not for keeping the sector indefinitely.
2. **The same land can produce other foods.** Or be restored with public support (H4).
3. **Concern for jobs doesn't justify the harm to animals.** Even if it justifies care in the transition.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The jobs argument is about real people, and they deserve support. But it doesn't answer whether it's fair to raise and kill animals. If it isn't, the question becomes how to make the transition fairly, not whether it should happen.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Proving too much | The argument would block any reform |
| Epistemological | Unrealistic scenario | Assumes an abrupt change |
| Moral | Economic interest as justification | Jobs outweigh the harm |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports ending other harmful activities.** Even when they created jobs.
2. **Doesn't worry about slaughterhouse workers.** Only about their jobs.
3. **Accepts technological change that eliminates jobs.** In other sectors.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J3 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo bias | The current economy seems the only one possible | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| System justification | The sector seems essential and untouchable | Linked to J4 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an activity creates jobs, it shouldn't be changed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asbestos | It shouldn't have been banned | Banned in the EU and many other countries |
| Child labor | It shouldn't have been banned, because it brought in income | No one accepts this |
| Wildlife trafficking | It shouldn't be fought, because it brings in income | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Jobs in the meat supply chain are real, and many families and towns depend on them. Ignoring this would be unfair and politically naive. A transition to more plant-based food systems needs to include workers and rural communities.

But the need for a just transition isn't a reason to keep the system. It's a reason to plan the change carefully.
