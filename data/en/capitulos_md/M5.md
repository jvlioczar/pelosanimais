# M5. "Meddling with nature is wrong; we could make everything worse"

**Thesis:** the risk of making things worse is real, and that's exactly why the serious proposal isn't to start intervening, but to study first: this is what's called welfare biology. Medicine can also make things worse, and the response was to create research, trials and caution, not to abandon the sick. Besides, we already meddle with nature all the time, for ourselves; what's missing is taking into account the animals that live in it.

**Family:** M. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Every time humans meddle with nature, it goes wrong. Let nature take care of itself."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Ecosystems are complex systems, with knock-on effects that are hard to predict.
2. **P2.** Human interventions in ecosystems have already caused disasters, such as introducing species that became pests.
3. **P3.** An intervention to reduce suffering could end up increasing it.
4. **C.** So we shouldn't intervene in nature to help animals.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and need to be taken seriously. The problem is the conclusion: from the risk of getting it wrong, what follows isn't a ban on acting, but a requirement to act with knowledge.

The argument is **invalid**: the correct conclusion from the premises is "we shouldn't intervene without study", not "we should never intervene".

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "Every human intervention goes wrong." |
| Everyday conversation | "Let nature take care of itself." |
| Environmentalism | "Remember the cane toads in Australia?" |
| Social media | "They'll kill the predators and nature will collapse." |
| Academic version | "The consequences of large-scale interventions are unpredictable." |
| Disguised version | "So should we stop lions from hunting?" (which is F3) |

## 3. Answer for children

When a doctor isn't sure which medicine will work, they don't leave the person untreated: they study, run tests and try carefully. It's the same with animals in nature. Scientists need to study a lot before helping, so as not to make things worse. But studying is already a way to start helping.

## 4. Short answer

The risk of making things worse is real. That's why no serious person proposes meddling with ecosystems at random: they propose studying first, testing on a small scale, and only expanding what works. It's what medicine does with risky treatments. And we already meddle with nature all the time for ourselves; the question is whether the animals living in it count too.

**Question to ask back:** "Since treatments can go wrong, should we close the hospitals, or should we research so we get it wrong less often?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument starts from a truth and draws too big a conclusion from it:

| What is true | What doesn't follow |
| --- | --- |
| Poorly planned interventions can make everything worse | Every intervention will make everything worse |
| Ecosystems are complex | The complexity can never be studied |
| We should be cautious | We should stand still forever |

The proposal of **welfare biology**, suggested by the economist Yew-Kwang Ng in 1995, is precisely to answer risk with knowledge: studying the wellbeing of wild animals and the effects of interventions before scaling them up.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Risk calls for study, not omission.** In every field where acting is risky, such as medicine, engineering or public policy, the response was to create methods for getting it wrong less: research, trials, small and reversible interventions.

**2. We already meddle with nature, a lot.** About half of the planet's habitable land is used for agriculture. We dam rivers, build cities, plant forests, reintroduce and eliminate species. We do this for human interests or environmental goals. What rarely enters the calculation is the wellbeing of the animals affected.

**3. Not intervening also has consequences.** Omission isn't neutral: it lets existing suffering continue. Comparing "intervening" with "doing nothing" as if the second option had no cost is an accounting error.

**4. The human test.** When a human population faces an epidemic, we know interventions can have unexpected effects. The response is to study and act carefully, not to let the epidemic run its course because "meddling makes it worse". Applying the rule of omission only to animals is deciding by species.

**5. Some kinds of help have low risk and known effects.** Rescuing animals in floods, treating the injured and vaccinating against specific diseases are limited, studied interventions that are already practised.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: we'll never know enough

The most serious version says: ecosystems are so complex that we'll never have enough knowledge to predict the consequences. Humility calls for not intervening.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Never knowing everything isn't the same as never knowing anything.** In medicine and ecology we act on partial knowledge all the time, and knowledge grows precisely through research and controlled experience.
2. **Humility cuts both ways.** We also don't know whether omission is the best option. Assuming not acting is always safe is a form of overconfidence.
3. **Small interventions teach.** Starting with what is local, reversible and observable lets us learn without gambling whole ecosystems.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | From some disasters it concludes every intervention goes wrong |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either large-scale intervention without study, or nothing |
| Epistemological | Omission bias | Treats not acting as a cost-free option |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts meddling with nature for human interests, but not for the animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts farming, cities and dams.** Large interventions in nature, made by us.
2. **Supports environmental programmes that kill animals.** Such as controlling invasive species, which are also interventions (M7).
3. **Trusts medicine.** Which is also risky and advances by studying its own mistakes.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M5 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Omission bias | Harms from not acting seem less serious | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |
| Status quo bias | The current state of nature seems the safe default | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Appeal to nature | The natural seems balanced and good | Linked to C1 and M7 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "since intervening could make things worse, we shouldn't intervene".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicine | No new treatment should ever be tried | No one accepts this |
| Humanitarian aid | We shouldn't help in complex crises | Aid exists and learns from its mistakes |
| Environmental conservation | Reintroduction or species-control programmes would be banned | Environmentalists intervene all the time |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets a lot right: ecosystems are complex, interventions have caused disasters, and anyone who wants to help wild animals needs humility and method. Grand, hasty proposals would be irresponsible.

But risk is a reason to study, not to give up. And if we accept meddling with nature for so many human reasons, there's no reason to exclude precisely the good of the animals that live in it.
