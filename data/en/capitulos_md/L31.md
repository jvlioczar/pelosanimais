# L31. "My food beats yours"

**Thesis:** it's a taunt, not an argument. Even if the meat dish tasted better to the speaker, the question isn't which food wins a taste contest, but what happened to whoever became the food. Besides, the comparison is false: much of what people eat every day is already vegan, and much of the flavor credited to meat comes from salt, fat, the grill and seasoning.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

**Note:** the Portuguese original is a cruder Brazilian taunt; the English phrasing keeps its spirit.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My food beats yours. While you eat lettuce, I'm eating steak."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Food exists to give pleasure, as well as nourishment.
2. **P2.** Dishes with meat give more pleasure than vegan dishes.
3. **P3.** Giving up a greater pleasure without need is irrational.
4. **C.** So eating meat is the rational choice, and the vegan is the one missing out.

P1 is true. P2 is debatable and varies from person to person. The main problem lies in P3, which treats the eater's pleasure as the only thing at stake.

The argument is **invalid** as a reply to veganism: it compares menus, while the vegan objection is about harm to others.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone said: "My toy is much cooler than yours, so I can take your friend's without asking." Having a cool toy doesn't let anyone harm others. It's the same with food. What matters isn't which dish tastes better; it's whether someone had to suffer for it to exist.

## 4. Short answer

Maybe you do like your food better. But the question isn't which dish tastes better; it's whether it's worth an animal's life. And honestly, the rice, beans, bread, pasta, fries and almost all the seasoning on your plate are already vegan.

**Question to ask back:** "If the best dish in the world were made of dog meat, would that settle the question?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The sentence mixes two different disputes:

| Dispute | What's at stake | Who decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which food tastes better? | The eater's pleasure | Each person's taste |
| Is eating animals just? | Another being's life and suffering | Reasons, not taste buds |

Veganism doesn't claim vegan food always tastes better. It claims the eater's pleasure doesn't justify the harm to the one eaten.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Pleasure isn't a license to cause harm.** In no other context do we accept "it tastes better" or "it's more fun" as a justification for harming someone (J5).

**2. The comparison is false.** Much of what people eat every day is already vegan: rice, beans, bread, pasta, fruit, vegetables, potatoes, most sauces and seasonings. Vegan food isn't a separate cuisine; it's most of ordinary cuisine, without the animal.

**3. Much of the flavor doesn't come from the animal.** Salt, fat, smoking, caramelization on the grill and seasoning account for much of what people associate with the taste of meat, and they can be reproduced with plant ingredients.

**4. Taste is habit.** Food preferences form through repeated exposure and change with it. People who say vegan food is bad have often tried little of it, or only carelessly made versions.

**5. It's a taunt, and taunts reveal the lack of an argument.** When a conversation moves from reason to mockery, it's almost always because the moral question went unanswered.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Piazza et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2015.04.011) | "Nice" is one of the four most common justifications for eating meat | The "4Ns": natural, normal, necessary and nice |
| Brehm, *A Theory of Psychological Reactance* (1966) | When people feel their freedom threatened, they react by reasserting the behavior | Helps explain the challenging tone of the phrase |
| [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550611415695) | Meat eaters tend to react against vegetarians because they expect to be judged | The taunt as a preemptive defense |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: pleasure is also a legitimate interest

The most serious version says: the pleasure of eating is an important part of a good life, tied to culture, memory and togetherness. Asking someone to give it up is asking a lot.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The pleasure doesn't have to disappear.** The swap is of ingredients, not of the pleasure of eating. Culture, memory and togetherness remain (D3).
2. **Legitimate interests have limits too.** The eater's pleasure is a real interest, but a small one next to another being's interest in not suffering and not dying.
3. **The disproportion decides.** On one side, the difference between two dishes; on the other, a whole life.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if the taunt were true and the meat dish tasted better to the speaker, that would only answer a question of taste. The moral question remains open: does the pleasure of a dish justify what is done to the animal?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L31 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the question | Swaps "is it just?" for "is it tasty?" |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Sets "steak" against "lettuce", ignoring plant-based cooking |
| Epistemological | Generalizing from little experience | Judges all vegan food by a few dishes |
| Moral | Pleasure as justification | Uses one's own pleasure to dismiss harm to another |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Eats vegan food every day.** Rice, beans and bread don't stop being tasty for having no animal in them.
2. **Doesn't accept "it's tasty" for other harms.** No one would defend eating dog meat for being flavorful.
3. **Uses seasoning to give meat flavor.** And the seasoning is plant-based.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L31 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rationalization through the 4Ns | Flavor works as a ready-made justification | [Piazza et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2015.04.011) |
| Psychological reactance | Feeling one's choice threatened leads to reasserting it emphatically | Brehm, 1966 |
| Do-gooder derogation | Ridiculing the vegan eases the feeling of being judged | [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550611415695) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if my option is more pleasurable, it's the right one".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dog or cat meat | It would be justifiable if it were tastier | Almost no one accepts this |
| Fun that hurts | It would be acceptable if it were more fun | We reject it when there's a victim |
| Any product | The most pleasurable would always be the most just, however it was made | No one defends this for slave labor |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Eating is a pleasure, and that pleasure matters. Anyone who wants to persuade someone to change their diet does well to show that vegan food can be delicious, not a sad sacrifice.

But flavor isn't the measure of justice. A food can be tasty and still cost the life of someone who didn't want to die.
