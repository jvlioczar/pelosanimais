# G12. "Vegans are weak, pale and depressed"

**Thesis:** it's a stereotype, resting on the false idea that a vegan diet is inherently inferior; the main nutrition bodies consider well-planned vegan diets adequate. There are vegans of every kind, including elite athletes. On depression, the studies are mixed: some meta-analyses find an association between vegetarian diets and depressive symptoms, but the direction isn't clear, and there are signs that people start avoiding meat after a diagnosis, not the other way round. Part of the distress vegans report may be moral and social rather than nutritional: psychologist Clare Mann called it "vystopia", the anguish of perceiving animal suffering in a world that ignores it, a proposed concept, not a clinical diagnosis. A correlation doesn't tell us what causes what.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Every vegan looks sick. They're missing meat in their heads."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some studies link vegetarian and vegan diets to a higher risk of depressive symptoms.
2. **P2.** Deficiencies in B12, iron and omega-3 can affect mood and energy.
3. **P3.** So the vegan diet can cause weakness and depression.
4. **C.** So the vegan diet harms physical and mental health.

P1 is true: some meta-analyses find an association. So is P2. The problem lies in P3: the association doesn't show causation, and the deficiencies are preventable.

The argument is **valid** if P3 is true. Its **soundness** fails because P3 isn't established.

## 3. Answer for children

You can't tell what someone eats just by looking at them. There are strong vegans, athletes, doctors, artists and people of every kind. Food made only from plants, when it's varied and well thought out, gives the body what it needs. Thinking that everyone in a group is the same isn't fair, and it isn't true. And when a vegan feels sad, sometimes it's because they care a lot about animals and see that many people don't.

## 4. Short answer

It's a stereotype, born of the false idea that a vegan diet is inferior: the main nutrition bodies consider well-planned vegan diets adequate, and there are vegans of every kind, including elite athletes. On depression, the results are mixed: some meta-analyses find an association, other studies don't, and the studies are mostly cross-sectional and don't show what causes what. There are signs that many people start avoiding meat after a diagnosis, not the other way round. Deficiencies in B12 or iron can affect mood, but they're preventable with planning. And part of the distress may come from elsewhere: psychologist Clare Mann calls it "vystopia", the anguish of perceiving animal suffering in a world that treats it as normal.

**Question to ask back:** "If many people stop eating meat after a diagnosis, did the diet cause the depression, or the other way round?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes a stereotype with a statistical association:

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| A vegan diet is inherently inferior | False: nutrition bodies consider well-planned vegan diets adequate |
| Vegans are weak and pale | Stereotype: there are vegans of every kind |
| There's an association between vegetarianism and depressive symptoms | Found in some meta-analyses; other studies don't find it, or find the opposite |
| The diet causes depression | Not established: the direction may be reversed |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a stereotype.** Appearance doesn't reveal diet. High-level vegan athletes and vegans of all ages contradict the image (G11).

**2. The premise that the diet is inferior is false.** The stereotype assumes that without meat a diet is incomplete. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, in the United States, states that well-planned vegetarian diets, including vegan ones, are healthy and nutritionally adequate at all stages of life. What matters is planning, as with any diet (G2, G3).

**3. The results on depression are mixed, and the association doesn't show the direction.** A 2022 systematic review of 23 studies found conflicting results: of the 25 outcomes analysed, 11 linked vegetarian or vegan diets to more depression, 7 to benefits and 7 to no association. Meta-analyses from 2021 found an association between vegetarian diets and depressive symptoms, but most of the studies are cross-sectional, and the authors themselves point out that it's impossible to know whether the diet leads to depression or the reverse.

**4. There are signs of reverse causation.** A longitudinal German study found that many people began avoiding meat after a diagnosis of a mental disorder. Sensitivity to others' suffering may also be linked to both factors.

**5. The deficiencies are preventable.** A lack of B12, iron or omega-3 can affect energy and mood, but it's prevented with supplementation and planning (G4, G5, G7).

**6. There's also a moral and social explanation: "vystopia".** Clare Mann, a psychologist working in Australia, coined the term *vystopia* (from "vegan" and "dystopia") to describe the anguish of becoming aware of the scale of animal suffering while living in a world that treats it as normal: isolation, anger, guilt and difficulty relating to people who don't see the same thing. She developed the idea in the book *Vystopia: The Anguish of Being Vegan in a Non-Vegan World*, published in 2018. It's a concept proposed from clinical practice, not a diagnosis recognized in psychiatric manuals. Research is still at an early stage: a 2025 study analysed more than 14,000 posts from an online forum on the subject and found accounts of strained relationships, pessimism and existential distress, linked mainly to moral concerns. If so, part of the sadness doesn't come from the plate, but from caring about those who suffer; and that isn't a sign of weakness.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Ocklenburg and Borawski, 2021, *Journal of Affective Disorders*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34375207/) | Vegetarians had higher depression scores, with great heterogeneity between studies | The authors stress that the direction of the relationship can't be determined |
| [Jain et al., 2022, *Nutrition Bulletin*](https://doi.org/10.1111/nbu.12540) | Systematic review of 23 studies: of 25 outcomes, 11 linked the diets to more depression, 7 to benefits and 7 to no association | The authors call for longitudinal and intervention studies |
| [Melina, Craig and Levin, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27886704/) | Well-planned vegetarian diets, including vegan ones, are healthy and nutritionally adequate at all stages of life | Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics |
| [Lugassy, 2021, *The Psychologist*](https://bps.org.uk/psychologist/vegan-non-vegan-world) | Presents Clare Mann's "vystopia" as a way of naming vegans' anguish in a non-vegan world and calls for more research | Magazine of the British Psychological Society; doesn't treat the term as a diagnosis |
| [Veitch and Gregson, 2025, *Psychology of Human-Animal Intergroup Relations*](https://doi.org/10.5964/phair.17679) | Analysis of 14,542 posts from the r/Vystopia forum: strained relationships, pessimism and existential distress, with more references to morality and negative emotion | Analysis of online texts; doesn't measure how many vegans feel this |
| [Michalak, Zhang and Jacobi, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3466124/) | Adopting a vegetarian diet tended to happen after the onset of mental disorders; there was no evidence of a causal role for the diet | A representative sample from Germany, with more than 4,000 people |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: even without certainty, there's a risk

The most serious version grants that causation is uncertain, but says that, given an association, it would be prudent not to adopt a vegan diet.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Prudence calls for supervision, not meat.** Preventing deficiencies takes care of the nutritional part of the risk.
2. **If the cause is reversed, changing diet doesn't help.** Going back to meat wouldn't treat depression that came before the diet.
3. **Diets with more plants are linked to benefits in other outcomes.** The body of evidence doesn't favor meat.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The stereotype isn't an argument, and the association with depression doesn't show that meat is necessary. The question becomes moral again: if vegans can be healthy with planning, what justifies the animals' suffering?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in G12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Stereotype | Attributes a baseless image to everyone |
| Logical | Correlation taken as causation | Concludes the diet causes depression |
| Epistemological | Ignoring reverse causation | Doesn't consider that depression may come first |
| Epistemological | False premise | Assumes a vegan diet is inherently inferior |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Knows people who eat meat and are depressed.** And doesn't blame meat.
2. **Doesn't judge omnivores' health by appearance.** The criterion only applies to vegans.
3. **Admires vegan athletes upon meeting them.** The stereotype doesn't survive contact.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a group has a statistical association with a problem, the group's trait causes the problem".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People who go to hospitals | Going to hospital would cause illness | Absurd |
| People in therapy | Therapy would cause psychological distress | Absurd |
| Diets with meat | Associations with heart disease would prove causation | Those who use the argument reject this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The association between vegetarian diets and depressive symptoms appears in some studies and deserves serious investigation. It's also true that many vegans report distress, which the idea of "vystopia" helps to name and which deserves support. Nutritional deficiencies can affect mood, and vegans should take care of them. It's also true that some vegans are in poor health, like people on any diet.

But a stereotype isn't evidence, and an association isn't a cause. With planning, a vegan diet supports healthy bodies and minds.
