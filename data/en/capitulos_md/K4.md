# K4. "There is no ethical consumption under capitalism"

**Thesis:** there's a partial truth here: almost every product involves some exploitation or harm in its supply chain. But using that truth to cancel out every difference is a mistake. Between buying beans and buying meat from animals raised and killed, the difference in harm is enormous. That there's no perfect consumption doesn't mean all consumption is the same.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Everything is exploitation. Your sneakers were made with slave labor."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Under capitalism, supply chains involve exploitation of workers, environmental harm and animal suffering.
2. **P2.** No consumer can avoid these harms completely.
3. **P3.** If no consumption is ethical, individual consumption choices have no moral significance.
4. **C.** So veganism makes no one more ethical, and change must be systemic.

P1 and P2 are largely true. The problem lies in P3: the impossibility of purity doesn't make all choices equivalent.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Almost everything we buy might have some problem in how it was made. But some things cause much more harm than others. Buying beans is very different from buying meat, which requires an animal's death. Even if nothing is perfect, we can choose what hurts less.

## 4. Short answer

It's true that almost every product involves some harm in its supply chain. But that doesn't make all choices the same. Between buying beans and buying meat, the difference in harm is enormous: one involves raising and killing animals, the other doesn't. That there's no perfect consumption doesn't mean there's no better consumption. And criticizing the system doesn't stop anyone from changing their own plate.

**Question to ask back:** "If nothing is perfect, isn't buying something that causes less harm still better?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument turns "no choice is perfect" into "all choices are equal":

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Almost all consumption involves some harm | True |
| No consumption is perfectly ethical | True |
| So all choices are equivalent | False: the differences in harm are enormous |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the nirvana fallacy.** The impossibility of perfection doesn't make striving for better irrelevant.

**2. The differences in harm are enormous.** Meat requires raising and killing animals, as well as more land, water and emissions (H6, H7). Beans and rice have some impact too, like all agriculture (H2), but far less, and they don't require raising and killing animals.

**3. Criticizing the system and changing consumption complement each other.** No one has to choose one or the other (K9).

**4. The argument works against any ethical choice.** If no consumption is ethical, there'd be no point avoiding products of slave labor either, which those who use the argument usually don't accept.

**5. Critics of capitalism themselves make choices.** Many boycott companies or products for ethical reasons.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Our World in Data, how many animals are factory-farmed](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-are-factory-farmed) | About 74% of farmed land animals live in factory farms | Shows the scale of the harm linked to meat |
| [Our World in Data, land use and diets](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets) | A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75% | Shows the difference in impact between diets |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: focusing on consumption depoliticizes

The most serious version says that focusing on individual choices distracts from structural change, and that veganism as a lifestyle can end up serving the market itself.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The two can go together.** Individual choices and political action reinforce each other (K9).
2. **Not buying meat isn't a lifestyle; it's no longer paying for deaths.**
3. **Structural change also depends on people.** Movements start with individuals acting consistently.

### (f) Back to the moral question

That no consumption is perfect doesn't answer the question about the animals. Among imperfect choices, one involves paying for the death of sentient beings, and the other doesn't. The question remains: why choose the one that kills?

| Level | Error | How it appears in K4 |

| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If nothing is perfect, nothing matters |
| Logical | False equivalence | Treats all choices as equal |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either systemic change or consumption |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Makes ethical consumer choices on other issues.** Such as boycotts.
2. **Recognizes differences between products.** When the subject is slave labor.
3. **Criticizes the system, but doesn't change what's up to them.**

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if no choice is perfect, they're all the same".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Products of slave labor | Buying them would be the same as buying others | No one accepts this |
| Transport | A plane and a bicycle would pollute equally | No one accepts this |
| Food | Meat and beans would be equivalent | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The critique of capitalist supply chains points to real problems: exploitation of workers, environmental harm and suffering behind many products. It's also true that structural change is needed, and that individual consumption has limits.

But recognizing those limits doesn't make all choices equivalent. Among the possible harms, no longer paying for animals to be killed is a simple and significant choice.
