# G8. "Plants have antinutrients"

**Thesis:** antinutrients exist, but in the usual amounts and with the usual preparation they matter little, and many of them bring benefits. Cooking, soaking, sprouting and fermenting reduce lectins, phytates and oxalates. Raw beans are harmful; cooked beans are a very nutritious food. And in large studies, eating legumes and whole grains is linked to lower mortality. In a fair balance, animal products also contain compounds of concern, such as those in processed meat, classified as carcinogenic, and these don't disappear with preparation. And the account includes the animals' suffering and death.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Beans have lectins. Spinach has oxalates. Plants have poisons to defend themselves."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Plants produce compounds that can reduce nutrient absorption or cause unwanted effects, such as lectins, phytates and oxalates.
2. **P2.** Plant-based diets contain more of these compounds.
3. **P3.** These compounds can harm health.
4. **C.** So plant-based diets are harmful, and animal foods are safer.

P1 is true. So is P2. The problem lies in P3: with the usual preparation, these compounds have small effects, and several of them are linked to benefits.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which ignores dose and preparation.

## 3. Answer for children

Some plants have substances that can cause a bit of trouble, like raw beans. That's why we cook beans! Once cooked, they're tasty and very healthy. And scientists show that cooked beans are good for you. Meat also has things that are bad for you, especially ham, sausages and bacon. And to have meat, animals suffer and die.

## 4. Short answer

Antinutrients exist, but cooking, soaking, sprouting and fermenting reduce most of them. Raw beans are harmful; cooked beans are healthy, which is why you don't eat raw beans. Many of these compounds, such as phytates and polyphenols, are even linked to benefits, and diets rich in legumes and whole grains are among those most linked to a long life. If the criterion is containing compounds of concern, animal products have them too: processed meat is classified as carcinogenic, and saturated fat is linked to cardiovascular disease. And in that account you also have to include the animals' suffering and death.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you eat your beans raw or cooked?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument ignores dose, preparation and positive effects:

| Compound | Where it's found | What reduces it | Real effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lectins | Raw beans and grains | Cooking | Practically eliminated by cooking |
| Phytates | Grains, legumes, seeds | Soaking, sprouting, fermenting | Slightly reduce mineral absorption; linked to antioxidant effects |
| Oxalates | Spinach, beetroot | Cooking, variety | Relevant only for people prone to kidney stones |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Dose and preparation matter.** Lectins are practically eliminated by proper cooking. Phytates and oxalates are reduced by simple techniques.

**2. Many antinutrients bring benefits.** The Harvard School of Public Health's nutrition page summarizes studies indicating that phytates, polyphenols and other compounds may have protective effects, and that the foods containing them are linked to lower disease risk.

**3. Foods with antinutrients are healthy.** Legumes, whole grains and vegetables are among the foods most linked to good health and longevity in large studies.

**4. Animal foods also contain compounds of concern, and the balance has to look at both sides.** If the criterion is "containing something that can do harm", animal products enter the account too, with stronger evidence of harm in some cases:

| Side | Compound or factor | What is known | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Animal | Processed meat (bacon, ham, sausages) | Classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as Group 1, "carcinogenic to humans", mainly for colorectal cancer; about 18% higher risk for every 50 g a day | Group 1 reflects the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk: it isn't "as dangerous as smoking" |
| Animal | Red meat | Classified as Group 2A, "probably carcinogenic"; about 17% higher colorectal risk for every 100 g a day | Limited evidence in humans; the mechanistic evidence was judged strong |
| Animal | Heme iron and N-nitroso compounds | Among the plausible mechanisms: heme iron promotes the formation of N-nitroso compounds in the gut, and so do the nitrites used in processed meat | Mechanisms under study; IARC didn't evaluate them on their own |
| Animal | Heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons | Form in meat cooked at high temperature, as in barbecuing and frying | According to the US National Cancer Institute, human studies haven't established a definitive link with cancer |
| Animal | Saturated fat | The WHO recommends limiting it to no more than 10% of energy, because of its association with cardiovascular disease; fatty meats, butter and cheese are major sources | Dietary cholesterol has a smaller and more debated effect than saturated fat |
| Animal | Calcium in milk | Reduces iron absorption in the same meal, as phytates do | In a varied diet, the effect of any single component is small |
| Plant | Lectins, phytates, oxalates | Reduce mineral absorption or are harmful raw; greatly reduced by soaking, cooking, sprouting and fermenting | Phytates and polyphenols are also linked to protective effects |

The balance doesn't say animal products are poison, nor that plants are harmless in every form. It says that "having compounds of concern" doesn't separate the two sides in the animals' favour: plant compounds are largely neutralized by preparation, and the foods containing them are linked to lower mortality, while some of the risks of animal products, such as those of processed meat, don't go away in the kitchen (G17).

**5. Plants aren't "poison".** That a plant produces defensive compounds doesn't mean it's toxic to those who prepare and eat it in normal amounts.

**6. Suffering also enters the account.** An honest balance doesn't compare only chemical compounds. On the side of animal products, there is also what they cost the animals: confinement, mutilations, separation from their young, and death. Even if both sides carried equivalent nutritional risks, that difference would remain, and it can't be soaked away.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some people are sensitive

The most serious version grants that, for most people, antinutrients aren't a problem, but points out that some people have sensitivities, such as intolerances or a tendency to kidney stones.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Sensitivities call for adjustments, not meat.** People prone to stones can cut back on high-oxalate foods.
2. **These are specific cases.** They don't justify conclusions for everyone.
3. **Variety solves it.** A varied plant diet doesn't depend on any one food.

### (f) Back to the moral question

If antinutrients are controlled by preparation and the foods that contain them are healthy, the argument doesn't justify eating animals. And when the full balance is drawn, animal products have their own compounds of concern and, beyond them, a cost no preparation removes: the suffering and death of those who are eaten. The question returns: what justifies that suffering when there are safe, nutritious plant foods?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in G8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Ignoring the dose | Treats the presence of a compound as proof of harm |
| Epistemological | Not knowing food preparation | Ignores that cooking eliminates lectins |
| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Highlights the compounds and ignores the foods' benefits |
| Epistemological | Double standard | Scrutinizes the compounds in plants but not those in animal products |
| Moral | Incomplete balance | Leaves the animals' suffering and death out of the account |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Eats cooked beans.** And feels no effect from the lectins.
2. **Consumes coffee, chocolate and wine.** Which also contain plant defense compounds.
3. **The animals they eat are fed on plants.** Grain and soy in their feed.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a food contains a potentially harmful compound, it should be avoided".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Processed meat | It should be avoided, because it's classified as carcinogenic | Those who use the argument don't conclude this |
| Milk and dairy | They should be avoided, because calcium reduces iron absorption and they are a source of saturated fat | Those who use the argument don't conclude this |
| Coffee | It should be avoided, because it has defense compounds | No one concludes this |
| Potatoes | They should be avoided, because they contain solanine | No one concludes this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that plants produce defense compounds and that some of them can reduce mineral absorption or cause discomfort. Proper preparation matters, and people with specific conditions should take care.

But dose and preparation turn what would be a problem into a detail. Foods rich in antinutrients are among the healthiest there are. And if the criterion applies to plants, it applies to animal products too, which besides their own risky compounds bring the animals' suffering and death.
