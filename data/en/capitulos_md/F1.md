# F1. "On a desert island, would you eat an animal?"

**Thesis:** maybe so, and that changes nothing. An extreme survival situation doesn't justify the practice under normal conditions. We also accept that a starving person may steal food, and no one concludes that stealing is acceptable in everyday life. The hypothetical case only shows that real necessity changes what can be demanded of someone. Philosophers disagree on whether it makes the act permissible or only excuses the person who does it, but in neither reading does it apply to someone with alternatives. And most people who ask aren't on an island.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "What if you were starving? In an apocalypse you'd eat meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In a situation of extreme hunger, even a vegan would eat an animal to survive.
2. **P2.** If eating an animal is acceptable in some case, then it isn't wrong in itself.
3. **P3.** What isn't wrong in itself depends only on circumstances and personal choice.
4. **C.** So eating animals is acceptable, and veganism is a preference.

P1 may be true. The problem lies in P2 and P3: almost no moral rule is absolute, and the existence of extreme exceptions doesn't make the practice acceptable under normal conditions.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which treats "may be justified in extreme cases" as "may be done at any time".

## 3. Answer for children

If you were lost in a forest with nothing to eat, you might have to do things you normally wouldn't. But that doesn't mean you can do those things every day, when you're at home with a full fridge. An emergency is different from normal life.

## 4. Short answer

Maybe I would, just as a starving person may steal food, and no one concludes that stealing is acceptable in everyday life. In extreme need, nobody would blame the person who does it. But none of that holds when there's no need. None of us is on a desert island: we live near stores full of alternatives. The question isn't what I'd do to survive, but what I do when I don't need to.

**Question to ask back:** "Are you on a desert island right now, or somewhere with a grocery store?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses an extreme case to draw a conclusion about the ordinary one:

| Situation | Is there necessity? | What morality allows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Desert island, extreme hunger | Yes | Perhaps an exception, or at least an excuse |
| Supermarket with alternatives | No | The general rule: don't cause unnecessary harm |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Exceptions don't cancel rules.** Almost every moral rule admits extreme exceptions: killing in self-defense, stealing so as not to starve, lying to save a life. No one concludes that killing, stealing or lying are acceptable in general. But self-defense has a limit: it is against an attacker. The animal attacks no one, so the island is more like killing an innocent bystander than like defending yourself.

**2. Justifying isn't excusing.** A justified act is right. An excused act is still wrong, but the person who does it deserves no blame or punishment, because of the pressure they were under. In animal ethics, some hold that necessity makes it permissible to kill an animal to survive, such as Peter Singer, Tom Regan and Alasdair Cochrane. Others hold that it remains wrong but excusable, such as Gary Francione. A paper by Joshua Jarvis-Campbell (2025) argues that the second reading fits better with what many philosophers say about killing innocent humans: you may not kill an innocent even to save your own life, but someone acting under extreme pressure can be excused (F2). In both readings, someone with alternatives has neither justification nor excuse.

**3. Veganism already allows for necessity.** The classic definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" (D7). On the island, not eating an animal may be beyond what's possible.

**4. The extreme case confirms the principle.** If only extreme hunger justifies or excuses eating an animal, then under normal conditions, without that necessity, there's neither justification nor excuse.

**5. The question shifts the focus.** The debate is about what we do every day, not about what we'd do in a shipwreck.

**6. Extreme situations would lead even further.** In real survival cases, people have resorted to extreme measures, including eating the human dead. No one draws a rule for normal life from that.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: veganism isn't absolute, so it's a preference

The most serious version says that if a vegan admits eating animals in some case, they agree the question depends on circumstances, and then everyone can assess their own.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Depending on circumstances isn't being a preference.** The prohibition on killing also depends on circumstances (self-defense against an attacker), and it isn't a preference.
2. **The relevant circumstances are objective.** Extreme hunger is a real necessity; liking the taste isn't.
3. **The person isn't in the exceptional circumstance.** What holds on the island doesn't hold in the supermarket.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization from an exception | An extreme exception becomes the rule |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either the rule is absolute or it's a preference |
| Epistemological | Irrelevant case | Uses a situation that doesn't apply to whoever asks |
| Moral | Invented necessity | Treats normal life as if it were survival |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts exceptions to other rules without abandoning them.** Self-defense doesn't make homicide acceptable.
2. **Doesn't live on an island.** The question describes a situation that isn't theirs.
3. **Would also eat other things on the island.** Including things they'd never eat at home.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if something is allowed in an emergency, it's always allowed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stealing out of hunger | Stealing would always be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Self-defense | Killing would always be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Running a red light to get someone to hospital | Running red lights would always be acceptable | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The hypothetical has a legitimate point: veganism isn't an absolute, blind rule, and real necessity changes what can be morally required. It's honest to recognize this, and animal advocates don't need to pretend they'd let someone starve on principle.

But that is exactly why the argument doesn't work. If only extreme necessity justifies eating animals, or at least excuses those who do it, then in ordinary life, without that necessity, both the justification and the excuse disappear.
