# I21. "A rodeo animal is a well-treated athlete and only bucks for a few seconds"

**Thesis:** rodeo bulls and horses buck because they are provoked: the flank strap, a rope or strap tightened around the flank, on the underside of the belly near the groin, and spurs are part of the event, and the animal doesn't choose to compete. The strap is tightened as the animal leaves the chute precisely to make it buck more violently, and it causes discomfort, possibly pain, and stress. No one would accept tying such a strap around a person's belly to make them jump in front of an audience. Brazil's own rodeo law, from 2002, bans pointed spurs and electric shocks, which shows the risk of cruelty is recognized. Veterinary bodies, such as the Australian Veterinary Association, and welfare organizations, such as the RSPCA, criticize the use of flank straps and spurs. The eight seconds in the arena are the end of a life of transport, confinement and training for it.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "A rodeo bull is an athlete, worth a fortune, treated better than a lot of people. And he only bucks for eight seconds."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Rodeo animals are valuable, and their owners have an interest in keeping them healthy.
2. **P2.** The ride lasts a few seconds, and there are welfare rules and inspection.
3. **P3.** Rodeo supports jobs and is part of rural culture.
4. **C.** So rodeo isn't cruelty.

P1 is partly true: a valuable animal tends to receive veterinary care. P2 is true about the duration, but ignores how the bucking is provoked and what happens outside the arena. P3 is true, but says nothing about the animal.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and in the step from "well cared for" to "doesn't suffer".

## 3. Answer for children

The rodeo bull doesn't buck because he likes to play. He bucks because a rope is pulled tight around his belly, near his back legs, which bothers him a lot, and because someone is on top of him that he wants to throw off. He didn't choose to be there. No one would think it was right to do that to a person so others could have fun. You can have a party with music, food and horse rides without making the bull buck in discomfort.

## 4. Short answer

The animal bucks because it is provoked: the flank strap, a rope tightened around the flank near the groin, causes discomfort and stress to make it buck more violently, and spurs are part of the event. Would you accept doing the same to a person to entertain an audience? And the animal didn't choose to compete, so calling it an athlete is misleading. Brazil's own rodeo law bans pointed spurs and electric shocks, recognizing the risk of cruelty, and veterinary bodies criticize flank straps and spurs. Being well fed doesn't change what happens in the arena, or the years of transport and confinement. So I'll ask: do a few seconds of show justify that?

**Question to ask back:** "If the bull liked bucking, why would the flank strap be needed?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Claim | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "He's an athlete" | He gets food and care | He doesn't choose to compete and can't quit |
| "It's only eight seconds" | The ride is short | Flank strap, spurs, transport, confinement, roping events |
| "There are rules" | Law 10,519/2002 requires a vet and bans some instruments | The law allows flank straps and blunt spurs |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The bucking is provoked.** The flank strap is a rope or strap passed around the animal's flank, the sensitive area on the underside of the belly, in front of the hind legs and near the groin. It is loose inside the chute and tightened just before release, so that the animal bucks more violently; spurs are used by the rider. RSPCA Australia describes the pressure on this area as causing discomfort and possibly pain, and a 2005 opinion by a German association of veterinarians for animal protection recommended banning flank straps and spurs. The industry says the flank strap doesn't injure: the rules of the main US rodeo association require soft cotton rope for bulls and fleece or neoprene lining for horses. It is also often said that the strap squeezes the testicles; this is disputed, and the sources consulted describe the strap on the flank, not on the genitals. But the point doesn't depend on it: either way, the strap exists to bother the animal, and the animal bucks to get rid of it.

**2. We wouldn't accept the same with humans.** Imagine tightening a rope around a person's belly and groin, without their consent, so that they jump and thrash in front of an audience. No one would call it sport, however well fed the person was outside the arena. If the practice would be unacceptable with a human because of the discomfort, fear and stress it causes, the question is what morally relevant difference would make it acceptable with a bull or a horse.

**3. "Athlete" is a misleading metaphor.** A human athlete chooses to compete, knows the risks and can stop. The animal is transported, confined and ridden without a choice.

**4. The law recognizes the risk.** Law 10,519/2002 bans spurs with pointed rowels and any instrument that causes injuries, including electric shock devices. A law only bans what was happening or could happen.

**5. Veterinary and welfare bodies criticize the practice.** RSPCA Australia opposes rodeos and cites injuries and stress in bulls and bucking horses, and the Australian Veterinary Association is concerned about the use of flank straps and spurs and doesn't support events such as calf rope-and-tie.

**6. There are events besides riding.** At many rodeos, calves are roped at a run and thrown to the ground, which can injure their necks and legs.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: without rodeo, these animals wouldn't even exist

The most serious version says bucking bulls are bred for this, live better than beef cattle and, without rodeo, would never have been born.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Existing isn't a favor that justifies any use.** The same argument would justify breeding any animal for entertainment.
2. **Living better than beef cattle isn't living well.** Comparing with something worse doesn't prove rodeo is acceptable.
3. **The culture can continue without the suffering.** Music, food, horse rides and gatherings don't depend on provoking the animal.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to provoke an animal into trying to throw someone off, for an audience's entertainment. Caring for the animal well outside the arena doesn't answer that.

| Logical | False analogy | Compares the animal to an athlete who chooses to compete |
| Empirical | Selective framing | Counts only the eight seconds of the ride |
| Moral | Entertainment above welfare | The show justifies provoking the animal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the animal likes to buck.** And needs the flank strap to make it buck.
2. **Says there are welfare rules.** And the rule exists because there was cruelty.
3. **Calls the animal an athlete.** And they can never quit.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the animal is well cared for outside the arena, what happens in it is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cockfights | They'd be acceptable, because the roosters are well treated | Brazil's Supreme Court banned cockfights |
| Farra do boi (an ox-chasing festival) | It would be acceptable outside festival days | It was also banned |
| Festivals without animal events | They keep the culture without the problem | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that many rodeo animals receive veterinary care, that the ride lasts a few seconds, and that rodeo matters to many communities and jobs.

But the animal bucks because it is provoked, doesn't choose to be there, and the law itself recognizes the risk of cruelty. The festival can go on without it.
