# L15. "Vegans are speciesists too"

**Thesis:** it's true that vegans, like everyone else, have preferences and biases toward some animals: they like dogs more than cockroaches and are more moved by charismatic mammals. That's worth admitting. But having a bias doesn't license harming anyone, nor does it show the bias is right: it shows it needs correcting. Speciesism is disregarding interests without justification because of species, not any difference in feeling or treatment. And pointing to the speaker's bias ("you too") doesn't answer what was said about animals.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You prefer dogs to cockroaches too. You also think you're superior to animals, so much so that you decide for them. So don't lecture me about speciesism."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegans condemn speciesism, which is treating animals unequally because of their species.
2. **P2.** Vegans also treat species unequally: they prefer dogs to insects, are more moved by mammals and make decisions on animals' behalf.
3. **P3.** Whoever practices what they condemn has no basis for condemning it.
4. **C.** So the vegan critique of speciesism doesn't hold.

P2 is largely true, and it's honest to say so. The problem lies in P1, which defines speciesism too broadly, and in P3, which is a tu quoque: the speaker's failing doesn't make what they say false.

The argument is **invalid** in its usual form: nothing about what is owed to animals follows from a vegan's bias. With P3 made explicit it becomes valid, but it isn't **sound**.

## 3. Answer for children

Everyone finds some animals cuter than others. A puppy makes you want to hug it more than a cockroach does, and it's fine to feel that. But liking one more doesn't mean we can hurt the other. It's like at school: you have friends you like more, and you still can't hit the classmates you like less.

## 4. Short answer

It's true: I also like some animals more than others, and in that I'm like everyone else. But speciesism isn't feeling a preference; it's disregarding someone's interests, without justification, because of their species. I can find a dog more likeable than a hen and still recognize that neither should be confined and killed without need. When I discover a bias in myself, the conclusion is to correct it, not to use it as a license.

**Question to ask back:** "If I have this bias too, is the right thing for both of us to try to correct it, or for both of us to keep it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument lumps three different things under the word "speciesism":

| What the argument points to | Is it speciesism? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feeling more sympathy for dogs than for cockroaches | No, it's an affective preference | Feeling isn't the same as treating unjustly |
| Giving different weights to beings with different capacities to feel | No, if the reason is sentience | The criterion is the interest at stake, not the species |
| Ignoring the suffering of a sentient animal just because it belongs to a certain species | Yes | It's the unjustified disregard of interests |
| Deciding for those who can't decide | Not in itself | It depends on whether the decision serves the one cared for or the one deciding |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a tu quoque.** Pointing out that the critic has the same failing is a form of attack on the person: it shifts the subject from the act to the speaker (L11). If all vegans were speciesists, hens and pigs would go on feeling just the same.

**2. The bias exists, and that deserves to be said.** A 2019 study of more than 2,000 people, who chose between photos of animals of various species, found that reported empathy and compassion fall as the evolutionary distance between the species and humans grows. There's no reason to suppose vegans are immune, and within the movement itself it's common to hear the criticism that fish and invertebrates get less attention than mammals.

**3. Preferring isn't discriminating.** The Spanish philosopher Oscar Horta defines speciesism as the unfavorable, and unjustified, consideration or treatment of those who don't belong to a certain species. The word that matters is "unjustified". Liking your own dog more doesn't harm the cockroach. Confining and killing a hen for food, when there's an alternative, harms the hen.

**4. A difference in treatment with a reason isn't speciesism.** Giving more weight to a pig than to an oyster can rest on the evidence of sentience, not on species as such (A3, F6). The criterion is the same for all: similar interests deserve similar consideration.

**5. A discovered bias calls for correction.** Knowing that our empathy favors animals that resemble us is a reason to distrust it and look more carefully at fish, birds and invertebrates. The argument draws the opposite conclusion: if everyone is biased, then it makes no difference.

**6. Deciding for someone isn't claiming a right to use them.** Parents decide for small children, and no one concludes they may exploit them. Having more power and more knowledge increases responsibility. The superiority invoked in A9 is used to justify use; the care of someone who rescues, neuters or feeds an animal runs the other way: the one who benefits is the animal.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: if not even vegans escape it, speciesism is inevitable

The most serious version says: if even people who devote their lives to animals keep preferring some over others, partiality among species is part of human psychology. An ethics that demands total impartiality asks for something no one can deliver, so it can't be demanded of anyone. Besides, the movement really does concentrate its effort on the most charismatic animals, which shows the real criterion is sympathy, not sentience.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Being common doesn't make it right.** Favoring those who resemble us is also a frequent human trait among people, and we don't accept discrimination on that account. We recognize the bias and create rules to contain it.
2. **No one is asking for impartial feelings.** What's asked is more modest: not to cause serious, avoidable harm to those who feel. That's compatible with liking some more than others.
3. **The criticism of the focus on charismatic animals is good, and it leads to more consideration.** If the movement neglects fish, chickens and insects, the correction is to pay attention to them, not to stop paying attention to all.
4. **The counter-reply applies to whoever uses it.** If bias disqualifies the vegan, it also disqualifies those who eat some animals and protect others (E11).

### (f) Back to the moral question

The discussion about vegans' biases is interesting, but it's about vegans. For the hen in the cage, it changes nothing that her defender likes cats more. The question stays the same: is it fair to make those who feel suffer and die when we don't need to? If we all have biases, the best use of that discovery is to correct them together.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Tu quoque | Uses the critic's bias to reject the criticism |
| Logical | Equivocation | Calls any preference or difference in treatment speciesism |
| Logical | False equivalence | Equates liking a dog more with confining and killing a hen |
| Epistemological | From fact to value | Concludes a bias is acceptable because everyone has it |
| Moral | Changing the subject | The vegan's character takes the place of what happens to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Uses the concept it claims to reject.** To accuse the vegan of speciesism, one has to accept that speciesism is a fault.
2. **Demands impartiality from one side only.** Someone who prefers dogs to pigs and eats the pigs doesn't consider themselves disqualified from having a view.
3. **Accepts that adults decide for children.** And sees no license to use them in that.
4. **Corrects biases in other areas.** No one says that, since we all have prejudices, prejudice is allowed.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever has a bias can't criticize that bias and needn't correct it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone who criticizes racism and finds prejudices in themselves | Should stop criticizing racism | The opposite is expected: recognize and correct |
| Judges, who also have biases | Wouldn't need rules of impartiality | The rules exist precisely because of the biases |
| Parents who have a favorite child | Could mistreat the others | Preference doesn't authorize harm |
| Vegans | Should stop talking about speciesism | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets something important right. Vegans do have biases toward charismatic animals, mammals and pets, and fish, invertebrates and wild animals tend to get less attention. It's also right to distrust anyone who talks as if they were free of all partiality. The criticism helps when it leads to the question: which animals are we forgetting?

But the concession has a limit. Having a bias isn't the same as defending unjust treatment, and discovering a bias is a reason to correct it. The argument would only be an objection if its conclusion were "so let's consider more animals". When the conclusion is "so we can keep using all of them", it turns a common human failing into a license.
