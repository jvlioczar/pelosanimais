# F10. "What about meat that gets thrown away?"

**Thesis:** eating something that would be thrown away anyway, without creating demand, is a different case from buying meat. But it's a rare case that doesn't describe everyday eating, and it still has a cost: it reinforces the idea that animals are food. Besides, waste isn't an argument for continuing to produce; it's one more reason to produce less. It's also the reasoning of freeganism, which recovers discarded food, including animal products, so as not to feed consumption: a serious position that holds for genuine leftovers, but not for everyday meat.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If it'll be thrown out, better to eat it. Party leftovers don't count."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The harm to the animal happens in raising and slaughter, not in eating.
2. **P2.** If the meat has already been produced and will be thrown away, eating it doesn't increase demand.
3. **P3.** If there's no increase in demand, eating that meat causes no new harm.
4. **C.** So eating meat that would go in the trash isn't wrong.

P1, P2 and P3 may be true in specific cases. The argument is stronger than it looks. The problem is that it only holds for those cases, and even in them there are indirect effects.

The argument is **valid** and perhaps **sound** for the strict case. It fails when used to justify ordinary consumption.

## 3. Answer for children

If there's a piece of meat left over at a party and it's going in the trash, eating it or not changes nothing for that animal, who has already died. But if people get used to thinking "it's fine to eat it", they keep buying more meat. And then more animals die. The best thing is to have less meat left over, by buying less.

## 4. Short answer

It's true that eating something that would be thrown away anyway, without anyone buying more because of it, doesn't increase demand. It's a rare and different case. But it doesn't describe everyday life: most meat is bought, and every purchase creates demand. And even leftovers have a cost: eating them reinforces the idea that animals are food, for whoever eats and whoever watches. Waste is a reason to produce less, not to keep producing.

**Question to ask back:** "Was most of the meat you eat leftovers that would have gone in the trash?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument separates consumption from demand:

| Situation | Does it create demand? | A common case? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buying meat at the store | Yes | Yes |
| Ordering meat at a restaurant | Yes | Yes |
| Eating leftovers that would be binned, unplanned | Perhaps not | No, it's rare |
| Planning to "take the leftovers" every time | Yes, leftovers become expected | No |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a rare case.** Almost all meat consumed is bought. The leftovers case doesn't describe everyday eating (K1).

**2. Recurring leftovers create demand.** If someone always eats the leftovers, whoever cooks starts counting on it, and the leftovers stop being leftovers.

**3. There are indirect effects.** Eating meat, even meat that would be thrown away, reinforces the idea that animals are food, for whoever eats and whoever watches. That affects the willingness to change.

**4. Waste is part of the problem.** According to the FAO, about a third of the food produced in the world is lost or wasted. With meat, that means animals raised and killed for nothing. The answer is to produce less, not to normalize consumption.

**5. The argument changes the subject.** The question was about buying meat, not about what to do with leftovers.

**6. Freeganism takes the argument seriously, and shows its limits.** The word *freegan* combines *free* and *vegan*. Freegans are people who, in protest against consumerism and waste, avoid buying and eat largely from discarded food. According to the sociologist Alex Barnard, most participants in the New York freegan group she studied were vegetarian or vegan before becoming freegan, and some of them began eating animal products recovered from the trash, with this chapter's reasoning: if it was going to be thrown away, it creates no demand. It's a consistent position for the strict case, but it holds only for that case. For whoever buys meat, nothing changes, and the indirect effect of treating the animal as food remains. The debate on whether this is compatible with veganism is on the page [The definition of veganism, debated](../alem/definicao-de-veganismo.html).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: wasting it also disrespects the animal

The most serious version says that throwing away the meat of an animal who has already died is a form of disrespect, and that eating it honors, in some way, the life that was taken.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The animal doesn't benefit.** Eating or not eating changes nothing for them.
2. **Respect would lie in not killing.** Honoring the life taken would mean preventing others from being taken.
3. **The counter-reply doesn't apply to ordinary consumption.** It's still about a rare case.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Fallacy of accident (converse) | A rare case becomes a defense of ordinary consumption |
| Epistemological | Ignoring indirect effects | Assumes eating doesn't affect norms or future demand |
| Moral | Changing the subject | From buying to leftovers |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Buys meat, not just leftovers.** The case doesn't describe their own consumption.
2. **Wouldn't eat leftovers from any animal.** Leftover dog meat wouldn't be eaten "so as not to waste it".
3. **Fights waste by producing more?** Waste is an argument for reducing production.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains F10 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | The rare case serves to excuse change in the ordinary case | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Functional categorization | Meat is still seen as food, not as an animal | [Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569805/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if something would be thrown away, consuming it has no moral cost".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discarded products of slave labor | Buying them would be acceptable | Depends on not creating demand |
| Seized ivory | It could be sold | Many countries destroy it so as not to stimulate the market |
| Meat from any animal | All leftovers would be food | Including from animals the person wouldn't eat |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is stronger than many admit. Eating something that really would go in the trash, without creating any demand, is different from buying meat, and some philosophers consider it acceptable. It's also true that food waste is a serious problem.

But the case is rare, the indirect effects exist, and waste points to the need to produce less, not to normalizing consumption.
