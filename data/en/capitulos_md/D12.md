# D12. "I'll pay for my mistakes in my next life"

**Thesis:** the phrase admits that eating animals is a wrong, and only postpones the consequence for the one who eats. But the one paying now is the animal, and no future bill undoes what they suffered. Besides, the traditions that speak of karma and rebirth usually teach the opposite of what the phrase suggests: non-violence (*ahimsa*) is a central virtue in Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism, and the first Buddhist precept forbids taking the life of any sentient being, animals included. Karma is a reason to avoid harm, not a license to cause it.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I know eating meat isn't right. I'll pay for my mistakes in my next life."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Every wrong action has a consequence for the one who does it, in this life or another.
2. **P2.** If I accept paying that consequence, the choice is mine.
3. **C.** So I can keep eating animals, as long as I accept the consequences.

P1 is a religious belief many people hold. The problem lies in P2: the choice doesn't affect only the one choosing. The animal suffers and dies now, and someone accepting to pay later changes nothing for them. A debt doesn't authorize doing the harm.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P2 treats harm done to another as a personal matter.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people believe that when we do something wrong, one day it comes back to us. But in the meantime, the one who suffers is the animal. Paying later doesn't take away the pain they felt. The best thing is not to do the wrong in the first place, rather than saving the bill for later.

## 4. Short answer

You're saying it's a wrong, and I agree. But the one paying now is the animal, and no future bill undoes what they suffered. And the traditions that speak of karma and rebirth teach precisely non-violence toward all beings: in Buddhism, the first precept is not to take the life of any sentient being, animals included. Karma is a reason to stop, not to carry on.

**Question to ask back:** "If it's a wrong you'll pay for, why not stop doing it now?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The phrase concedes the moral point and changes the subject:

| What the phrase admits | What it changes | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eating animals is a wrong | The question becomes about the consequence for the one who eats | The harm the animal suffers now |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The phrase admits the wrong.** No one expects to pay for something right. As in E12, the moral point has already been conceded, and what remains is the decision to act.

**2. The one paying now is the animal.** The eater's debt is future and uncertain. The animal's suffering is present and real. Accepting to pay later doesn't authorize causing the harm, just as accepting to go to prison doesn't authorize stealing.

**3. The traditions of karma teach non-violence.** *Ahimsa*, non-violence toward all living beings, is a central virtue in Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism. In Buddhism, the first of the five precepts is not to take the life of any sentient being, and it includes animals. In Jainism, non-violence is the most important principle and leads to strict vegetarianism.

**4. Honesty about the tradition.** Not all of these traditions require vegetarianism. In the early Buddhist texts, the Buddha accepted the food he was offered, meat included, as long as the animal hadn't been killed for him, and Theravada monks follow this rule to this day. But the rule was about accepting alms, not about buying meat, and later Mahayana sutras condemn eating meat. In none of these traditions is knowing you're doing wrong a license to carry on.

**5. Karma is about intention.** In many Buddhist readings, what weighs in karma is intention (*cetanā*). Choosing to carry on with something you know is wrong, knowing it causes suffering, is exactly the kind of intention these traditions teach people to avoid.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: my faith says justice comes later, and it's not your place to judge

The most serious version says: in my belief, justice is cosmic or divine, and each person answers for their own acts. No one needs to hold anyone to account here; the bill will be settled in due time.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **No one is judging the person.** The conversation is about the animal, who suffers now, not about the fate of the one who eats.
2. **Future justice doesn't protect the present animal.** Even if the bill is settled later, the harm will already have happened.
3. **The beliefs themselves ask that the wrong be avoided.** No tradition teaches that, because there is punishment later, it's acceptable to do wrong now.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the subject | Moves from the harm to the animal to the consequence for the one who eats |

| Moral | Akrasia | Admits the wrong and decides to carry on |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Admits it's a wrong.** But carries on.
2. **Believes in karma.** But uses the belief to justify what it condemns.
3. **Says the choice is theirs.** But the immediate consequence falls on another.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "I can do something I know is wrong, as long as I accept paying for it later".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Theft | Whoever accepts going to prison could steal | Absurd |
| Assault | Whoever accepts the punishment could assault | Absurd |
| Any harm | Accepting the punishment would be enough to make the harm acceptable | Punishment doesn't undo the harm |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right to admit the wrong, which is more honest than denying it. It's also right that actions have consequences, and that each person's faith deserves respect.

But if it's a wrong, the best moment to stop it is now. The traditions that speak of karma are, almost all of them, traditions of non-violence.
