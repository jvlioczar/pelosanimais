# M4. "Being vegan is hard if you travel a lot"

**Thesis:** traveling takes more planning for vegans, and sometimes the options are few. But almost every place has something without animal products: rice, beans, bread, fruit, vegetables, nuts. Restaurant-finder apps, supermarkets and a snack in the bag solve most cases. The difficulty calls for planning, not for exceptions. Real necessity, having no food at all without animal products, is extremely rare when traveling, and the very definition of veganism ("as far as is possible and practicable") already covers genuine impossibility. In any case, it would never justify giving up veganism at home, where most meals are eaten.

**Family:** M. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I travel a lot for work. Being vegan on the road, in airports, in small towns is impossible. I gave up."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** People who travel a lot depend on what they find along the way.
2. **P2.** In many places, vegan options are few or nonexistent.
3. **P3.** No one is obliged to go hungry or to eat poorly.
4. **C.** So people who travel a lot aren't obliged to be vegan.

P1 and P3 are true. P2 is sometimes true, but it's usually exaggerated. And the conclusion goes too far: even if some meals on a trip were difficult, that wouldn't justify eating animals at home or in places where there are options.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: it extends the difficulty of a few situations to a whole life.

## 3. Answer for children

When we travel, we can bring a snack in our backpack: fruit, nuts, a sandwich. And almost everywhere there's rice, beans, bread or fruit. With a little planning, you can travel without eating animals. And even if a trip were hard, that wouldn't be a reason to go back to eating animals at home.

## 4. Short answer

Traveling does take more planning. But almost every place has something vegan, even if it's simple: rice, beans, bread, fruit, salad. A snack in your bag, a restaurant app like HappyCow, a supermarket and a question to the server solve most cases. Your difficulty calls for planning, not for a general license. Having truly no food without animal products is extremely rare, and the very definition of veganism, "as far as is possible and practicable", already covers that case. And none of this would justify dropping veganism at home. For the animals, it makes no difference whether you're at home or in another city: what they lose is the same.

**Question to ask back:** "In how many of your meals this week do you really have no option at all?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses the hardest, and rarest, case to decide all the others:

| Situation | Is it really hard? | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At home | No | Eat vegan |
| In a big city | Rarely | Restaurants and supermarkets |
| On the road | Sometimes | Snacks in the bag, simple dishes, supermarkets, asking |
| In a remote place | Sometimes | Plan ahead: bring food, research the destination |
| No food without animal products at all, not even in the bag | Extremely rare | Not an exception: the definition of veganism ("as far as is possible and practicable") already covers genuine impossibility |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The difficulty concerns some meals, not all of them.** Even people who travel a lot eat a good share of their meals in places with options. The conclusion of giving everything up doesn't follow from a few difficult meals.

**2. Almost every place has basic vegan food.** Rice, beans, bread, pasta with tomato sauce, fruit, vegetables, nuts. It may not be a special meal, but it's food.

**3. Planning solves most cases.** Bringing snacks, looking up restaurants in apps like HappyCow, which lists places with vegan options in more than 185 countries, choosing accommodation with a kitchen, asking what goes into each dish, asking for adapted dishes. That's what people with allergies, religious restrictions or celiac disease do.

**4. The options are growing.** Restaurants, fast-food chains and supermarkets offer more and more vegan products, including on highways and in airports.

**5. Difficulty calls for planning, not for exceptions.** A difficult meal is a problem of organization, not a necessity. Real necessity would be having, in fact, no food at all without animal products, neither on site nor in the bag, and that is extremely rare for travelers, even to remote places.

**6. Genuine impossibility is already built into veganism.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable". Someone who, in a genuine case, truly has nothing else to eat isn't making an exception to veganism: they are at the limit the definition itself recognizes. And even then, it would never license eating animals at home, where most meals are eaten.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |

| [The Vegan Society, definition of veganism](https://www.vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism) | Defines veganism as excluding the exploitation of and cruelty to animals "as far as is possible and practicable" | Definition by the organization that coined the term, in 1944 |
| [Vegconomist, 2024](https://vegconomist.com/company-news/vegan-platform-happycow-celebrates-25th-anniversary/) | HappyCow lists more than 240,000 businesses with vegan options and is used in more than 185 countries | Figures reported by the company itself |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: it's exhausting to live always planning

The most serious version says: the problem isn't one meal, but the fatigue of always having to plan, search, ask and explain. For someone who travels every week, that weighs on them.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Planning becomes a habit.** After a few trips, a person knows what to bring, where to stop and what to order. The effort shrinks over time.
2. **Fatigue justifies simplifying, not giving up.** Eating the simplest dish, always bringing the same snack, having a few trusted places.
3. **Fatigue isn't impossibility.** The fatigue of planning makes a trip more work, but it doesn't leave a person with nothing to eat. It calls for simpler routines, not for exceptions.
4. **The cost to animals doesn't shrink with fatigue.** The discomfort of planning is still much smaller than the harm avoided.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if traveling made veganism more work, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die to avoid the work of planning a meal? Most of the time, the answer is a snack in the bag. When not even that is possible, which is extremely rare, there is no choice to justify, and veganism itself, "as far as is possible and practicable", already recognizes that limit. In no case does travel change what one eats at home.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | From the difficulty of a few meals, concludes everything is impossible |
| Logical | From the hard case to the rule | Uses the remote place to decide what to do at home |
| Logical | Confusing difficulty with necessity | Treats a meal that takes work as if it were an impossible meal |
| Moral | Forgetting who is affected | Weighs the work of planning, not the harm to the animal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Plans the rest of the trip.** Tickets, hotel, meetings, itinerary.
2. **People with medical restrictions travel.** And they eat by planning.
3. **Also gave up at home.** Where travel is no obstacle.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M4 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Availability bias | The difficult meal is remembered, the easy ones are forgotten | Linked to J5 |
| Status quo bias | The usual menu seems the only one possible | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a rule is hard in some situations, it can be dropped in all of them".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Religious restriction | Someone who travels could stop following their religion at home too | No one accepts this |
| Celiac disease | Someone who travels could always eat gluten | Absurd |
| Recycling | Someone who can't find a recycling bin on a trip could stop recycling at home | Absurd |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that traveling takes more planning, that in some places the options really are few and that the fatigue of always planning weighs on people. It's also right that no one should go hungry because of a rule.

But difficulty calls for planning, not for exceptions. A real lack of any food without animal products is extremely rare when traveling, and the definition of veganism already covers genuine impossibility. And none of this would justify giving up veganism at home, where most meals are eaten. The good answer is to share travel strategies.
