# M4. "It's impossible to solve suffering in nature; it's too big and complex"

**Thesis:** not being able to solve everything is no reason to do nothing. There are already ways of helping wild animals that work, such as vaccinating foxes against rabies, which helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe, and rescuing animals in disasters. And the most important part today is cheap: researching, taking the problem seriously, and not treating it as absurd.

**Family:** M. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "There are trillions of animals. We'll never end predation, hunger and disease. It's pointless."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We only have a duty to do what is possible.
2. **P2.** Eliminating suffering in nature is impossible, because of the scale and complexity of ecosystems.
3. **C.** So we have no duty regarding the suffering of wild animals.

P1 is true ("ought implies can"). P2 is also true, if we mean eliminating all suffering. The mistake is moving from "we can't solve everything" to "we have no duty at all".

The argument is **invalid**: the conclusion would only follow if the only possible action were solving everything at once.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "There are trillions of animals, it's pointless." |
| Everyday conversation | "We'll never end predation." |
| Activism | "It's an impossible cause; let's focus on what we can do." |
| Social media | "Are you going to give medicine to every ant?" |
| Academic version | "The problem is intractable at the relevant scale." |
| Disguised version | "One person doesn't make a difference." (which is K1) |

## 3. Answer for children

No one can clean the whole world alone. But if everyone picks up the litter where they are, things get better. It's similar with animals in nature: we can't help all of them at once, but we can already help some, and scientists can find ways to help many more.

## 4. Short answer

We'll never end all human diseases either, and we don't stop treating those we can. There are already ways to help wild animals: vaccinating foxes against rabies helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe. And the first step is cheap: researching and taking the problem seriously.

**Question to ask back:** "Since we can't end all the poverty in the world, do you think we shouldn't help anyone?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses "solve" in an all-or-nothing sense:

| What is asked | Possible today? |
| --- | --- |
| Eliminating all suffering in nature | No |
| Helping some animals in specific situations | Yes, it's already done |
| Researching the wellbeing of wild animals | Yes, it has begun |
| Considering wild animals in decisions we already make | Yes |
| Spreading the idea that this suffering matters | Yes |

The duty under discussion is the one in the lower rows, not the first.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. We already help, and it works.** Oral rabies vaccination of foxes, begun in Switzerland in the late 1970s, helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe, protecting other animals and people as well. There are also rescues in floods and fires, treatment of injured animals, and vaccination of endangered animals.

**2. Today's impossible can be tomorrow's possible.** Problems that seemed too big, such as smallpox, eradicated in 1980, became tractable through research and coordinated effort. Starting with study is the way, and that's why a whole field, welfare biology, is proposed to study how wild animals live and what would help them.

**3. The human test.** We won't end all poverty, hunger and disease among humans. No one concludes from this that helping people is useless. The scale of a problem is a reason to prioritize well, not to give up.

**4. Scale increases importance.** If trillions of animals suffer, even small improvements at scale can prevent a great deal of suffering. The size of the problem is an argument for taking it seriously.

**5. The first task is changing attitudes.** Today, the biggest barrier isn't technical: it's the idea that this suffering doesn't matter. Changing that costs little and is a precondition for everything else.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: opportunity cost

The most serious version says: resources are limited. Every dollar spent on wild animals doesn't go to exploited animals, where we know what to do. It would be better to focus on the tractable problem.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It concedes the problem matters.** Discussing priorities already admits there's a duty, and the question becomes how to distribute efforts.
2. **Research is cheap and opens paths.** What's proposed today is mainly study and outreach, which costs little compared with what is spent on other causes.
3. **The causes reinforce each other.** Teaching that every sentient animal matters, whoever caused the harm, also strengthens the fight against exploitation (M8).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If everything can't be solved, nothing is worth doing |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either we eliminate all suffering, or we do nothing |
| Epistemological | Ignorance of what already exists | Ignores interventions that already work |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts partial help for humans and denies it to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports partial help for humans.** Vaccines, donations and relief don't solve everything and are still valued.
2. **Approves of rescuing animals in disasters.** Which is partial help for wild animals.
3. **Doesn't say medicine is useless.** Though it will never end all disease.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M4 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nirvana fallacy | The unreachable ideal becomes an excuse not to do what's possible | Linked to L5 |
| Scope insensitivity | Huge numbers stop moving us and start paralyzing us | Linked to K1 |
| Omission bias | Not acting seems safer and less serious | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |
| Proportion bias | Helping millions seems pointless when they are a tiny fraction of trillions | [Bartels, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2005.10.004) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if we can't solve the whole problem, we have no duty to help".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human poverty | Giving or helping would be pointless | No one accepts this |
| Disease | Medicine would be pointless, because there will always be disease | Absurd |
| Animal exploitation | Veganism would be pointless, because farming won't end tomorrow | The same reasoning is rejected in K1 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that the problem is immense and complex, that we can't solve it today, and that large, poorly planned interventions would be irresponsible. It's also right that priorities have to be chosen.

But the answer to complexity is research and caution, not indifference. What we already do shows that helping is possible, and what we don't yet know how to do is exactly what's worth studying.
