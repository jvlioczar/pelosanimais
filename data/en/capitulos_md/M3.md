# M3. "Being vegan limited me when eating at my relatives' homes"

**Thesis:** the discomfort of eating at family homes is one of the most real costs of veganism, because it touches on affection, tradition and belonging. But it has well-known solutions: bringing a dish to share, talking beforehand, eating whatever on the table is vegan. In the Faunalytics survey, 13% of former vegetarians and vegans cited social issues as a reason for giving up, and 63% didn't like standing out. Awkwardness at the table, however real, doesn't outweigh an animal's life.

**Family:** M. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Being vegan limited me. Every time I went to my mom's or my grandma's, it was a problem. They got upset, and I ate almost nothing."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Shared food is an important part of family bonds.
2. **P2.** Refusing the family's food causes hurt and distance.
3. **P3.** Family bonds are an important good, worth protecting.
4. **C.** So it's reasonable to leave veganism to preserve family life.

P1, P2 and P3 are largely true. The problem lies in the conclusion: it assumes there are only two ways out, refusing everything or eating everything, and that the good of family bonds outweighs the lives of the animals served.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: there are other ways to preserve family life, and the cost to the animals is left out of the account.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "At my grandma's, there was nothing to eat." |
| Everyday conversation | "My mom got hurt when I turned down her food." |
| Everyday conversation | "At Christmas, I was the family's problem." |
| Social media | "Veganism pushed me away from my family." |
| Tradition version | "Barbecues bring the family together." (which is D3) |
| Practicality version | "Eating out, traveling and parties become impossible." (which is J4) |

## 3. Answer for children

At grandma's house, food is a way of showing love. Someone who doesn't eat meat can bring a tasty dish for everyone to try, and explain kindly that they don't eat animals because animals feel pain. That way, the family stays together, and no one has to hurt an animal.

## 4. Short answer

This is one of the most real costs, because it involves affection, not just food. But there are ways out: letting people know beforehand, bringing a dish for everyone and eating what's already vegan on the table, like rice, beans and salad. Many families adapt over time, especially when they realize the person isn't rejecting anyone. The discomfort of a few meals doesn't outweigh the lives of animals.

**Question to ask back:** "If you brought a dish to share, would your family turn it down?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument imagines only two paths, when there are several:

| Path | Effect on the family | Effect on the animals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refusing everything, without talking | Hurt | Spares animals |
| Eating everything so as not to hurt anyone | No conflict | Doesn't spare them |
| Talking beforehand and bringing a dish | Little or no conflict | Spares animals |
| Eating whatever on the table is vegan | Little conflict | Spares animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the food being refused, not the person.** Much of the hurt comes from the family feeling that their affection was rejected. Explaining calmly, praising what you do eat and thanking them for the invitation changes the conversation a lot.

**2. Bringing a dish changes the dynamic.** Instead of arriving with a restriction, the person arrives with a contribution. Many families end up trying it and even asking for the recipe.

**3. Many family tables already have plenty that's vegan.** Rice, beans cooked without meat, side dishes, salad, vegetables, fruit. In many cases, it's enough to ask for some of the beans to be set aside before the bacon goes in.

**4. Other restrictions are already respected.** Families adapt to relatives with diabetes, allergies, celiac disease or religious restrictions. Adapting to veganism is the same kind of thing.

**5. The social cost is real, but it usually shrinks.** Over time, the family gets used to it, and the person also learns what to do. In the Faunalytics survey, 34% of former vegetarians and vegans gave up in the first three months, precisely when these situations are still new.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Faunalytics, 2014](https://faunalytics.org/a-summary-of-faunalytics-study-of-current-and-former-vegetarians-and-vegans/) | Social issues were the reason for giving up for 13%; 63% didn't like standing out; 34% gave up within three months | Survey conducted in 2014 in the United States |
| [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) | Omnivores rated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups that are common targets of prejudice | Helps explain why the pressure is strong |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: family matters more than a food choice

The most serious version says: family bonds are one of the most important goods in life. A food choice shouldn't cost someone their relationship with their mother or grandmother.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **You rarely have to choose.** There's almost always a way to keep family life going without eating animals. The choice between family and veganism is usually a false one.
2. **It isn't just a food choice.** There are others affected: the animals. The fair comparison isn't between family and a taste, but between the comfort of a few meals and the lives of animals.
3. **Giving in on everything doesn't protect the family.** Strong relationships can handle differences. Hiding your own convictions to avoid conflict also has a cost.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if eating at family homes became harder, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die to avoid discomfort at the table? The family's affection is real, and it can be kept without that.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either refuse everything, or eat everything |
| Moral | Forgetting the one affected | Weighs comfort at the table, not the animal's life |
| Moral | Confusing affection with food | Treats refusing meat as refusing the person |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **The family adapts to other restrictions.** Diabetes, allergies, religion.
2. **The person themselves thought animals mattered.** So much that they became vegan.
3. **Says family is what matters most.** But accepts the idea that the family wouldn't tolerate a difference.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M3 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social conformity | Going back to the norm relieves the tension | [Faunalytics, 2014](https://faunalytics.org/a-summary-of-faunalytics-study-of-current-and-former-vegetarians-and-vegans/) |
| Prejudice against vegans | The pressure on those who are different is strong | [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) |
| Status quo bias | The usual menu seems irreplaceable | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "to preserve family life, it's worth giving up a moral conviction".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A family that tells racist jokes | You'd have to laugh along to avoid conflict | Unacceptable |
| A family that drinks and drives | You'd have to get in the car | Unacceptable |
| Religious restriction | The person should eat what their religion forbids | No one asks for this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that food is part of affection, that refusing it can hurt and that family bonds matter a great deal. It's also right that many vegans don't know how to handle these situations and end up creating unnecessary conflicts.

But the problem has a solution: talking, a dish to share and patience. The good answer to someone who went through this is to share strategies, not to judge.
