# L21. "Vegans care more about animals than people"

**Thesis:** it's a false dilemma and a generalization. Empathy isn't a zero-sum resource: caring about animals doesn't reduce caring about people. A small 2010 neuroimaging study found that vegetarians and vegans showed greater activation of empathy-related areas in response to human suffering too; it's a hint, not proof. And many animal advocates also work for human rights. It's worth noting the other side: the overwhelming majority of people care more about humans than about animals, and research suggests this is largely due to a bias, speciesism. Even those who keep that preference don't have in it a license to use animals when they don't need to.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You cry for the cow but not for the homeless. Vegans hate people."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some vegans show more indignation at animal suffering than at human suffering.
2. **P2.** Humans should have moral priority.
3. **P3.** Whoever reverses that priority has distorted values.
4. **C.** So veganism reflects distorted values.

P1 may be true of some. The problem lies in the conclusion: it generalizes from a few and assumes that caring about animals takes care away from humans.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it generalizes and assumes a competition that doesn't exist.

## 3. Answer for children

The heart has no limit on how many people or animals we can love. Caring for your dog doesn't make you love your family less. It's the same with vegans: caring for animals doesn't make anyone care less about people.

## 4. Short answer

Empathy isn't zero-sum: caring about animals doesn't reduce caring about people. A small 2010 study found that vegetarians and vegans showed greater activation of brain areas linked to empathy in response to human suffering too. Many animal advocates work for human rights, against hunger and for social justice. And no longer eating animals takes nothing away from anyone. In fact, almost everyone cares more about humans than about animals, and research indicates that this is largely a bias; but, even for those who think that way, caring more about people doesn't give a right to make animals suffer and die without need.

**Question to ask back:** "What would you stop doing for people if you stopped eating animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assumes empathy is limited and competitive:

| Assumption | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Those who care about animals care less about humans | No evidence; there are hints of the opposite |
| Vegans in general despise humans | A baseless generalization |
| No longer eating animals takes something from humans | False (J20) |
| Caring more about humans legitimizes using animals | It's what almost everyone does, and it's largely a bias; preferring doesn't license exploiting |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a false dilemma.** No one has to choose between animals and humans.

**2. Empathy isn't zero-sum.** Filippi and colleagues found in 2010 that vegetarians and vegans showed greater activation of empathy-related regions in response to scenes of human suffering, not less. The study is small, with 60 people, and measures brain activation, not behavior; but it points in the opposite direction from the stereotype.

**3. It's a generalization.** Judging all vegans by a few is unfair, as it would be for any group.

**4. Many animal advocates work for humans.** The animal movement has strong ties to social justice movements.

**5. Livestock farming also affects humans.** Slaughterhouse workers, communities affected by deforestation and public health (J20).

**6. Those who care more about humans are the vast majority, and that's largely a bias.** The accusation treats the vegan who is moved by animals as odd, but the common pattern is the opposite. In studies by Caviola and colleagues published in 2022, most participants ranked humans above animals even when the animals were described as having equal or greater mental capacities; the authors concluded that speciesism, the preference for one's own species as such, explains much of that hierarchy. Another study, from 2021, with children aged 5 to 9 and adults, found that children prioritize humans over animals much less than adults do, which suggests the strong hierarchy is partly learned; the authors themselves treat that conclusion with caution. Part of the priority given to humans may have reasons, such as differences in capacities in cases of real conflict (A12). But a preference, however understandable, doesn't legitimize using animals: just as loving your own family more doesn't give a right to harm strangers (L16), caring more about humans doesn't give a right to make animals suffer and die when there's no conflict at all, only the habit of eating them.

**7. The accusation is old, and history refutes it.** One version came from part of the left: for the Marxist philosopher Howard Parsons (1977), animal protection was a pursuit of the well-off classes that diverted concern that should go to humans. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane breaks the charge into its parts and answers each one: animal protection isn't only for the rich, and there are strong movements in countries such as India; no one can see into the motives of a whole movement, and suspecting a cause just because it takes a moral stand is unfair; and compassion doesn't have to choose between humans and animals (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 102–105). The 19th-century reformers show this in practice: William Wilberforce fought slavery and was among the founders of the society that would become the RSPCA, and Henry Salt's Humanitarian League combined social reform and animal protection (p. 102; see J20).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some vegans really do despise people

The most serious version points to real cases of vegans who make disdainful remarks about humans or compare people to animals offensively.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Some don't represent all.** There are unpleasant people in every group.
2. **Such behavior should be criticized.** Including by vegans themselves.
3. **It doesn't change the question about animals.** The question is still about them.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Caring about animals doesn't require despising anyone. The moral question stays the same: do animals deserve not to suffer and die without need? Answering yes takes nothing away from people.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L21 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either animals or humans |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Judges all vegans by a few |
| Epistemological | Empathy as zero-sum | Assumes caring for some reduces care for others |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Cares for pets.** And doesn't think that reduces care for people.
2. **Isn't helping the homeless person.** The argument serves to accuse, not to help.
3. **Admires people who care for both animals and people.**

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever cares about one cause cares less about others".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Those who care for the elderly | They'd care less about children | Absurd |
| Environmentalists | They'd care less about people | Absurd |
| Vegans | They'd care less about humans | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Some vegans express themselves badly about humans, and disrespectful comparisons can hurt. Animal advocates should take care that their cause doesn't seem indifferent to human suffering.

But empathy isn't zero-sum, and the little evidence there is points away from the stereotype rather than toward it. There's no need to choose.
