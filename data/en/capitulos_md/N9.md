# N9. "It became an obsession; I lived with guilt"

**Thesis:** perfectionism, guilt and burnout exist among vegans and activists, and they deserve care, including professional help when the distress is serious or when there are signs of an eating disorder. But veganism asks for "as far as is possible and practicable", not purity. Chasing traces on labels and a perfect 100% is a mistake this guide criticizes, not a requirement of animal ethics. The way out of excessive guilt is a veganism with healthy limits, not going back to funding the exploitation of animals.

**Family:** N. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I read every label, researched every brand, and it was never enough. I lived with guilt. Veganism wrecked my mental health. I got tired of carrying the weight of the world."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** As a vegan, I lived watching every detail and feeling guilty about everything I couldn't avoid.
2. **P2.** A practice that makes someone ill can't be demanded of anyone.
3. **P3.** The only alternative to that vigilance was to leave veganism.
4. **C.** So leaving veganism was the right decision.

P1 describes real suffering. P2 is true: mental health counts, and "practicable" includes what a person can bear. The problem lies in P3: between total vigilance and total abandonment there is an ordinary veganism, with no magnifying glass and no guilt over what doesn't depend on the person.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P3 is a false dichotomy.

## 3. Answer for children

Wanting to help animals is a very good thing. But no one can save every animal in the world on their own, and no one has to. If a child feels sad all the time because of this, they need a hug and a rest. Afterward, they can keep helping in the way that's possible: by not eating animals, and by not being so hard on themselves.

## 4. Short answer

What you went through is serious, and your health comes first. If it still weighs on you, it's worth talking to a therapist, and, if food became a source of anguish, to someone who understands eating disorders. But veganism never asked for what you were demanding of yourself: the definition itself says "possible and practicable". You can avoid eating animals without reading the fine print, without blaming yourself for what you don't control, and without watching slaughterhouse footage. The remedy for too much guilt is setting limits, not going back to funding what hurt you to see.

**Question to ask back:** "What weighed on your decision: not eating animals, the pressure to do it perfectly, or both? Can you keep the first without the second?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The account mixes three things with very different weights:

| What weighed | Is it part of veganism? | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not eating or using animals | Yes, it's the core | Keep it, in the simplest way possible |
| Investigating traces, factories and every doubtful ingredient | No: it goes beyond "possible and practicable" | Drop it without guilt (L5) |
| Feeling responsible for all the suffering in the world | No: no one has that duty | Limit exposure, rest, seek support |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The suffering is real and calls for care.** Constant guilt, anxiety around food and exhaustion aren't fussiness. When the distress is serious, the path is professional help. If there are signs of an eating disorder (fear of eating, restriction that keeps growing, weight loss), health comes first, and treatment is led by a specialized team. G19 deals with the relationship between veganism and eating disorders.

**2. Veganism doesn't ask for purity.** The Vegan Society, the British organization founded in 1944 by the people who coined the term, defines veganism as a philosophy and way of living which seeks to exclude, "as far as is possible and practicable", all forms of exploitation of, and cruelty to, animals. The words "possible" and "practicable" are there on purpose. No vegan lives without causing any harm at all (L5), and the definition never promised that.

**3. Chasing traces is a mistake, not a duty.** A "may contain traces of milk" warning signals a risk of accidental contact in a factory that also handles milk: it's an alert for people with allergies, not an ingredient list. The Vegan Society itself allows products carrying that warning to bear its vegan trademark. The milk wasn't bought to make that product, and refusing it spares no animal. The effort spent on this weighs on mental health without ever reaching the animals.

**4. Perfectionism is common, and those who study the subject don't recommend it.** In a Faunalytics study that followed 222 people through their first six months of change, the share agreeing with the statement "I can't feel satisfied unless I follow my dietary goal perfectly" went from 61% to 73%. The authors, even though they found perfectionism went together with closer adherence, cautioned against recommending it, because of the risk of people blaming themselves too much.

**5. No one carries the world alone.** Each person answers for what they do, not for everything that happens. Activist burnout has been studied: a 2019 study of 17 animal rights activists in the United States, by Paul Gorski, Stacy Lopresti-Goodman and Dallas Rising, describes dedicated people who stepped away out of exhaustion, and lists among the causes a culture that demands sacrifice and looks down on self-care. Activism is optional and can have breaks. Not eating animals doesn't require watching a single video.

**6. Going back to eating animals doesn't treat the cause.** If the problem was perfectionism or anxiety, they need care of their own, with or without veganism. And there is a cost to consider: someone who cared about animals to the point of becoming ill probably still cares.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: for me, any food rule reignites the obsession

The most serious version says: it wasn't just perfectionism. For some people, especially those with a history of eating disorders, any list of forbidden foods works as a trigger. For them, eating with no rules at all is part of the treatment.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **In those cases, health comes first.** If the team caring for the person recommends dietary flexibility, that is what should be done. "Practicable" includes mental health, and no one should feel guilty for getting treatment.
2. **The exception covers what the treatment requires, for as long as it requires it.** It doesn't show that animal exploitation has become acceptable, nor does it apply to people who aren't in that situation, who are the great majority.
3. **Veganism goes beyond the plate.** Even someone who needs flexibility with food can avoid leather, fur, products tested on animals and shows that use animals, none of which touch on eating.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Guilt, tiredness and anxiety say a lot about how the person was living veganism, and nothing about the animals. They go on feeling pain and fear whether or not someone is too hard on themselves. The moral question was never "are you pure?". It was always: is it fair to make an animal suffer and die when it can be avoided? Answering it with one's life takes only what is possible and practicable, and that includes taking care of oneself.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either total vigilance, or total abandonment |
| Logical | False cause | Attributes to veganism what came from perfectionism and a lack of limits |
| Epistemological | Hasty generalization | Concludes from one's own experience that veganism makes people ill |
| Moral | Changing the question | Swaps "what is fair to the animals?" for "was I being pure enough?" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Suffered from caring about animals, and the solution was to go back to eating them.** The reason for the pain still exists, now with the person's participation.
2. **Rejects the demand for perfection, but accepts it as the definition.** Leaving veganism for not being perfect only makes sense if it required perfection, and it doesn't.
3. **In other areas, the person would adjust the dose.** Someone who burns out at work looks for limits and rest, and doesn't conclude they should never work again.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if fulfilling a duty in a perfectionist way made me ill, the duty no longer exists".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A parent exhausted from trying to be perfect | They could stop caring for their children | What people do is ask for help and ease the pressure |
| A nurse worn out from caring | They could start mistreating patients | They cut the load and rest, and the patients still matter |
| A scrupulous person tormented by the fear of lying by accident | They could start lying freely | The excessive scruple gets treated, and honesty still holds |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that excessive guilt makes people ill, that activist burnout is real and that part of the movement feeds a culture of purity and sacrifice that does harm. It's right that mental health comes before any ingredient list, and that seeking help is a sign of good sense, not weakness. Whoever tells this story deserves a warm welcome, not one more demand.

But what caused the illness was the demand for perfection, which veganism doesn't make. The answer is to change the way of doing it: simple rules, clear limits, rest and support. That way the person stays well, and the animals don't go back to paying for the exhaustion of someone who tried to protect them.
