# F13. "Vegan restaurants also call the exterminator and kill insects"

**Thesis:** it's true that vegan restaurants, like any food business, carry out pest control, and that local councils spray insecticide in parks, schools and streets. Many vegans face this at home too: the building's body corporate sprays the common areas, the lease asks for the property to be treated, termites attack the timber. But a restaurant doesn't get to choose whether to control pests: food-safety rules require every food business to keep the premises free of cockroaches, rats and flies, and Brazil's own rule says to prevent first and use chemical control only when prevention doesn't work. Pest control responds to a real conflict with animals who contaminate food; raising animals to eat brings billions of victims into existence with no conflict at all. Public spraying and the spraying of a building are decided by the authorities or by the owners' meeting, not by each resident. And those who eat meat escape none of this: animal farming also uses insecticides, in poultry sheds, for example. The consistent path is to demand the methods that kill the fewest, not to give up on sparing those who can be spared.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** killing mosquitoes, rats and cockroaches yourself is covered in F6; this chapter is about pest control carried out by companies, in restaurants, apartment buildings, rented homes and public places

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That vegan restaurant gets the exterminator in every month. And the council sprays poison in the park and you don't say a word. In your building, the body corporate sprays the car park and you pay the levy. So you kill animals too."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegan restaurants carry out pest control, and the buildings and houses vegans live in are sprayed too, which kills insects and rodents.
2. **P2.** Vegans eat at and support these restaurants, and they pay the taxes that fund the spraying of public places.
3. **P3.** If veganism were consistent, vegans would refuse everything that kills animals.
4. **C.** So vegans also accept killing animals when it's convenient, and can't condemn those who eat meat.

P1 is true. P2 is true, but opening a restaurant, living in an apartment building or living in a city isn't choosing to kill insects: pest control is required by law of every food business, vegan or not, and spraying a building is decided collectively. P3 confuses veganism with a promise never to cause any death; the most widely used definition talks about avoiding exploitation and cruelty "as far as is possible and practicable". And the conclusion doesn't follow: even if a vegan fell short here, that wouldn't make raising and slaughtering animals acceptable.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which demands a purity veganism doesn't promise, and in the leap to the conclusion, which turns a possible inconsistency into permission to cause more harm.

## 3. Answer for children

Every kitchen that cooks for lots of people has to stay clean and free of cockroaches, rats and flies, because they can make people sick. That's true of every restaurant, vegan or not, and of the buildings we live in too. But there are ways to keep these animals away without killing them: closing up the holes, keeping food in sealed containers, taking the rubbish out every day. Poison is only for when none of that works. That's very different from raising a chicken just to eat her later: the chicken wasn't bothering anyone.

## 4. Short answer

Every restaurant, vegan or not, is required by law to keep its kitchen free of cockroaches, rats and flies, because they contaminate food. And Brazil's own rule says to prevent first and use poison only when prevention isn't enough. That's a real conflict with animals who threaten health, and it isn't the same as raising animals to eat. Spraying parks and schools is decided by the council, and spraying the building by the body corporate; what a vegan can do, at home and in the city, is push for prevention and methods that kill fewer. And those who eat meat don't escape it: animal farming also uses insecticides, in poultry sheds, for example. If these deaths can be reduced, the way is to demand methods that kill fewer, not to kill more.

**Question to ask back:** "If the vegan restaurant calls the exterminator, and so does the one serving chicken, what changes when you choose the chicken?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument lumps different situations together:

| Situation | Who decides | Is there a real conflict? | What reduces the deaths |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pest control in a restaurant kitchen | Food-safety law requires it; the owner chooses the method | Yes, cockroaches, rats and flies contaminate food | Sealing, cleaning, closed bins, monitoring; poison only if prevention fails |
| Spraying parks, schools and streets | The authorities | Sometimes, as in dengue outbreaks | Removing breeding sites, managing the environment, methods like *Wolbachia* |
| Spraying an apartment building or a rented home | The owners' meeting, the building manager or the lease | Sometimes; often it's done as routine | Push for sealing, cleaning and companies that apply products only where there are pests |
| Termites in the house's timber | The resident | Yes, they can damage the structure | Controlling moisture, keeping timber off the soil, inspecting, treating only where there's an infestation |
| Insect control in poultry sheds | The farmer | Yes, but the conflict exists because the birds were confined there to become food | Not raising the animals |
| Raising animals to eat | The buyer | No, the animal threatens no one | Other foods |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. A restaurant doesn't choose whether to control pests.** In Brazil, ANVISA's resolution RDC 216/2004 requires every food service to keep its premises free of vectors and urban pests, with ongoing prevention. In Australia, Standard 3.2.2 of the Food Standards Code requires a business to do everything it reasonably can to prevent pest problems. This applies to every restaurant, vegan or not, just as it applies to hospitals, schools and supermarkets.

**2. The rule itself puts poison last.** RDC 216 says chemical control should be used when prevention measures aren't effective, and carried out by a specialized company. It's the same order defended in F6: prevent first, then use the least harmful means, and kill only as a last resort. Integrated pest management, recommended by agencies such as the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), follows the same logic: monitor, prevent, and apply products only when needed, leaving broadcast spraying as a last resort.

**3. It's a conflict, not exploitation.** Cockroaches, rats and flies in a kitchen threaten the health of the people who eat there. That's different from bringing billions of animals into existence each year in order to kill them. Veganism is about avoidable exploitation and cruelty, "as far as is possible and practicable", and doesn't promise that no one will ever cause an animal's death.

**4. Public spraying isn't the taxpayer's choice.** Whoever decides to spray insecticide in a park or send out the fogging truck is the authorities. A vegan can, like any citizen, push for better methods, but can't stop paying tax or single-handedly prevent the spraying. Blaming each taxpayer for every government decision would also mean blaming meat-eaters for each one of them.

**5. At home, vegans often don't decide alone.** People who live in an apartment building pay the levy for spraying decided by the building manager or at the owners' meeting; renters may have to hand the property back treated, if the lease asks for it. What can be done is to propose alternatives: sealing gaps and drains, keeping common areas and bins clean, hiring companies that work with integrated pest management and apply products only where there are pests, not as routine. With termites, which can damage the timber and structure of a house, the conflict is real, and the order is the same as in F6: prevent (control moisture, keep timber off the soil, inspect), treat only where there's an infestation, and choose the method that causes the least harm.

**6. Prevention protects more, in public places too.** Against dengue, fogging kills only the adult mosquitoes it reaches and is reserved for outbreaks; the main measure is removing breeding sites. The *Wolbachia* method, which doesn't kill off mosquito populations, cut dengue by 77% in a randomized trial in Indonesia and was associated with 69% fewer cases in Niterói, Brazil. Demanding these methods is what a consistent animal advocate does.

**7. Those who eat meat escape none of this.** The restaurant serving chicken also has its kitchen treated. And animal farming has its own pest control: in poultry sheds, the lesser mealworm (*Alphitobius diaperinus*), a beetle that spreads through the birds' litter, is fought with insecticides and mechanical methods. Choosing meat doesn't reduce insect deaths in the slightest; it only adds the deaths of the animals raised.

**8. One failure doesn't justify another.** If some vegan or vegan restaurant used poison without need, that would be their failure, not a licence to raise and slaughter animals. It's the tu quoque fallacy (L11).

**9. Insects matter too.** There's a realistic possibility that insects are sentient (A3). So the answer shouldn't be "insects don't count", but "we can kill fewer": vegan restaurants can prefer companies that work with integrated management, sealing and monitoring, and the movement can demand the same of the authorities.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: nobody has to open a restaurant

The most serious version says that opening a restaurant is a choice. Whoever opens one knows they'll need to kill insects and rats, and opens it anyway, for profit. So the vegan restaurant owner accepts killing animals for an economic interest, the same kind of interest that drives animal farming.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **A vegan restaurant is good for society, and for animals.** Every dish served there replaces a meal that, in another restaurant, would have had meat, dairy or eggs. Vegan restaurants show that food without animal products is tasty and doable, welcome people who are starting to change, and help many people cut back. People will keep eating out; if there's no vegan restaurant, they'll eat where animals are served, and where pest control happens too. Not opening the restaurant wouldn't spare any cockroaches; it would only spare fewer animals.
2. **All food production faces pests.** Kitchens, markets, warehouses, vegetable gardens and farms all have to deal with them, and every society needs places that make food. What's at stake isn't whether there's any pest control, but how much harm it causes.
3. **The harm isn't the goal.** In a restaurant, a cockroach's death is a side effect that can be reduced with sealing and cleaning; if no cockroach shows up, all the better. In animal farming, the animal's death is the product itself.
4. **Profit isn't what weighs here.** Pest control doesn't exist to make a profit, but to protect the health of the people who eat there, a serious interest. In animal farming, the animal is killed to satisfy a taste that can be satisfied in other ways (J6).
5. **Consistency points to killing fewer.** If the criticism is that the restaurant could kill fewer, it's welcome: the way is to demand better methods, not to give up on caring about animals.

| Logical | False analogy | Equates legally required pest control with raising animals to eat |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Expects restaurants to follow food-safety law.** And accuses the vegan restaurant for following it.
2. **Eats at restaurants that spray.** And thinks that's only a problem for vegans.
3. **Pays the same taxes.** And blames only vegans for public spraying.
4. **Says insects matter.** Only when their deaths serve as an argument against veganism.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if you take part in something that kills animals, you lose the right to criticize any other way of killing animals".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hospitals | Anyone who uses a hospital that sprays for pests couldn't criticize cruelty to dogs | Nobody accepts this |
| Taxpayers | Anyone who pays tax couldn't criticize any public policy | Nobody accepts this |
| Meat-eaters | They couldn't criticize cockfighting or elephant hunting either, because their restaurant sprays | Nobody accepts this |
| Road safety | Anyone who drives and has hit an insect couldn't support speed limits | Shows the principle doesn't hold |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument points to something real: vegan restaurants, like all others, kill insects and rodents, and public spraying kills many animals, including ones who threaten no one. Animal advocates shouldn't pretend this doesn't count. It's also true that not all pest control follows the order the rule itself sets: there's routine spraying with no need.

But the consistent response is to demand prevention and methods that kill fewer, from the restaurant and from the council, not to conclude that it makes no difference. Pest control responds to a real conflict; raising animals to eat is a choice that can be avoided.
