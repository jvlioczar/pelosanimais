# C3. "We naturally crave meat"

**Thesis:** the craving for meat exists, but it is largely learned and reinforced: by culture, by childhood, by salt, fat and seasoning. And even if it were entirely natural, a desire isn't a justification. We have many strong cravings that we don't follow when they would harm others.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's my body asking for it. The smell of a barbecue is irresistible for a reason."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many people feel a strong, spontaneous craving to eat meat.
2. **P2.** Strong, spontaneous cravings point to the body's needs.
3. **P3.** Meeting the body's needs is legitimate.
4. **C.** So eating meat is legitimate.

P1 is true. The problem lies in P2, which treats desire as need, and in P3, which assumes that satisfying the body never conflicts with the interests of others.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes we really want to eat candy. That doesn't mean the body needs candy: it means candy tastes good and we're used to it. Meat is similar. And even when the craving is strong, we can think about whether it hurts someone before doing it.

## 4. Short answer

Wanting isn't needing. Much of the taste for meat is learned from childhood and reinforced by salt, fat and seasoning, which also make well-seasoned plant versions very tasty. And even if the desire were natural, desires don't justify causing harm: we have many strong cravings we don't follow when they would hurt someone.

**Question to ask back:** "Is there any other strong craving you don't follow because it would harm someone?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes three things:

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| I crave meat | True for many people |
| That craving is a bodily need | False: well-planned vegan diets are adequate |
| So I may satisfy it even if someone else suffers | Doesn't follow from anything above |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Desire isn't need.** The body needs nutrients, not specific foods. Protein, iron and other nutrients can be obtained without meat, according to the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

**2. Much of the taste is learned.** Food preferences form through repeated exposure, especially in childhood, and change over time. Many people who stop eating meat report that the craving fades after a few weeks or months.

**3. Much of the appeal is flavor, not the animal.** The smell of a barbecue comes largely from charred fat, salt, smoke and seasoning. Plant products prepared the same way aren't identical, but they trigger many of the same responses, which shows that much of the desire is for flavors and textures that don't depend on anyone's death.

**4. Desires don't justify harm.** We have many strong cravings we don't follow when they would harm someone. That isn't denying nature; it's having ethics.

**5. The argument leads to J5.** If the craving isn't a need, what's left is pleasure, and the eater's pleasure has to be weighed against what the animal loses.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27886704/) | Well-planned vegan diets are appropriate at all stages of life | Shows the craving doesn't correspond to a need |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: cravings are hard to overcome

The most serious version doesn't say the craving justifies anything, but that it's real and strong, and that asking people to ignore it is asking too much. Ethics has to be realistic about what people can do.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It shifts to difficulty, not justification.** Being hard doesn't make it right (E10).
2. **The difficulty usually fades.** With substitutes, seasoning and time, the craving tends to lose strength.
3. **Being realistic means helping people change, not excusing them from it.** A realistic ethics offers paths, not excuses.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | Takes desire as proof that something is good |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "the body asks for it" as if it meant "the body needs it" |
| Epistemological | Not knowing how taste forms | Treats a learned preference as a fixed instinct |
| Moral | Desire as justification | The eater's pleasure outweighs the life of the one eaten |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Controls other cravings.** No one follows every strong desire without thinking about the consequences.
2. **Has changed tastes before.** Almost every adult has come to like foods they rejected as a child.
3. **Would like the same seasoning on a plant food.** What attracts is the flavor, not the origin.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I feel a strong craving, satisfying it is legitimate".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anger | Attacking whoever annoys us would be legitimate | No one accepts this |
| Sexual desires | Any desire would justify acting, even without consent | No one accepts this |
| A craving to eat the neighbor's dog | It would be legitimate, if strong enough | The principle doesn't depend on species |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The craving for meat is real, and it's dishonest to pretend otherwise. For many people, meat is tied to memories, affection and pleasure, and changing takes effort. Recognizing that matters for helping people through the transition.

But recognizing the strength of a craving isn't accepting it as a justification. Taste can change, flavors can be recreated, and the animal's life can't.
