# H9. "Agriculture can't work without manure"

**Thesis:** agriculture works with green manure, composting, crop rotation with legumes, which fix nitrogen in the soil, and mineral fertilizers. Besides, manure comes from animals fed on crops: it returns to the soil part of the nutrients that were taken out to grow their feed. Without animals, far less land would be needed, and nutrients could go straight from plants back to the soil.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Without animals there is no fertilizer. Organic farming depends on cattle."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Plants need nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
2. **P2.** Animal manure is a traditional source of these nutrients, especially in organic farming.
3. **P3.** Without animals, there wouldn't be enough manure.
4. **C.** So agriculture depends on raising animals.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: there are other sources of nutrients, and manure doesn't create nutrients, it only returns part of what the animals ate.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it ignores the alternatives and where the nutrients in manure come from.

## 3. Answer for children

Plants need food to grow, which comes from the soil. Manure helps, but it isn't the only way. You can make fertilizer from plant scraps, leaves and peels, and grow beans and other plants that make the soil stronger. That way, plants grow well without needing animals.

## 4. Short answer

Agriculture has many ways to fertilize: green manure with legumes, which fix nitrogen from the air into the soil, composting plant waste, crop rotation and mineral fertilizers. Manure doesn't create nutrients: it returns to the soil part of what the animals ate, and those animals eat crops. Without livestock, far less land would be needed, and nutrients could go straight from plants back to the soil.

**Question to ask back:** "If manure comes from animals that eat crops, where do the nutrients in manure come from?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats manure as a source of nutrients, when it's a middleman:

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Feed crop | Takes nutrients out of the soil |
| The animal eats the feed | Uses part of the nutrients; the rest becomes manure |
| Manure returns to the soil | Returns part of what was taken out |

Without the animals, plant residues could go back to the soil directly.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Manure is a middleman.** It returns to the soil nutrients that came from the plants the animals ate, with losses along the way.

**2. There are well-known alternatives.** Green manure, composting, rotation with legumes, cover crops and mineral fertilizers are established farming practices.

**3. Legumes fix nitrogen.** Beans, soy, peas and other legumes host bacteria that capture nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil.

**4. Without livestock, far less land would be needed.** A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%. Less cropland means less need for fertilizer.

**5. Vegan farming exists.** There are growers who farm without animal inputs, sometimes called "veganic", and even a certification for it, the [Biocyclic Vegan standard](https://www.biocyclic-vegan.org/), used mainly in Europe.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: manure is a useful byproduct

The most serious version grants that there are alternatives, but says that as long as there are animals, using their manure is better than wasting it.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Using it doesn't justify producing it.** That manure is useful isn't a reason to raise animals.
2. **Excess manure is a problem.** In regions of intensive livestock farming, manure pollutes rivers and soils.
3. **The transition can be gradual.** Agriculture can reduce its dependence on manure as livestock farming declines.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Agriculture doesn't depend on raising animals to kill them. If there are ways to fertilize without them, the argument doesn't justify livestock farming, and the moral question returns to the center.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Eats vegetables grown with mineral fertilizers.** Most conventional farming uses mineral fertilizer.
2. **Ignores the cropland for feed.** Which also needs fertilizer.
3. **Doesn't demand manure for all the food they eat.** The criterion only comes up against vegans.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a byproduct is useful, the activity that generates it is necessary".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ash from burned forests | Burning forests would be necessary for fertilizer | No one concludes this |
| Heat from coal plants | The plants would be necessary for heating | No one concludes this |
| Manure | Livestock farming would be necessary for fertilizer | There are alternatives |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Manure is indeed a traditional and useful fertilizer, and integrating animals and crops has a long history. A transition to systems without animals requires knowledge and investment, and doesn't happen all at once.

But agriculture doesn't depend on animals. Plants can feed the soil directly, and without livestock, far less land would be needed to feed the world.
