# H9. "Cattle graze where crops can't grow"

**Thesis:** part of it is true: about two thirds of the world's pastures aren't suitable for crops. But that land produces very little food: livestock farming uses about 80% of farmland and provides only 17% of the world's calories. And land that can't be cropped could be rewilded, storing carbon and sheltering biodiversity. The question isn't whether the land suits crops, but whether we need it for food.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Cattle turn grass into food. That land is only good for cattle."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Much of the world's pasture isn't suitable for crops, because of terrain, climate or soil.
2. **P2.** Ruminants can turn grass, which we don't eat, into food.
3. **P3.** Without cattle, that land wouldn't produce food.
4. **C.** So raising cattle on that land is an efficient way to produce food.

P1 and P2 are true. So is P3, in the strict sense. The problem lies in the conclusion: the land doesn't need to produce food, and the amount of food it produces is small for the area it occupies.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it assumes all land must produce food, and ignores the alternative of rewilding.

## 3. Answer for children

In some places, like mountains and dry lands, you can't grow rice or beans. So some people raise cattle there. But those places can also become forests and grasslands with wild animals, which help the planet. And we can grow enough food on the land that's good for planting.

## 4. Short answer

It's true that about two thirds of pastures aren't suitable for crops. But that land produces very little food: livestock farming uses about 80% of the world's farmland and provides only 17% of calories. A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%, freeing up that land. It doesn't need to produce food: it can go back to being forests, savannas and native grasslands, which store carbon and shelter biodiversity.

**Question to ask back:** "If we don't need that land to feed ourselves, why does it have to produce meat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assumes the land must produce food:

| Use of non-arable land | Benefit |
| --- | --- |
| Cattle pasture | Little food per hectare; methane emissions; the animals' deaths |
| Rewilding | Carbon stored in vegetation and soil; biodiversity; water resources |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The land doesn't need to produce food.** With a plant diet, the world would need far less farmland. Non-arable land could be freed up.

**2. Food production is small for the area.** According to Our World in Data, livestock farming occupies about 80% of farmland and provides 17% of the world's calories and 38% of its protein.

**3. Rewilding is a valuable alternative.** Forests and native vegetation store far more carbon than pastures and shelter biodiversity. In Brazil and elsewhere, many pastures occupy land that was once forest or savanna.

**4. Many cattle don't eat only grass.** Much of livestock farming uses grain and soy feed, which competes with human food (H1).

**5. Efficiency is low.** Cattle convert about 2% of the energy they consume into meat. Even using grass, production per hectare is very low.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Our World in Data, land use and diets](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets) | About two thirds of pastures aren't suitable for crops; a global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%; cattle have an energy efficiency of about 2% | Based on Poore and Nemecek, 2018 |
| [Our World in Data, land for agriculture](https://ourworldindata.org/global-land-for-agriculture) | Livestock uses 80% of farmland and provides 17% of calories and 38% of protein | Global data |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: communities depend on herding

The most serious version points out that some communities, such as herders in arid regions, depend on livestock to live, and have no economic alternative.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That justifies exceptions.** Communities without alternatives are in a different situation from someone buying meat at the supermarket (D7).
2. **Those who use the argument don't live on that land.** Supermarket meat doesn't come from dryland herders.
3. **Transitions can be planned.** Public policy can support communities through change, as in other sectors.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if cattle make use of land with no other agricultural use, they are still raised to die. The argument talks about efficiency, but doesn't answer the moral question: if we don't need this meat to live, what justifies the animals' deaths?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Eats meat from feed-fed animals.** Which doesn't come from grass alone.
2. **Values forests and savannas.** Which could occupy that land.
3. **Doesn't live in regions that depend on herding.** The meat they buy comes from elsewhere.

## 9. Biases

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if land isn't suitable for crops, it should be used for cattle".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected areas | They should become pasture | No one openly defends this |
| Mountains and native forests | They should be converted | That's what causes deforestation |
| All non-arable land | It should produce meat | Ignores the value of nature |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument has a true part: many pastures aren't suitable for crops, and ruminants can make use of grass. In some regions and communities, herding plays a real economic and cultural role, and that should be respected in a transition.

But the land doesn't need to produce meat. With plant diets, the world would need far less land, and those areas could return to nature, benefiting the climate, biodiversity and the animals themselves.
