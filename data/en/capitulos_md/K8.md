# K8. "Radical veganism pushes people away"

**Thesis:** there may be some strategic truth here: certain tones and tactics put some people off. But that isn't a moral refutation, nor a personal excuse. That a messenger is unpleasant doesn't make the message false. Besides, radical isn't the same as extremist: a radical is someone who goes to the root of a problem and calls for deep change, while an extremist is, typically, someone who rejects pluralism and accepts violence. Radicalism has been part of other social movements, and research on the so-called radical flank effect shows that radical voices can either increase or decrease support for moderate ones. What matters, for the animals, is the overall effect of each strategy.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You scare people off. Why don't you just promote reduction? Toxic veganism."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Messages perceived as radical or judgmental provoke rejection.
2. **P2.** Rejection reduces people's willingness to change.
3. **P3.** A more moderate approach, such as encouraging reduction, would reach more people.
4. **C.** So radical veganism harms its own cause.

P1 and P2 have support in some contexts. P3 is a plausible but uncertain strategic hypothesis. The problem is that the conclusion, even if correct, is about strategy, not about whether it's right to eat animals. Besides, the argument tends to call both the goal (not using animals) and the tactics (confrontational protests) "radical", and research suggests the two have different effects.

The argument is **valid** as a strategic criticism. It fails when used as a justification for not changing.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes a person speaks in a way that makes us angry, even when they're right. But the way they speak doesn't change whether something is right or wrong. If someone rudely warns us that we're hurting someone, it's still worth stopping. And wanting a big change isn't the same as being violent: many people who changed the world were called radicals.

## 4. Short answer

It may be that some tones and tactics put some people off, and it's worth discussing the best way to communicate. But that doesn't change whether it's right to eat animals. An unpleasant messenger doesn't make the message false. Radical also isn't the same as extremist: a radical is someone who calls for change that goes to the root of the problem, which doesn't imply violence. Movements such as abolitionism and the fight for women's votes had radical wings, and research shows they can help or hurt, depending on the case. What matters is the overall effect for the animals. And the question for you remains: do animals deserve to be spared, regardless of who speaks for them?

**Question to ask back:** "If a vegan were kind to you, would the answer about the animals change?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes a strategic question with a moral one, and often also confuses two concepts:

| Question | Type | Does the argument answer it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What's the best way to persuade people? | Strategic | Partly |
| Is it right to eat animals? | Moral | No |
| Is being radical the same as being extremist? | Conceptual | It assumes so, but it isn't |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The form doesn't change the content.** That a message is delivered unpleasantly doesn't make it false.

**2. It isn't a personal excuse.** Other people's tone doesn't excuse anyone from examining the question for themselves.

**3. Radical isn't the same as extremist.** The word "radical" comes from the Latin *radix*, root: in political use, since the 19th century, it has described those who advocate a thorough reform that goes to the root of the problem. In the literature on radicalization, researchers such as Alex Schmid distinguish the two terms: radicals aren't violent by definition and have historically tended to be more open to debate and compromise, while extremists tend to reject pluralism, disregard the rule of law and favor the use of force. In this sense, advocating that animals not be used is a radical position, because it calls for fundamental change, but it isn't extremism. In practice, though, more confrontational strategies, such as protests that disrupt events, are often labeled radical, and that's usually what the argument is about (L5).

**4. "Radical" often just means consistent.** Saying animals shouldn't be killed without need is called radical, but it applies a principle almost everyone accepts.

**5. Radicalism has always been part of other social movements.** Abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, who from 1831 demanded the immediate, not gradual, emancipation of enslaved people, were seen as radicals. In the United Kingdom, the suffragettes of the Women's Social and Political Union adopted militant tactics, such as smashing windows and going on hunger strike, alongside the moderate suffragists. In the US civil rights movement, the sociologist Herbert Haines, who coined the term "radical flank effect", found in 1984 that the rise of more radical Black groups coincided with an increase, not a fall, in funding for moderate organizations. Historians still debate how much each wing contributed to the victories.

**6. The radical flank effect can be positive or negative.** In 2022, two experiments with about 2,770 participants, one of them on the animal movement, found that the presence of a group using radical tactics increased support for moderate groups in the same movement, who came to seem more reasonable by comparison; the effect came from the tactics, not from radical goals, which changed support little. A study with representative samples in the United Kingdom, published in 2024, found a similar effect after road blockades by Just Stop Oil, a climate group. But there are negative effects too: a 2020 study of Black protests in the United States estimated that nonviolent protests increased the Democratic vote in nearby counties, while violent ones shifted white voters toward Nixon in 1968. For animals, a Social Change Lab study of the Animal Rising protest at the Grand National, a British horse race, in 2023 found more donations to and sign-ups for the group, but also less agreement with criticisms of animal use among those who knew about the protest; six months later, these negative effects had largely faded. The evidence is limited and mixed.

**7. What matters is the overall effect for the animals.** If a form of radicalism tends to have negative effects for the animals, less radical or different strategies would be needed. Likewise, if it tends to have positive effects, it should be kept. The strategic question isn't settled by how uncomfortable a tactic makes people, but by what it produces overall, and the answers may vary by tactic, country and moment.

**8. Eating more meat out of irritation is a curious effect.** If irritation with vegans leads someone to harm animals more, the problem isn't with the animals.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: strategy matters for the animals

The most serious version says that if the goal is to help animals, the effectiveness of communication is a moral question, because it determines how many animals are spared. And there's evidence that some confrontational tactics provoke rejection.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's valid for activists.** Animal advocates should think about the best strategy and look at the research on what works, including when it shows a tactic is counterproductive.
2. **It doesn't apply to whoever receives the message.** For that person, the question is still about their own plate.
3. **Different strategies can coexist.** Moderate and firmer voices can complement each other, and the radical flank effect shows that the presence of some can change how the others are seen.
4. **The radical goal isn't the problem.** In the experiments, what changed support was the tactics, not the goal of ending animal use.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument is about vegans, not animals. But animals don't choose who speaks for them. The moral question stays the same, whoever the messenger: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts ideas defended by unpleasant people on other subjects.**
2. **Doesn't change opinion on other matters because of tone.**
3. **Says they'd support a moderate approach.** But doesn't change when they meet one.
4. **Admires movements that were called radical.** Such as abolitionism or the fight for women's votes.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a message is delivered radically, I needn't consider it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radical abolitionists | Slavery wouldn't need questioning | Absurd |
| Suffragettes | Women's votes wouldn't need to be taken seriously | No one accepts this today |
| Climate activists | Climate change wouldn't need to be taken seriously | No sensible person concludes this |
| Vegans | The question of animals wouldn't need considering | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The way we communicate matters, and some animal advocates do push people away with judgment, aggression or contempt. Some confrontational tactics can also provoke rejection, and research shows the radical flank effect isn't always positive. Discussing communication strategies is legitimate and necessary, and the movement should learn from criticism and from the evidence.

But criticism of the messenger doesn't answer the message. Whatever the tone of whoever speaks, the question about the animals is still waiting for an answer.
