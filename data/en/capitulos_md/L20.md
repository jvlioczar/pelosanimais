# L20. "Vegans defend animals, but they support abortion"

**Thesis:** veganism has no position on abortion: there are vegans on both sides, and the phrase generalizes. It's also a tu quoque: even if a vegan were inconsistent, that wouldn't justify exploiting animals. And the two cases have different structures. In abortion there is a conflict with a person's body, which doesn't exist in eating animals; and, by the criterion of sentience, the question about the fetus is when it begins to feel, a point on which science still disagrees for the second trimester. But there is one point of broad agreement: up to about 12 weeks, in the first trimester, the structures needed to feel pain are not yet formed, and even the researchers who argue that pain may be possible earlier don't place it before that. By that criterion, an abortion at that stage doesn't harm a being that feels. For slaughtered animals, that doubt doesn't exist. This chapter does not say which position on abortion is correct.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You cry over a chick and support killing babies. If life matters, why not the life of the fetus?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegans say we shouldn't kill beings who can suffer when it can be avoided.
2. **P2.** A human fetus is, or will be, a being capable of suffering.
3. **P3.** Many vegans support abortion rights.
4. **P4.** People who apply a principle only when it suits them don't deserve to be taken seriously.
5. **C.** So the vegan defense of animals can be dismissed.

P1 is true. P2 depends on the stage of pregnancy, and it's the point where science is consulted. P3 is true of many vegans and false of others. The central problem lies in P4 and the conclusion: the consistency of the speaker doesn't decide whether what they say about animals is true.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it generalizes in P3, treats two structurally different cases as the same, and draws a conclusion about animals from a flaw in those who defend them.

## 3. Answer for children

This is a grown-up conversation about a hard subject, and grown-ups think different things about it. Among people who don't eat animals, some think one way and some think another. One thing doesn't change: animals feel pain and fear, and that stays true whatever anyone thinks about other subjects.

## 4. Short answer

Veganism is about the use of animals and has no position on abortion. There are vegans for and against legal abortion, as in any group. And even if I were inconsistent, that wouldn't change what happens to animals. The two cases are also different: in abortion there is a conflict with a person's body, and science debates when the fetus begins to feel. Even within that debate, no one with serious evidence places pain before about 12 weeks: by the criterion of sentience, a first-trimester abortion doesn't affect someone who feels. With a pig or a chicken there is no comparable conflict, and no one seriously doubts that they feel. If what worries you about the fetus is its capacity to suffer, we share a reason.

**Question to ask back:** "What, for you, makes someone's life matter morally? Does that reason apply to a pig or a hen?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats as equal two cases that differ on decisive points:

| Point | Abortion | Eating animals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there a conflict with another person's body? | Yes: pregnancy takes place in the pregnant person's body | No |
| Does the being in question feel? | It depends on the stage of pregnancy: up to about 12 weeks, there is broad agreement that it doesn't; after that, science disagrees about the timing | Yes, beyond reasonable doubt, for mammals and birds |
| Does the vegan movement have a position? | No | Yes |

Recognizing these differences doesn't settle the abortion debate. It only shows that a person can answer the two questions differently without contradiction.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Veganism has no position on abortion.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding, as far as is possible and practicable, exploitation of and cruelty to animals. It says nothing about abortion. There are vegans for and against legal abortion. There are even books by animal advocates examining whether the two positions fit together, such as *Beating Hearts* (2016), by the American legal scholars Sherry Colb and Michael Dorf, which argues that they do; the book has drawn criticism, including from people who share its defense of animals. In the opposite direction, the philosopher Cheryl Abbate argued in 2015, in the journal *Ethical Theory and Moral Practice*, that a consistent animal rights ethic grounded in sentience, such as Gary Francione's, is committed to the fetus's right to life from the moment it can feel, and that the duty to help animals we have made dependent on us has a parallel in the case of a sentient fetus. The philosopher Nathan Nobis recommends both works to anyone who wants to study the link between the issues (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 106). The guide records the disagreement without taking a side on abortion.

**2. It's a tu quoque.** Pointing to an inconsistency in the speaker doesn't refute what was said. If a vegan were inconsistent, the conclusion would be that they should revise one of the two positions, not that animals may be exploited (L11).

**3. Abortion involves a conflict that doesn't exist on the plate.** Pregnancy takes place inside a person's body. One strand of philosophy holds that this weighs decisively, even if the fetus has a full right to life: that is the 1971 argument of the American philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson. Another strand disagrees, such as the American philosopher Don Marquis, for whom abortion deprives the fetus of a valuable future. The guide doesn't settle that dispute. What matters here is that eating animals involves no comparable conflict: no one needs a pig's body to sustain their own life or autonomy when there are alternatives.

**4. By the criterion of sentience, the question about the fetus is empirical, and science disagrees.** The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), the British professional body, concluded in 2010 that the fetus cannot feel pain before 24 weeks, for lack of connections to the cerebral cortex. In its December 2022 review, it concluded that pain perception before 28 weeks is "unlikely". Taking a different view, the psychologist Stuart Derbyshire and the clinician John Bockmann, who state that they hold divergent views on abortion, argued in 2020 in the *Journal of Medical Ethics* that neuroscience cannot definitively rule out pain before 24 weeks, and that an "immediate and unreflective" pain may be possible from as early as 12 weeks; they themselves note that some evidence points to a later stage. On mammals and birds, by contrast, the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) speaks of strong scientific support for conscious experience.

**5. In the first trimester, there is broad agreement that no one is there to suffer.** By the criterion of sentience, being harmed requires the capacity to feel; that criterion is an ethical thesis, not a scientific finding, but it's the same one that grounds the defense of animals. Science comes in to say when that capacity appears. Besides the RCOG, a systematic review published in 2005 in *JAMA* concluded that fetal perception of pain is unlikely before the third trimester, because the connections between the thalamus and the cortex, considered necessary for the conscious experience of pain, are not yet formed. The disagreements concentrate on the second trimester: the earliest threshold defended in the scientific literature, Derbyshire and Bockmann's, is about 12 weeks. Up to that point, in the first trimester, the positions converge: an abortion at that stage would cause no pain or other harm to a being that feels. That doesn't settle the abortion debate. Those who value the fetus for other reasons, such as its potential (A19), membership of the human species (A8) or faith (A17), won't be persuaded by this fact, and positions on abortion still vary among vegans, as in any group.

**6. The reverse version doesn't settle things either.** "If you're against abortion, you should be vegan" has the same form: it demands consistency. It has force for those who oppose abortion because of the fetus's capacity to suffer, since that reason applies to animals too. But many people oppose it for other reasons, such as the fetus's potential (A19), membership of the human species (A8) or faith (A17), and those reasons need to be discussed one by one. As an attack on the person, the reverse version is as weak as the original.

**7. The best-known inverse version is Peter Singer's, and it should be read with caution.** In *Practical Ethics* (ch. 6), Singer notes that the conservative argument against abortion uses "human" in two senses: member of the species *Homo sapiens* and person. Comparing capacities such as consciousness, pleasure and suffering, he judges that the cow, the pig and the chicken are ahead of the fetus at any stage of pregnancy, and that, before three months, a fish shows more signs of consciousness. From this he concludes that those who protest against abortion but eat chicken, pork or beef show a partial concern for their own species. He also asks that, in late abortion, the method receive the same care that is asked of slaughter, so as not to cause suffering to a fetus who may feel it. This guide records the comparison, but doesn't import the conclusions Singer draws from it about late abortion and about newborns, which have been strongly contested, including by disabled people (A27). And Singer's data on fetal pain date from 1993; for the current state of research, see point 4.

**8. The attack commits the fallacy of denying the antecedent.** A common form goes: "you think that, if a being is conscious, it has rights; the fetus early in pregnancy isn't conscious; so you think it has no rights". This is the invalid form known as [denying the antecedent](../falacias.html#denying-the-antecedent): from "if A, then B" and "not A" you can't conclude "not B". The philosopher Nathan Nobis shows the flaw with a case of the same form: if Eve attends the state college, she is a college student; she doesn't attend it; so she isn't a college student. The premises can be true and the conclusion false, because Eve may attend a private college (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, pp. 97–98). The sentience criterion says that feeling is enough to count, not that it's the only thing that can count. So the arguments for animals are neutral about the fetus early in pregnancy: they show neither that it has rights nor that it lacks them. This is consistent with point 5: saying that an abortion at that stage doesn't affect a being who feels isn't the same as saying no other reason weighs against it. To reach any position on abortion, other arguments are needed.

**9. "It's alive, so it's wrong to kill" fails on both topics.** Sometimes people try to link the two issues through life: the fetus is a living being, and so are animals. But the premise that it's wrong to kill any living being would condemn pulling a weed, harvesting a carrot or cleaning the mould in the bathroom, and almost no one accepts it (Nobis, 2016, pp. 86–87). So it supports neither opposition to abortion nor the case for animals. The case for animals rests on the capacity to feel, not on being alive (A4).

**10. Negative rights and positive rights.** Respecting animals mostly requires leaving them alone: not confining them, not hurting them, not killing them. These are negative rights. A right of the fetus to gestation would be, largely, a positive right: it would require the continuous help of another person's body for months (Nobis, 2016, p. 100). It's the difference in point 3 put in other words, and it shows why the two topics aren't settled by the same principle. In the case of abortion, the distinction is contested: critics of Thomson, such as the philosopher John Finnis (1973), reply that abortion, by most methods, is killing and not merely declining to help, and that parents have special duties towards their children. Nobis draws pro-choice conclusions from the distinction, which this guide doesn't adopt. What it shows, whatever one's position on abortion, is that the duty not to exploit animals doesn't depend on the answer to that other question.

**11. The case for reproductive control, taken seriously, reaches exploited females.** One defence of the right to abortion says that pregnancy, giving birth and raising children are profound experiences, and that the decision to live them should be under the woman's control. Nobis notes that anyone who accepts this reason has grounds to look at the females used in farming and in some research: dairy cows are artificially inseminated and separated from their calves, and laying hens don't brood their eggs or raise their chicks (Nobis, 2016, pp. 103–104; see B5). There's no need to equate the experience of a woman with that of a cow to see the point: if control over one's own reproduction matters for those who can feel it, it matters too for females of other species who feel. The point is conditional: it holds for those who already accept this defence of abortion, and doesn't decide abortion for those who reject it.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: those who give fish the benefit of the doubt should give it to the fetus

The most serious version says: vegans ask for caution when there is doubt about the sentience of fish and insects (A2). If some scientists allow for fetal pain from 12 weeks, the same caution should apply to the fetus. Not applying the principle here would be a double standard.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a good argument within the abortion debate.** A vegan who uses the precautionary principle has to take that question seriously, and many do. Even so, the neuroscientific evidence doesn't reach the first trimester: not even the authors who allow for earlier pain place it before about 12 weeks. Some take precaution further: Cheryl Abbate proposes giving the fetus the benefit of the doubt from around 8 weeks, as is done for some invertebrates (Nobis, 2016, p. 92). That's a philosophical choice about how much caution to apply, not new data. And that's different from showing that the defense of animals is wrong.
2. **Caution weighs differently when another body is involved.** Sparing a fish out of caution costs nothing to anyone's body. In pregnancy, caution has a cost for a person, and it's that conflict that divides philosophers and societies. The guide doesn't say how it should be resolved.
3. **The counter-reply strengthens the criterion.** It only works if the capacity to suffer matters. Whoever uses it has already accepted the premise they need in order to look at the pig, the cow and the chicken.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Abortion is one of the hardest topics in ethics, and serious people disagree about it. None of that changes what happens to animals. They feel, they are killed by the billions, and in their case there is no conflict of bodies that makes the decision tragic. Whatever your position on abortion, the question remains: what justifies making an animal suffer and die when there is an alternative?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Demands consistency from vegans.** But if a life that feels matters, that applies to the person demanding it too.
2. **Says the fetus's capacity to suffer matters.** And doesn't consider it for animals who undoubtedly suffer.
3. **Treats vegans as a bloc.** And wouldn't accept all meat eaters being treated that way.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone who defends a cause seems inconsistent on another issue, the cause can be dismissed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People against abortion and in favor of the death penalty | Opposition to abortion could be dismissed | Those who use L20 wouldn't accept that |
| People who defend children and don't donate to fight hunger | The defense of children could be ignored | Absurd |
| Vegans, whatever their position on abortion | The question of animals could be ignored | The same mistake |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right to demand consistency: anyone who says the capacity to suffer is what matters has to apply that criterion to every case, including the fetus at the stages when pain is possible, and can't treat the scientific doubt as if it didn't exist. It's also right that some vegans talk about abortion carelessly, or use the reverse version to attack religious people.

But that consistency is a task for each person, not an objection against animals. Concern for a being who may feel and concern for beings who certainly feel come from the same place. Whoever takes the first seriously has one more reason, not one fewer, to take the second seriously.
