# I1. "Animal testing has saved millions of lives"

**Thesis:** benefit doesn't justify using those who can't consent. Testing on children or on people unable to consent would also bring benefits, and no one accepts it. Besides, about 90% of drugs that pass preclinical testing fail in human trials, partly because animals aren't good models of human disease. In 2022 the United States stopped requiring animal testing for new drugs, opening the way for alternative methods.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Would you rather test on children? Without rats there'd be no vaccines. Medicine needs it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Research on animals has contributed to many medical advances, such as vaccines and treatments.
2. **P2.** Those advances have saved millions of human lives.
3. **P3.** Saving human lives justifies causing animals some suffering.
4. **C.** So animal testing is justified.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: benefit to some doesn't, by itself, justify using others without consent, and the usefulness of the tests is smaller than people tend to think.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, and P1 is weaker than it looks.

## 3. Answer for children

Scientists have used many animals to test medicines. Some tests helped discover important things. But the animals didn't choose to take part, and many suffered. Today there are new ways to test medicines without using animals, such as cells in a lab and computers, and lots of people are working to have them replace animal tests.

## 4. Short answer

That testing brought benefits isn't enough: testing on people who can't consent would too, and no one accepts it. Besides, about 90% of drugs that pass preclinical testing fail in humans, partly because animals don't reproduce human diseases well. In 2022, the United States stopped requiring animal tests for new drugs, allowing alternative methods.

**Question to ask back:** "If benefit justifies using those who don't consent, why don't we test on humans who can't consent?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument depends on two ideas:

| Idea | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Animal tests have brought benefits | Partly true |
| Benefit justifies using those who don't consent | Accepted in no other case |
| Animal tests are indispensable today | Disputed: high failure rate and growing alternative methods |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Benefit doesn't justify using those who don't consent.** Testing on humans without consent would also bring benefits, and it was banned after historical abuses. The criterion that protects vulnerable humans should apply to other sentient beings.

**2. The failure rate is high.** According to a 2022 analysis, about 90% of drug candidates that enter clinical trials fail. Among the reasons cited is the biological difference between animal models and humans. The figure doesn't prove animal tests are useless, since failures have many causes; it shows they are far from guaranteeing safety and efficacy, which weakens the idea that they're indispensable.

**3. There are alternative methods.** Organoids, organs-on-chips, human cell cultures and computer models are being developed and validated.

**4. The law is changing.** The FDA Modernization Act 2.0, of 2022, removed the requirement for animal testing of new drugs in the United States, allowing non-animal methods. In the European Union, the directive on animals used in science (2010/63/EU) already sets full replacement as its ultimate goal.

**5. The argument says nothing about eating animals.** Even if testing were justified, that wouldn't justify meat, milk and eggs.

**6. Even under utilitarianism, the burden lies with the experimenter.** Peter Singer doesn't rule out every experiment in principle: if a few animals suffered to save thousands, his theory could accept it. But, as the political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane summarizes, animal pain counts as much as human pain, and it's up to the experimenter to show the experiment will relieve more suffering than it causes, which already rules out speculative research with little chance of a useful result. Singer adds a test: anyone who wouldn't use orphaned humans with severe and irreversible brain damage, but uses animals with similar capacities, is deciding by species, not by capacities (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 34–35; see A8). Rights theories, such as Tom Regan's or Cochrane's own, go further and reject sacrificing one individual for the benefit of others (pp. 45, 143).

**7. The experimenter's dilemma, and what has changed since 1993.** Peter Singer pointed out in *Practical Ethics* a dilemma for those who defend experiments: if an experiment tells us something about humans, it's because the animal resembles us in crucial respects, including the capacity to feel stress and pain (ch. 3). Denying the resemblance to ease one's conscience undermines the experiment's own justification. Singer also recalled that many experiments had no vital medical purpose, citing the LD50 lethal-dose test, eye-irritation tests on rabbits (Draize), irradiated monkeys forced to run on a wheel at a US military institute and Harry Harlow's maternal-deprivation experiments. Some of this has changed: the OECD removed the classic LD50 test from its guidelines, effective December 2002, replacing it with methods that use fewer animals (guidelines 420, 423 and 425), and the European Union banned animal testing for cosmetics in stages, from 2004 to 2013 (I3). So have the numbers: Singer cited official figures of about 140,000 dogs and 42,000 cats killed each year in US laboratories (ch. 5); the USDA report for fiscal year 2023 records about 47,000 dogs and 14,000 cats used or held in research facilities. The two counts don't measure exactly the same thing, and USDA reports exclude mice and rats bred for research, birds and fish, which the US Animal Welfare Act doesn't cover and which make up the vast majority of animals used.

**8. "Testing benefits humans" is ambiguous about quantity.** The sentence may mean that some tests have benefited some people, which is true and which almost no critic denies, or that every test benefits humans, which is false: many experiments are done with no expectation of any medical benefit. The philosopher Nathan Nobis notes that defenders of testing tend to prove the modest version and conclude the ambitious one, that every experiment is permitted, including those not expected to bring any benefit (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, pp. 61–62). And even the modest version isn't enough: someone might benefit from a vital organ taken from a healthy person, and that benefit doesn't license taking it (pp. 62–63).

**9. "Necessary" and "no alternative" change meaning halfway through.** In a trivial sense, every experiment is "necessary" for doing that experiment. In the sense of "there's no other way to get this result", some experiments on non-consenting humans would also be "necessary", and no one concludes from this that they're permitted (Nobis, 2016, pp. 63–64). Nobis gives an example: to rob a bank, a getaway car may be the only means, and that doesn't make using it acceptable. Being the only means to an end doesn't justify the means; what decides is whether the end, and the cost imposed to reach it, are justified (p. 110). The same equivocation appears in I4 and G1.

**10. By the standards of research ethics, animal testing wouldn't pass.** The rules for research on human beings, such as the Belmont Report (United States, 1979) and Brazil's National Health Council Resolution No. 466/2012, require respect for autonomy, with free and informed consent, that harm to participants be avoided or minimized, and that the burdens and benefits of research be distributed fairly. Nobis applies these criteria to the animal: the test harms them, brings them no benefit, is imposed without consent and makes them carry a problem that isn't theirs (p. 109). To avoid the conclusion, defenders of testing need to point to a difference between the animal and the human that justifies treating them in opposite ways, which is what A8 and A22 attempt.

**11. "What if it were your child?"** A common way to close the discussion is to ask: if your child were dying and an animal test could save them, wouldn't you accept it? Many people would, and the wish is understandable. But what a desperate person would accept doesn't show what is right. Nathan Nobis answers with a parallel case: someone who, to save their own child, experimented on the neighbour's children would act wrongly, because those children would be harmed and used as mere tools for someone else's benefit; and those reasons apply to the animals used in tests too (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 110). He adds that animal testing would probably help that child little and that other methods tend to be more promising, an empirical claim that should be read in the light of points 2 and 3. On tragic choices about whom to save, see F3.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: there aren't alternatives for everything yet

The most serious version grants the problems, but says there aren't yet alternatives for every type of test, and that abandoning testing all at once would delay life-saving treatments.

The best-known academic formulation is Carl Cohen's, in a 1986 article in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. Cohen grants that humane treatment requires dropping an experiment when an alternative method, such as cell culture or computer simulation, gives the same result. But he argues that no method in sight, and perhaps none ever, will fully replace testing a drug, a procedure or a vaccine in living organisms: every advance will have to be tried for the first time on someone, and if not on an animal, then on a human being. So, he concludes, animal use shouldn't decrease but increase, to take risk away from human volunteers. For Cohen, animals have no rights, because they aren't part of a community of moral agents (A22), and anyone counting the suffering of the animals used must put on the scales the human suffering that research has prevented and will prevent.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That justifies investing in alternatives.** If tests are still used, the priority should be replacing them.
2. **It doesn't justify unnecessary tests.** Many tests are done because of bureaucratic requirements, not scientific necessity.
3. **The dilemma doesn't apply to food.** Eating animals doesn't save human lives.
4. **Cohen's dilemma has a third way, and it leaned on a law that has changed.** The first test in a whole human organism happens anyway, in clinical trials; the question is how much prior animal testing reduces the volunteers' risk, and the high failure rate (point 2 of (b)) shows it reduces it less than was assumed. Methods using human cells, organs-on-chips and computer models compete for that role (point 3). And Cohen relied on the US legal requirement to test on animals before humans, which ended in 2022 (point 4).
5. **Cohen's conclusion depends on the thesis that animals have no rights.** That thesis also requires excluding humans who aren't moral agents, and Cohen only includes them by saying that what counts is the kind, not the individual (A18). Besides, even if animals had no rights, it wouldn't follow that they may be used: harm can be wrong for other reasons (A22).
6. **One defender of testing recognized that consistency comes at a high price.** The philosopher R. G. Frey, who defended animal experimentation, argued that whoever justifies painful animal experiments by their benefits must explain why humans with a very low quality of life, sometimes lower than that of some animals, would be protected from them. Without that explanation, two ways out remain: accept some such experiments on humans, or give up painful experimentation on animals (Frey, 1983; restated in Frey, 2001). Frey said he could see no secure, non-arbitrary way of separating the two cases and reached, with reluctance, the first way out, which almost no one accepts (Nelson, 1988). Whoever rejects it is left with the second, or must find the difference Frey couldn't find.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Testing is one of the hardest cases, because it involves human lives. But even here the moral question doesn't disappear: is it acceptable to use those who don't consent for the benefit of others? For food, where no human lives are at stake, the answer is even clearer.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either animal testing or testing on children |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | From "some tests have benefited some people" it concludes that every test is permitted |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Necessary" slides from the trivial sense to "the only means", and "the only means" is treated as "justified" |
| Epistemological | Overestimating effectiveness | Ignores the high translational failure rate |
| Moral | Selective consequentialism | Applies the cost-benefit calculation only to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept testing on humans without consent.** Even if it brought benefits.
2. **Uses the argument to justify meat.** Which doesn't save lives.
3. **Supports alternatives in other areas.** Such as cosmetics not tested on animals.
4. **Rejects the way out that consistency requires.** As R. G. Frey admitted, whoever justifies painful animal experiments by their benefits would have to accept the same experiments on some humans with a very low quality of life, or give up painful experimentation on animals. Almost no one accepts the first option, and few take the second.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if using someone benefits many, it's justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testing on people without consent | It would be justified | Banned after historical abuses |
| Forced organ removal | It would be justified to save several lives | No one accepts this |
| Pets | They could be used in tests | Many people object |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Animal testing has contributed to real medical advances, and many people are alive because of treatments developed with it. It's also true that there aren't yet alternatives for every type of test, and that the transition requires investment and care.

But the benefit doesn't erase the cost to those who didn't consent. Science is developing alternatives, and the law is beginning to follow. And above all, none of this justifies eating animals.
