# G7. "Only fish provides omega-3"

**Thesis:** fish produce little long-chain omega-3 (EPA and DHA): most of it comes from the algae at the base of the marine food chain, which fish eat directly or indirectly. Algal oil supplements provide the same DHA at the source, without the fish. And short-chain omega-3 (ALA) is found in flaxseed, chia and walnuts.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You have to eat fish twice a week. Flaxseed doesn't give you DHA."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Long-chain omega-3 (EPA and DHA) is important for the heart, brain and eyes.
2. **P2.** Common plants provide only ALA, which the body converts to EPA and DHA in small proportions.
3. **P3.** Oily fish are the main dietary source of EPA and DHA.
4. **C.** So you have to eat fish to get adequate omega-3.

P1, P2 and P3 are true. The problem lies in the conclusion: the EPA and DHA in fish come from algae, which can be consumed directly as a supplement.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it ignores the original source of omega-3.

## 3. Answer for children

Omega-3 is a fat that's good for the brain. Fish have omega-3 because they eat algae, tiny sea plants that make this fat. We can get omega-3 straight from algae, without needing the fish. And there's omega-3 in flaxseed, chia and walnuts too.

## 4. Short answer

Fish make little omega-3: most of it comes from algae, directly or indirectly. Algal oil supplements provide the same DHA, straight from the source. Flaxseed, chia and walnuts provide ALA, another type of omega-3. Fish are middlemen, and they bring risks such as mercury and other contaminants with them. The need is for omega-3, not for fish.

**Question to ask back:** "If the omega-3 in fish comes from algae, why not go straight to the source?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument ignores where omega-3 comes from:

| Type of omega-3 | Who produces it | Where to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ALA (short chain) | Plants | Flaxseed, chia, walnuts, canola oil |
| EPA and DHA (long chain) | Marine algae | Algae, algal oil supplements; fish, who accumulate them |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Algae are the source.** The EPA and DHA in fish come from the microalgae at the base of the marine food chain. Farmed fish are often given fish oil or algal oil in their feed.

**2. Algal oil provides DHA directly.** Algal oil supplements are a vegan source of EPA and DHA, recognized by the US National Institutes of Health.

**3. ALA is in common plants.** Flaxseed, chia and walnuts provide ALA. Conversion to DHA is limited, which is why many dietitians recommend an algal supplement on vegan diets.

**4. Fish carry contaminants.** Fish can accumulate mercury and other pollutants. Microalgae grown in tanks for supplements are kept away from that contamination.

**5. The recommendation is for omega-3, not fish.** Advice to eat fish exists because it's a common source, not because it's the only one.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [NIH, omega-3, fact sheet for health professionals](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/) | EPA and DHA come from fish and algal oil; ALA comes from flaxseed, chia, walnuts and plant oils | Official fact sheet of the US National Institutes of Health |
| [Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27886704/) | Well-planned vegan diets are adequate; mentions microalgae as a source of DHA | Official position |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: supplements aren't the same as food

The most serious version grants that algal oil provides DHA, but says eating fish also brings other nutrients, and that there are more studies on fish than on algal supplements.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The other nutrients in fish are in other sources.** Protein, iodine and vitamin D can be obtained in vegan ways.
2. **DHA is the same molecule.** The body doesn't distinguish its origin.
3. **Fishing has enormous costs.** An estimated 0.8 to 2.3 trillion fish are caught each year, and fishing damages the oceans (H14).
4. **Trials of supplements have mixed results.** That applies to fish oil as much as to algal oil; the argument doesn't favor fish.

### (f) Back to the moral question

If omega-3 can be obtained straight from algae, the need to fish disappears. And fish, according to current science, are sentient beings (A2). The question becomes moral again: what justifies killing fish to obtain something algae provide directly?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts supplements in other cases.** Many people take fish oil capsules, which are also supplements.
2. **Doesn't eat fish twice a week.** Most people who use the argument don't follow the recommendation.
3. **Worries about mercury.** And mercury is found precisely in fish.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an animal accumulates a nutrient, we need to eat that animal".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin B12 | We'd need meat, even though B12 comes from bacteria | This is the argument of G4 |
| Calcium | We'd need milk, even though calcium comes from the soil | This is the argument of G6 |
| Omega-3 | We'd need fish, even though it comes from algae | The pattern repeats |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Long-chain omega-3 is important, and it's true that conversion from common plant sources is limited. Vegans should consider a source of DHA, such as algal oil, and it's good to say so clearly.

But fish are middlemen. The source is algae, and it's available directly, without fishing and without contaminants.
