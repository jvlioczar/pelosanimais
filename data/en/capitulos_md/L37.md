# L37. "I hate vegans"

**Thesis:** hatred isn't an argument. Psychology helps explain where it usually comes from: people who don't eat meat go against a majority habit and are felt as a threat to values, traditions and identities, and meat eaters tend to put down vegetarians especially when they imagine being judged by them. It's an understandable defensive reaction, but it turns against people who, at bottom, are asking for less suffering for animals. And whatever anyone feels about vegans, the animals' suffering and death remain the issue.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I hate vegans. I can't stand people who don't eat meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegans, through what they say and do, morally condemn meat eaters and threaten habits, traditions and identities that many people value.
2. **P2.** It's natural to feel aversion toward those who condemn us and threaten what we value.
3. **P3.** If aversion toward a group is natural, there's no need to take what that group stands for seriously.
4. **C.** So I can reject vegans and what they stand for.

P1 has a true part: some vegans condemn others explicitly, and veganism does challenge a majority habit. P2 describes a real psychological reaction. The problem lies in P3: an emotional reaction says something about whoever feels it, but nothing about whether the rejected idea is true.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which swaps the assessment of the idea for a feeling about the people, and partly at P1, because much of the condemnation is anticipated rather than spoken.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine a classmate asks everyone to stop pulling the school dog's tail, because it hurts him. Someone might find the classmate annoying, or get angry at being corrected. But being angry at the classmate doesn't make the dog's pain go away. The question is still: is it right to hurt the dog?

## 4. Short answer

I understand that many people feel judged, and some vegans really do speak aggressively. But psychology shows that much of this anger comes from feeling threatened: in a 2012 study, meat eaters rated vegetarians worse just after imagining how vegetarians would judge them. Hating people who ask for less suffering for animals doesn't answer what they're asking. You may not like me, and the animals keep suffering just the same.

**Question to ask back:** "If no vegans existed, would what happens to the animals become right?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The sentence offers no reason; it expresses a feeling about a group. But it's possible to reconstruct what it usually defends:

| What the sentence says | What it usually defends |
| --- | --- |
| "I hate vegans" | A habit and an identity that seem to be questioned |
| "They think they're better" | One's own moral self-image, which seems threatened |
| "People who don't eat meat" | The majority norm, which the group goes against |
| "The worst kind of people" | A generalization that spares one from listening to any particular vegan |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Hatred isn't an argument.** Feeling aversion toward a group says something about whoever feels it, but doesn't show the group is wrong. The question about the animals remains open.

**2. Much of the anger is a defense against anticipated judgment.** Minson and Monin showed in 2012 that meat eaters tend to put down vegetarians, and that simply asking people to imagine how vegetarians would judge them makes their ratings of vegetarians worse. In the first study, participants saw a small difference between the morality of meat eaters and that of vegetarians, but expected vegetarians to see it as almost ten times larger. The authors called this do-gooder derogation.

**3. Vegans are felt as a threat to the status quo.** MacInnis and Hodson proposed that people who abstain from meat go against the majority's values and are felt as a symbolic threat. In their study, omnivores rated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as or more negatively than several groups that are common targets of prejudice; only drug addicts were rated worse. Vegans were rated worse than vegetarians, and those who abstain for the animals or the environment were rated worse than those who do so for health.

**4. Meat is also identity.** Dhont and Hodson measured this threat with statements such as that eating meat is part of our cultural habits and identity, and showed that it helps explain why people with more right-wing ideologies are more accepting of animal exploitation. Rothgerber showed that, for many men, eating meat is tied to feeling masculine (L6). When a habit is part of who someone is, criticism of the habit is felt as criticism of the person.

**5. The target of the hatred is whoever asks for less suffering.** What veganism asks, in essence, is that animals not be made to suffer and die without need. Hating those who ask this doesn't answer the request; it turns a question about animals into a dispute about identity.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Minson and Monin, 2012, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) | 47% of participants associated at least one negative term with vegetarians; imagining being morally judged by them made ratings worse | Preprint of the article; samples of US undergraduates |
| [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017, *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) | Omnivores rated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as or more negatively than several prejudice target groups; the bias was stronger among those with more right-wing ideologies, explained by perceived threat | The studies measure attitudes, not hatred; in the first study, participants weren't less willing to hire or rent to vegans |
| [Dhont and Hodson, 2014, *Personality and Individual Differences*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2014.02.002) | Perceived threat from vegetarianism and belief in human supremacy help explain greater acceptance of animal exploitation and higher meat consumption | Created the vegetarianism threat scale |
| [Rothgerber, 2013, *Psychology of Men & Masculinity*](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030379) | Men use more direct justifications for eating meat, and this use is related to masculinity | Meat as part of identity |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: vegans provoke the rejection they get

The most serious version says: hatred doesn't come from nowhere. Some vegans insult people, compare meat eaters to criminals and turn every meal into a sermon. People treated that way react, and the blame for the hostility lies, at least in part, with those who provoked it.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It explains part of the reaction, but not all of it.** The studies show hostility appears even without any insult: imagining the judgment is enough. In Minson and Monin's study, participants were given no new information about vegetarians.
2. **It doesn't extend to everyone.** That some vegans insult people doesn't justify hating all of them, not even those who never said anything (L1).
3. **It doesn't change the question.** Even if every vegan were unpleasant, what happens to the animals would stay the same. The form of the message can be criticized; the content needs an answer.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Liking vegans or not is a matter of feeling. The question they ask is a different one: is it just to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? It doesn't depend on who asks it, or on how we feel about that person. Whatever one feels about vegans, the animals remain the issue.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L37 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Ad hominem and poisoning the well | Rejects the idea because of who defends it |
| Logical | Appeal to emotion | Uses a feeling instead of a reason |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Judges all vegans by the worst examples |
| Moral | Changing the question (red herring) | The feeling about vegans replaces the question about animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accuses vegans of intolerance.** But declares hatred of a whole group over a food choice.
2. **Says vegans want to impose.** But it's the hatred that ends the conversation.
3. **Doesn't hate people who defend dogs and cats.** Whoever reports cruelty to a dog is usually admired, though they ask for something similar.
4. **Usually likes animals.** And hates precisely those who ask that animals not suffer.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L37 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do-gooder derogation | Discrediting the vegan eases the moral judgment one imagines receiving | [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) |
| Symbolic threat | The vegan is felt as a threat to the majority's values and traditions | [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253); [Dhont and Hodson, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2014.02.002) |
| Identity-protective cognition | Criticism of the habit is felt as criticism of the person | [Rothgerber, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030379) |
| Status quo bias | The current habit seems right just for being current | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564) |
| Messenger effect | The message is judged by dislike of whoever delivers it | [Questão de Ética, vol. X, ch. 6](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v10/06-some-biases-that-can-influence-the-choice-of-strategies.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I feel aversion toward those who defend an idea, I don't need to consider the idea".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abolitionists | Those who detested them could ignore slavery | They were widely vilified in their time, and they were right |
| People who report cruelty to dogs | Disliking the reporter would be enough to ignore the dog | No one accepts this |
| Any cause | Every moral question could be dismissed out of dislike | Morality would become a matter of personal taste |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that the feeling of being judged is real and uncomfortable, and that some vegans communicate aggressively, which makes dialogue harder. The movement should listen to this criticism, and those who defend animals gain by speaking respectfully. It's also right, unintentionally, in showing how closely food is tied to identity: that's why the conversation is hard.

But hatred doesn't answer the question; it only turns it into a dispute about who we are. And while people argue over whether vegans are bearable, the animals keep suffering and dying.
