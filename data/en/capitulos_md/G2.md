# G2. "We need animal protein"

**Thesis:** we need protein, not animal protein. Legumes such as beans, lentils, chickpeas and soy, along with grains, nuts and seeds, provide all the protein and all the essential amino acids an adult needs, as long as the diet is varied and supplies enough energy. Nutrition bodies in several countries, such as the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the British Dietetic Association and Dietitians of Canada, recognize that well-planned plant-based diets can be adequate at all stages of life; others, such as the German Nutrition Society, don't yet take a position for pregnant women, children and older people, and call for professional supervision.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Where do you get your protein? Without meat you'll be weak. You need quality protein."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Human beings need protein, with all the essential amino acids.
2. **P2.** Animal foods are concentrated, complete sources of protein.
3. **P3.** It's hard to get enough complete protein from plants alone.
4. **C.** So we need to eat animal products.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: the evidence shows that varied plant diets provide enough complete protein for adults.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Protein helps the body grow and get strong. And it isn't only in meat! Beans, lentils, chickpeas, peanuts and soy have lots of protein. Rice and beans, for example, is a great combination. And lots of people, even athletes, don't eat meat and get all the protein they need.

## 4. Short answer

We need protein, not animal protein. Beans, lentils, chickpeas, soy, tofu, nuts and grains provide all the protein and all the amino acids we need, as long as we eat a varied diet over the day and in sufficient quantity. There's no need to combine foods in the same meal. The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the British Dietetic Association and Dietitians of Canada recognize that well-planned plant-based diets can be adequate at all stages of life. Rice and beans, a staple from Latin America to the Caribbean and West Africa, is already an excellent source of protein.

**Question to ask back:** "If beans, lentils and soy provide all the protein we need, is the need for protein or for meat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses the nutrient with the source:

| What the body needs | Where it can come from |
| --- | --- |
| Protein | Meat, eggs, milk, but also beans, lentils, chickpeas, soy, tofu, nuts, grains |
| Essential amino acids | Plant foods contain all nine essential amino acids, but in different proportions: cereals are low in lysine, which legumes make up for. Variety over the day solves it |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Plant protein is enough.** A 2019 review concluded that protein-rich foods such as legumes, nuts and seeds are enough to ensure protein adequacy for adults on vegetarian and vegan diets.

**2. The amino acids are in plants.** The idea that foods must be combined in the same meal is an old myth. Eating a varied diet over the day provides all the essential amino acids (G3).

**3. Nutrition bodies recognize adequacy.** The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics states that well-planned vegan diets are healthy, nutritionally adequate and appropriate for all stages of life, including pregnancy, childhood and for athletes. The British Dietetic Association says that carefully planned plant-based diets can support healthy living at every age and life stage, and notes that vitamin B12 has to come from fortified foods or a supplement. In Canada, a joint position of Dietitians of Canada with the American Dietetic Association reached the same conclusion about all stages of the life cycle. It isn't unanimous: the German Nutrition Society considers a vegan diet adequate for healthy adults but, for lack of data, neither recommends nor advises against it for pregnant women, children and older people, and calls for professional supervision. None of these bodies says animal protein is necessary.

**4. Traditional plates are already rich in protein.** Rice and beans, dal and rice, hummus and bread: traditional combinations around the world provide good-quality protein.

**5. Animal protein comes from plants.** Chickens and pigs get their protein from soy and corn feed; cattle, from pasture and grain, with the help of rumen microorganisms that the human digestive system lacks. So it doesn't work to say "if the ox gets strong eating grass, so would we": the digestion is different. The point is another: eating the animal means getting, by a longer route and with more losses, nutrients that came from plants, and humans get enough protein by eating legumes, grains and seeds directly.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some groups need more attention

The most serious version grants that healthy adults get enough protein from plants, but points out that older people, children and athletes have greater needs, and that some vegans eat less protein than they should, especially those who eat little overall. Mariotti and Gardner's review acknowledges these risks and adds that the digestibility of plant protein is only a few percentage points lower.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Needing attention isn't needing meat.** Groups with greater needs can meet them with more legumes, soy and planning.
2. **Every diet requires attention.** Many people who eat meat also have inadequate diets.
3. **The solution is planning, not exploitation.** Guidance from a dietitian solves most cases.

### (f) Back to the moral question

This is an indirect argument: it doesn't say animals don't matter, but that we need them. Since the need doesn't hold for those with access to a varied diet, the question returns to its starting point: if we don't need it, what justifies making animals suffer and die?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in G2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing nutrient and source | "We need protein" becomes "we need meat" |
| Epistemological | Nutrition myth | Assumes plant protein is insufficient |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the official positions | Ignores the consensus of nutrition bodies |
| Moral | Invented necessity | Uses a need that doesn't exist to justify the harm |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Eats beans.** And knows they contain protein.
2. **Doesn't ask where people who eat badly get their protein.** The concern only comes up with vegans.
3. **The animals they eat get protein from plants.** Chickens and pigs eat soy and corn feed.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains G2 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rationalization through the 4Ns | "Necessary" is one of the most used justifications | [Piazza et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25865663/) |
| Confirmation bias | Remembers cases of vegans with deficiencies and forgets the others | General literature on confirmation bias |
| Illusory truth effect | After hearing so often that only meat has protein, the phrase seems like fact | [Questão de Ética, vol. X, ch. 4](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v10/04-cognitive-biases-and-their-influence-on-our-decisions.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a food contains a nutrient, we need that food".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin C | We'd need oranges, not any fruit with vitamin C | No one thinks this way |
| Calcium | We'd need milk, not other sources | This is the argument of G6 |
| Protein | We'd need meat, not beans | The error is the same |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Concern about nutrition is legitimate. Protein is essential, and any diet, vegan or not, needs to be planned to provide enough. Some groups, such as older people and children, deserve special attention.

But the need is for protein, not for animals. With beans, lentils, soy and variety, protein is covered, and the moral question becomes the main one again: if we don't need it, why carry on?
