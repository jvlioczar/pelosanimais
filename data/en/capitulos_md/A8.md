# A8. "They're just animals"

**Thesis:** "they're just animals" isn't an argument; it's a refusal to give one. The phrase declares that species, by itself, decides who counts morally, without pointing to any characteristic relevant to what's at stake. It's what philosophy calls bare speciesism, and it's as arbitrary as any other boundary drawn by the group that benefits from it.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR · **Root chapter:** this is where most chains of objections end

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's just a chicken. Humans are humans, animals are animals."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Members of the human species have full moral status.
2. **P2.** Members of other species don't.
3. **P3.** The difference between P1 and P2 is species membership itself, not some capacity derived from it.
4. **C.** So we may use animals and may not use humans.

This is the most honest formulation of the argument, and also the hardest to defend. When P3 is dropped and a capacity is pointed to (rationality, language, soul, self-awareness), the argument stops being A8 and becomes A15, A16, A17 or A21, each already answered. When P3 is kept, the argument assumes species is morally relevant in itself. This is called **bare speciesism**.

The term "speciesism" was coined by Richard Ryder in 1970, in a leaflet distributed in Oxford, and popularized by Peter Singer in 1975. The current definition, in Oscar Horta's formulation, is unjustified disadvantageous consideration or treatment of those who don't belong to a certain species.

The argument is **valid**, but circular in practice: P3 asserts exactly what was under discussion. Its force isn't logical; it's psychological.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone says: "you can push that boy because he's from another school". You'd ask: so what? Being from another school doesn't change whether the push hurts. When someone says "it's just an animal", they're doing the same thing: saying the pig is from another group. But being from another group doesn't change the fact that he feels fear and pain just the same.

## 4. Short answer

That phrase gives no reason; it just repeats the group the animal belongs to. If there's a characteristic that matters, say which: intelligence, language, self-awareness? Each of these answers already fails, because there are always human beings who also lack it and whom we go on protecting. If the answer is "no characteristic, it's just being another species", then we have a prejudice, not an argument. And research shows that's exactly what happens: people devalue certain species even when they believe them to be intelligent and sentient.

**Question to ask back:** "What characteristic, which every human has and no animal has, justifies this difference?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

This chapter is different from the previous ones. A8 doesn't offer a wrong criterion: it refuses to offer a criterion. That's why it works as the last trench, and why the other objections tend to end up here.

There's a simple test that structures the whole chapter. Ask the speaker for a characteristic **C** such that:

1. every human being has it, including babies, people with profound intellectual disabilities and people with advanced dementia;
2. no animal has it;
3. it's relevant to the treatment in question, that is, to being killed, confined or hurt.

[[ARVORE]]

In more than two thousand years of philosophy, no one has produced a characteristic that passes all three tests. Either it excludes humans we protect, or it's irrelevant to the harm in question, or it coincides with sentience.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's circular.** P3 says species matters because it matters. The conclusion is the premise itself, in other words.

**2. Species is a biological category, not a moral one.** Species are groupings useful to biology, with fuzzy boundaries, hybrids and gradual transitions over evolution. As early as 1970, Ryder put it this way: if all organisms are on a single physical continuum, they should also be on a single moral continuum. Nothing in the definition of species explains why it would generate value.

**3. The structural analogy with other prejudices.** Bare speciesism has the same logical form as other exclusions: taking membership of a group as sufficient reason to discount interests. Care is needed here, and chapter L21 deals with it: asserting a similarity of **logical structure** isn't asserting equivalence between victims or comparing historical suffering. The point concerns the form of the argument, not who suffered more.

**4. There's empirical evidence that bare speciesism exists.** In the study by Caviola, Everett and Faber (2019), people assigned less moral value to certain species **even when their beliefs about those animals' intelligence and sentience were controlled for**. In other words, it isn't that they judge the pig less capable: they judge the pig less worthy for being a pig. And this attitude correlates with racism, sexism, homophobia and social dominance orientation.

**5. The boundary isn't even consistent within a culture.** Dogs are family here and food elsewhere; cows are sacred in India and burgers here; horses are food in France and companions in the UK and the US. The line between "animal to eat" and "animal to love" shifts with the map, which shows it doesn't track any property of the animals.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: we aren't speciesists, we're modal personists

Shelly Kagan proposes an elegant test. Imagine Superman: he doesn't belong to the human species, and yet no one thinks his suffering matters less for that. If species were really what we counted, we should discount him. Since we don't, Kagan concludes that what we value isn't species but being a person, or having been able to be one. He calls this **modal personism**: a baby and a person with a profound disability count because they could have been, or could become, full persons. The resulting thesis is that our differential treatment isn't brute prejudice but a defensible position.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It concedes the essential point.** If what matters isn't species, then "they're just animals" is wrong, and the debate becomes about which capacity matters. That already takes A8 off the stage and leads us to A15, A21 and A19.
2. **The "could have been" is obscure.** In what sense could a baby with anencephaly have been a person? If the answer is "because it's of the right species", modal personism dissolves back into speciesism, as several critics have pointed out.
3. **The empirical evidence contradicts the diagnosis.** Kagan argues people aren't speciesists. The data from Caviola and colleagues indicate the opposite: there's devaluation by species even when beliefs about capacities are controlled for.
4. **The practical conclusion doesn't follow.** Even granting degrees, intense suffering and death in exchange for taste aren't justified, as Kagan himself acknowledges by calling the current treatment of animals a moral atrocity (see A9).
5. **The Superman test works in favor of anti-speciesism.** If the suffering of a being of another species counts when he's strong and like us, it should count when he's weak and different. What changes between the two cases is our interest, not the principle.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Begging the question | Species matters because it matters |
| Logical | Absence of criterion | No characteristic is pointed to, just a label |
| Logical | Generalization by category | Treats all members of a group as equivalent and all outsiders as disposable |
| Logical | Definist fallacy | Uses the word "animal" as if it already contained the moral conclusion |
| Epistemological | Biological category treated as moral | Species is a useful classification in biology, with no normative content |
| Epistemological | Ignoring cultural inconsistency | The line between pet and food varies between countries and eras |
| Moral | Bare speciesism | Group membership as sufficient reason to discount interests |
| Moral | Violating equal consideration | Similar pains weigh differently because of the species label |
| Moral | Self-interested arbitrariness | The boundary is drawn exactly where it benefits whoever draws it |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Divides animals into loved and eaten.** The same person who says "it's just an animal" mourns their own dog and is outraged at people who eat dogs in other countries.
2. **Reacts to cruelty.** Whoever sees a video of cats being tortured feels immediate revulsion, which contradicts the thesis that species is enough to cancel consideration.
3. **Accepts biological continuity.** The same person accepts evolution, which denies any essential boundary between species, and insists on an absolute moral boundary.
4. **Rejects the logic when applied to humans.** "They're just foreigners", "they're just women" and "they're just poor people" sound immediately wrong, and have exactly the same form.
5. **Says speciesism doesn't exist and practices it proudly.** It's common to deny the concept exists and, in the next sentence, to claim humans are worth more for being human.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "belonging to another biological group is enough for someone's interests to be disregarded".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other human groups | Belonging to another group would be enough to lose consideration. The logical form is identical: "they're just X", without pointing to any relevant characteristic | The point is about the structure of the argument, not about equating historical suffering; see L21 |
| Neanderthals, had they survived | They'd be property. They were another species, though with large brains, tools, burials and probably language | Makes visible that the boundary is a historical accident: we lived alongside other human species for thousands of years |
| Sentient extraterrestrials | They could be raised for food, not being of our species. And, by the same rule, they could raise us | Almost no one accepts the second half, which reveals the principle isn't taken seriously |
| A hybrid or an intermediate species | Moral status would change in the middle of a biological continuum, with nothing changing in the capacity to suffer | Species boundaries are fuzzy; binary moral value doesn't track that |
| Dogs and cats | They'd have to go in the same bucket. If "it's just an animal" holds, it holds for all animals | Almost no one accepts this, and that refusal is already recognition that sentience matters |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There are two true things behind the phrase. The first is that humans and other animals really are different, and those differences have moral consequences: we have duties they don't, we create institutions, we answer for our actions. The second is that emotional closeness to our own is a fact of human life, and an ethics demanding total indifference between one's child and a stranger would be unrealistic (see A12).

The mistake lies in turning difference into dismissal. Recognizing that we're different doesn't authorize treating their pain as if it didn't exist. And when asked for the characteristic that would justify that dismissal, it never appears. Usually, "they're just animals" is the point where rational discussion ends and habit takes over. Recognizing that, out loud, is already progress: it's more honest to say "I know I don't have an argument" than to pretend there is one.
