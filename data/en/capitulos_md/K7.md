# K7. "Vegans shouldn't get into politics, because politics is dirty"

**Thesis:** politics carries real risks — of corruption, of co-optation, of concessions that hollow out the cause. But staying out doesn't keep the cause clean: it leaves the decisions to whoever is already there, and many of those already there live off the exploitation of animals. The laws that protect animals today, from cage bans to laws recognising sentience, were won in politics. And the case of the *vaquejada* in Brazil shows the other side: when the Supreme Court ruled the rodeo-style event cruel, Congress changed the Constitution to allow it. Staying out doesn't avoid politics; it just loses.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND · **Note:** the opposite argument, that only politics matters, is K6

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans shouldn't get mixed up in politics. Everyone who goes in gets corrupted, and the cause becomes a bargaining chip."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Institutional politics is marked by corruption, deal-making and economic interests.
2. **P2.** Whoever enters it tends to give in to those interests, and the causes they carry become bargaining chips.
3. **P3.** An ethical movement should preserve its integrity.
4. **C.** So animal advocates should stay out of politics and act only within society.

P1 describes something real, and P2 points to a genuine risk. The problem is the leap to C: the risk of corruption exists, but the alternative, staying out, hands every decision to people with no commitment to animals.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at the final leap: the existence of a risk is a reason to guard against it, not to abandon the place where the rules are made.

## 3. Answer for children

A country's rules are made by people. If the people who love animals don't help make the rules, other people make them, and some of them earn money by mistreating animals. That's why it matters for those who defend animals to be where the rules are decided too, taking care to keep doing what's right.

## 4. Short answer

Politics has risks, and they're real. But staying out doesn't keep the decisions clean: it leaves them to whoever is already there, and many of those already there defend exploitation. The cage bans in Europe and the laws recognising sentience were won in politics. And in Brazil, when the Supreme Court ruled the *vaquejada* cruel, Congress changed the Constitution to allow it. Staying out doesn't avoid politics: it just lets others decide.

**Question back:** "If animal advocates leave politics, who do you think will decide the laws about animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The sentence mixes a legitimate concern with a conclusion that doesn't follow from it.

| What is said | What's true in it | What doesn't follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Politics is dirty" | There is corruption, deal-making and strong economic interests | That all participation is corrupt |
| "They'll get corrupted" | The risk of co-optation exists | That the only defence is not taking part |
| "The movement's place is in society" | Changing the culture is essential | That culture changes the laws on its own |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Staying out isn't neutral.** Laws about animals will be made either way. If advocates don't take part, those who do take part decide, and among them are sectors that live off exploitation and have strong, organised representation, such as the agribusiness caucuses. Abstention doesn't keep the cause pure; it keeps the cause absent.

**2. The gains for animals came from politics.** The ban on conventional battery cages in the European Union came from a 1999 directive. The United Kingdom recognised in law, in 2022, that animals are sentient. In Brazil, rulings and laws against cruel practices depended on political and legal action. None of them would have happened through individual change alone.

**3. The *vaquejada* case shows the cost of not being there.** In 2016, Brazil's Supreme Federal Court struck down a law from the state of Ceará regulating the *vaquejada*, a rodeo-style event, for subjecting the animals to cruelty. The following year Congress passed Constitutional Amendment 96, stating that sporting practices involving animals that are cultural expressions are not considered cruel. A victory in the courts was undone in politics, by those who were organised to do it.

**4. Justice movements went into politics.** Comparison with other movements is instructive. In the fight against slavery, the boycott of goods made with slave labour had limited success and was abandoned in favour of political action, according to the Sentience Institute's review. Abolition, women's suffrage and civil rights required laws, and laws require people to propose, vote for and defend them.

**5. The risk of corruption calls for care, not absence.** Transparency, accountability, financial independence and the movement's own vigilance are ways of reducing the risk. The same reasoning that says to leave politics would say to leave any institution with power: the press, universities, companies.

**6. Taking part in politics isn't only holding office.** Voting with the cause in mind, responding to public consultations, lobbying representatives, supporting organisations that do advocacy, sitting on councils: all of that is politics, and none of it requires becoming a politician.

### (d) The best reply: co-optation

The serious version isn't contempt for politics. It is the observation, documented in the study of social movements, that institutionalisation can co-opt movements: leaders come to depend on the state, demands get softened to fit what is negotiable, and energy flows into institutions instead of changing society.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It recommends balance, not abstention.** The risk of co-optation is an argument for keeping independence and outside pressure alongside participation from within.
2. **Successful movements combined both.** Social mobilisation and institutional action reinforce each other: pressure from outside strengthens those negotiating inside.
3. **The alternative costs more.** A cause with no political presence is at the mercy of those who have one.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in K7 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Overgeneralisation | Some people's corruption becomes the nature of all participation |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either purity outside politics, or corruption within it |
| Moral | Omission disguised as integrity | Not acting is presented as the clean choice |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **They vote, complain about laws and hold governments to account.** Which is already taking part in politics.
2. **They celebrate the laws that protect animals.** Which only exist because someone took part.
3. **They don't ask other movements to leave politics.** The demand applies only to the animal cause.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up K7 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Omission bias | Not taking part seems safer than taking part, even when it costs more | Literature on omission bias |
| Moral purity | Concern for one's own integrity outweighs the outcome for the victims | Literature on moral psychology |
| Political cynicism | General distrust of politics turns into giving up | Literature on political trust |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if an institution carries a risk of corruption, those who defend a just cause should stay out of it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The abolitionist movement | It shouldn't have sought laws against slavery | Contrary to history |
| The environmental movement | It shouldn't work on environmental law | It would hand the rules to polluters |
| Any movement | No just cause could change a law | Only people committed to no cause would make the rules |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets something important right: politics carries real risks, and movements that enter it carelessly can be co-opted, have their demands diluted or trade the cause for positions. Independence, transparency and outside pressure are necessary protections, and cultural change remains essential.

But staying out isn't the answer. Laws will be made either way, and those who don't take part let others make them. The most lasting gains of justice movements came from combining change in society with change in the rules, and the animal cause is no exception.
