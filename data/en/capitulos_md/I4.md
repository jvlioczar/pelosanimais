# I4. "Medical and veterinary students need to practice on animals"

**Thesis:** learning anatomy, physiology and surgery is essential, but research shows that methods that don't harm animals teach just as well. A 2021 systematic review found 50 studies comparing the two kinds of teaching: in 90% of them, humane methods were as or more effective than harmful animal use. Simulators, models, ethically sourced cadavers and real supervised surgeries on animals who need them teach without killing.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Would you want to be operated on by a surgeon who never practiced on an animal? Students need to practice."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Health professionals need practice to work safely.
2. **P2.** Practicing on animals, living or killed for the purpose, is the most effective way to learn.
3. **P3.** Future patients' safety is worth more than these animals' lives.
4. **C.** So using animals in teaching is necessary.

P1 is true. P2 is contradicted by research on teaching. P3 would only apply if there were no effective alternative.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |

| School | "Dissecting the frog is part of biology class." |

## 3. Answer for children

In the past, in class, people cut open frogs and other animals to see what they looked like inside. Today you can learn with models, computer programs and dummies that imitate the body. Studies show that students learn just as well, without any animal having to die.

## 4. Short answer

Practice is essential, but research shows that methods without harm teach just as well: a 2021 review of 50 studies found humane methods as or more effective in 90% of them. Simulators, models, ethically sourced cadavers and supervised surgeries on animals who need them teach without killing. No one needs to kill a healthy animal to learn.

**Question to ask back:** "If students learn just as well with simulators, what does the animal's death add to the teaching?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Use in teaching | Harm-free alternatives |
| --- | --- |
| Dissection in biology classes | Anatomical models, interactive software, videos |
| Surgical practice on healthy animals | Simulators, synthetic models, cadavers donated after natural death |
| Physiology on live animals | Computer simulations, experiments with the students themselves |
| Veterinary clinical training | Real, supervised surgeries on animals who need them, such as spays at shelters |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The evidence on teaching favors alternatives.** Patronek and Rauch reviewed controlled studies in 2007 and found equal or better results with alternative methods. Zemanova and Knight expanded the review in 2021: in 90% of 50 studies, humane methods were as or more effective.

**2. Killing isn't necessary to practice.** Cadavers of animals who died naturally, donated by their guardians, and surgeries the animals actually need provide real practice.

**3. Many schools have already changed.** Medical schools in several countries have dropped the use of live animals in teaching, and Brazil regulates this use under the Arouca Law (Law 11,794/2008), with ethics committees that assess each project.

**4. Teaching also teaches values.** Using animals as disposable material conveys to future professionals an idea about these animals' worth.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Patronek and Rauch, 2007, *JAVMA*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17199490/) | Alternative methods had results equal to or better than animal use | Systematic review of controlled studies |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some skills require living tissue

The most serious version points out that the feel of operating on living tissue, with bleeding and physiological reactions, is hard to reproduce in simulators.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Real surgeries on animals who need them provide this.** Shelter spays and supervised treatments give practice on living tissue without killing healthy animals.
2. **Human medicine already teaches this way.** Doctors learn surgery with simulators, donated cadavers and supervised practice on patients, not by killing people.
3. **The evidence shows the result.** The reviews measure exactly learning, and the alternatives do well.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to kill a healthy animal to teach what can be taught without that. When the alternative works, the harm stops being necessary.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either practice on animals, or no practice |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the research on teaching | Assumes alternatives teach less |
| Moral | Animal as teaching material | The animal's life is worth less than the course's convenience |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Demands real practice.** And accepts that doctors learn without killing people.
2. **Teaches how to care for animals.** By killing healthy animals.
3. **Values science.** And ignores the reviews of its own teaching.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I4 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo bias | The traditional method seems the only effective one | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564) |
| Motivated reasoning | The course's tradition makes it unnecessary to assess alternatives | General literature on motivated cognition |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if practice is useful for learning, using animals is necessary".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teaching surgery on humans | It could use living people | No one accepts that |
| Schools that have dropped animal use | They'd train worse professionals | The reviews don't show this |
| Supervised surgeries on animals who need them | They're acceptable | Here the animal itself benefits |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that practice is essential and that future patients' safety matters a great deal. It's also true that some skills are hard to reproduce in simulators.

But research on teaching shows that alternatives work, and that it's possible to practice on living tissue without killing healthy animals.
