# L25. "Vegans support pseudoscience"

**Thesis:** some vegans do promote pseudoscience, as in any large group of people: diets that "cure" diseases, the idea that humans are herbivores, alkaline eating, distrust of vaccines. This guide has a whole list of claims like these that it asks people not to use. But the case for animals depends on none of them. It rests on sentience, which is what science describes, and the adequacy of a well-planned vegan diet is the position of the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates is a mistake that, applied consistently, would bring down any cause — including that of meat eaters, who have pseudosciences of their own.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND · **Note:** this chapter is about credibility. The idea that veganism is a fad is L24, and that the documentaries are manipulated is L26

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans believe anything: alkaline diets, meat rotting in your gut, milk causing cancer. It's all pseudoscience."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many vegans spread claims about health and nutrition with no scientific basis.
2. **P2.** A movement that tolerates or promotes pseudoscience isn't trustworthy.
3. **C.** So veganism's claims don't deserve credit.

P1 is true of some vegans. P2 is reasonable as a warning, but doesn't license C: the distrust should fall on the false claims, not on true claims made by the same movement.

The argument is **invalid**: the falsity of some claims doesn't make the others false. Each has to be examined on what it says.

## 3. Answer for children

In every group of people there are some who believe things that aren't true, and some vegans do too. But the main reason not to hurt animals doesn't depend on any of those ideas: it depends on something science shows very well, that animals feel pain and fear. One person's wrong idea doesn't make a right idea wrong.

## 4. Short answer

Some vegans spread pseudoscience, and that's bad: this guide has a whole list of those claims, asking people not to use them. But the case for animals doesn't depend on them. It rests on sentience, which science describes, and a well-planned vegan diet is considered adequate by the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates would bring down any cause, including meat eating, which has its own pseudosciences, such as the idea that an all-meat diet is the healthiest.

**Question back:** "Which claim, exactly, do you think is pseudoscience? If it's false, I'll gladly stop using it. But that animals feel pain isn't one of them."

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes three levels that need separating.

| Level | Example | Where it stands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False claims by some vegans | "Humans are biologically herbivores", "a vegan diet cures cancer" | False, and should be dropped |
| Well-founded nutritional claims | A planned vegan diet, with B12, is adequate at all stages of life | Position of the major nutrition bodies |
| The moral foundation | Animals feel, and that counts | Supported by the science of animal consciousness |

The argument uses the first row to discard the other two.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The case for animals doesn't depend on the false claims.** The foundation of veganism is that animals are sentient and that exploiting them causes suffering and death without necessity. That is what science supports: the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness states there is strong scientific support for attributing conscious experience to mammals and birds. Removing every pseudoscience from the debate leaves that foundation intact.

**2. The core nutritional claim is consensus, not pseudoscience.** The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics considers well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets appropriate for all stages of life. Some bodies are more cautious about specific groups, such as Germany's DGE, but adequacy for healthy adults with B12 supplementation is widely accepted (G1).

**3. Judging by the worst part is a reasoning error.** It is guilt by association: if the existence of advocates with false ideas invalidated a cause, none would survive. Medicine, environmentalism and meat eating itself all have advocates who talk nonsense.

**4. Meat eating has its own pseudosciences.** The idea that an all-meat diet is the healthiest (G16), that vegans' B12 shows the diet isn't natural (G4), or that milk is indispensable for bones (G6) are claims the evidence doesn't support. Nobody concludes from this that eating meat is a superstition.

**5. The movement itself corrects these claims.** Organisations and animal advocates do this work, and this guide keeps a list of claims to avoid, including several about nutrition and biology. A movement that criticises its own failings isn't the same as one that relies on them.

**6. Criticism, when specific, is useful.** Pointing to a concrete claim as false helps everyone. The argument only fails when it generalises: "you support pseudoscience, so nothing you say counts".

### (d) The best reply: the movement tolerates error when it suits it

The serious version doesn't say every vegan believes pseudoscience. It says the movement as a whole is lenient with exaggerated claims when they help the cause, and that this leniency undermines trust in the rest.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It asks for rigour, and rigour is welcome.** It is exactly what this guide tries to do: drop what doesn't hold up.
2. **It doesn't reach the foundation.** Even a lenient movement can be right about the main point, and the main point here is well supported.
3. **It applies to any movement.** Every cause has advocates who exaggerate, and nobody concludes from this that all causes are wrong.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in L25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Some people's false ideas make everyone's false |
| Logical | Hasty generalisation | The part becomes the whole |
| Logical | Ad hominem | The gullibility attributed to the advocate replaces examining the argument |
| Epistemic | Wholesale rejection | Throws out true claims together with false ones |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It doesn't dismiss medicine because of quacks.** Nor environmentalism because of exaggerations.
2. **It accepts pseudosciences on its own side.** Such as the all-meat diet as the healthiest.
3. **It demands rigour from some and not others.** The burden of proof falls only on those who defend animals.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up L25 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation bias | Examples of gullible vegans are remembered; rigorous ones are forgotten | Literature on confirmation bias |
| In-group bias | The other group's error seems representative; one's own, an exception | Literature on social identity |
| The meat paradox | Discrediting the messenger avoids dealing with the message | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if some advocates of an idea believe in pseudoscience, the idea doesn't deserve credit".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicine | The existence of quacks would invalidate medicine | Nobody accepts this |
| Tackling climate change | Some activists' exaggerations would invalidate the scientific consensus | Confuses the messenger with the evidence |
| Meat eating | The promises of all-meat diets would make eating meat a superstition | The criterion brings down its users too |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets something right, and it must be acknowledged: some vegans spread pseudoscience, and that harms the cause, the health of those who believe it and public trust. Some of these claims appear in popular documentaries and on social media, and they're worth correcting every time. That's why this guide keeps a list of claims not to use (see [Stop saying this](../argumentos-ruins.html)).

But the case for animals doesn't depend on those claims. It rests on what science describes well, that animals feel, and the adequacy of a planned vegan diet is the position of the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates is a standard no cause would survive, including that of meat eaters.
