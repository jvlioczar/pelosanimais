# N10. "I was vegan for my health, and I changed my mind"

**Thesis:** someone who adopted a plant-based diet only for health or only for the environment is right on one point: if that was the only reason and the person changed their mind about the facts, no reason was left. That is why indirect arguments are fragile. But someone who followed a plant-based diet for health was following a diet, not veganism, which is an ethical position about animals. In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, health was the only motivation cited by a majority of those who gave up (58%). The question that never got asked is the one about the animals, and it doesn't depend on any nutrition study.

**Family:** N. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I went vegan because of a health documentary. Then I read more and saw it wasn't quite like that. It was never about the animals. Now I eat everything, in balance."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** I adopted a plant-based diet because I believed it was the healthiest (or the best for the environment).
2. **P2.** After informing myself better, I changed my mind about those facts.
3. **P3.** Without that reason, there is no other reason to keep the diet.
4. **C.** So I have no reason to continue.

P1 and P2 are sincere accounts, and P2 may even be partly correct: many health promises made in the name of veganism are exaggerated. The problem lies in P3: one reason was left out of the count, the animals, and it hasn't been refuted, because it was never examined.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P3 is only true for someone who never considered the animals, and that is a gap, not an answer.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone who stopped pulling the cat's tail because they heard that cats bring bad luck. One day they find out the bad luck story is nonsense. Can they go back to pulling the cat's tail? No, because the real reason was always something else: it hurts the cat. With animals that become food it's similar. Even if a person changes their mind about health, the animals' reason is still there.

## 4. Short answer

That makes sense: if the reason was only health and you changed your mind, the diet lost its footing. But it shows you were following a diet, not veganism, which was never about the health of the one eating. It's about the one being eaten. On the facts: a well-planned plant-based diet is adequate, according to the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States, but it isn't a miracle cure or the only way to be healthy. The question that got left out is a different one: if it's possible to live well without making animals suffer and die, why do it?

**Question to ask back:** "Did you ever think about the question of the animals, or was it never part of your decision?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The same plate can have very different reasons behind it, and they hold up differently:

| Reason for the diet | What it depends on | What knocks it down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health | Nutrition studies and how the person feels | A new study, a new fad diet, feeling unwell (N1) |
| Environment | Calculations of emissions and land use | The belief that local or "regenerative" meat solves it (H8) |
| Animals | Their feeling pain and having a life to lose | Only proof that they don't feel, which doesn't exist (A5) |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The argument is coherent, and it shows how fragile indirect reasons are.** Someone who stops eating animals only for a benefit to themselves can turn back when the benefit looks smaller. This guide keeps indirect arguments separate for exactly that reason: health and the environment are good additional motives, but they aren't the heart of the matter.

**2. It was a diet, not veganism.** Veganism is the refusal, as far as is possible and practicable, to exploit animals: in food, clothing, entertainment and testing (L13). Someone who only changed their plate for health never got there. This isn't a criticism of the person: it's a description. And it means that "I used to be vegan and I know what it's like" (N6) describes, in this case, the experience of a diet.

**3. The data fit the account.** In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, the only motivation cited by a majority of former vegetarians and vegans was health (58%). Among those who continued, five motivations passed the halfway mark: health (69%), animal protection (68%), disgust at meat (63%), the environment (59%) and taste (52%). In a 2013 study of 292 vegetarians, published in the journal *Appetite* by Hoffman and colleagues, those with ethical motivation had stronger conviction and had kept the diet longer than those motivated by health. There is a caveat: in a 2022 Faunalytics study that followed 222 people for six months, the type of main motivation made no difference to success over that period. What counted most was the level of commitment.

**4. The health facts, with no exaggeration either way.** The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States stated in 2016 that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are healthful and adequate at all stages of life. In its 2025 update, which covers only adults who aren't pregnant or lactating, it maintained that these patterns can be nutritionally adequate and offer long-term benefits. "Well planned" includes supplementing B12. None of this makes a plant-based diet a cure for everything, and no one needs it in order to be healthy: anyone who heard those promises in a documentary is right to be wary (L20). The mistake is to conclude the opposite from the exaggeration (G12).

**5. The environmental facts.** Buying local meat changes little: transport accounts for less than 10% of emissions for most foods. In the data compiled by Our World in Data, the lowest-impact beef emits about 9 kg of CO2 equivalent per 100 g of protein, against 0.8 kg for the highest-impact peas (H8).

**6. The question that was never asked.** A diet can be swapped when the science changes. The animal's situation in the slaughterhouse doesn't change with any nutrition review. Someone for whom it was "never about the animals" hasn't yet answered the question about them: they have only set it aside.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: I thought about the animals and wasn't convinced

The most serious version says: it's not that I never thought about it. I considered the argument about animals and it didn't convince me. My reason was health, that's legitimate, and no one can force me to have another.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's legitimate as far as the diet goes.** No one should keep a health diet they have stopped believing in. But in that case the conversation moves elsewhere: it becomes about the animals, and the chapters are different ones (A5, A16, B1, B5).
2. **"I wasn't convinced" calls for an argument.** Animals feel pain and fear. For that not to count, one has to point to a relevant difference between their suffering and ours. Species alone isn't that difference.
3. **Changing your mind about nutrition doesn't change the facts about animals.** The two questions are independent. You can think a plant-based diet isn't the healthiest and still conclude that one shouldn't kill someone who feels when there is an alternative.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Health and the environment are reasons about the one who eats and about the planet. The third party involved was missing: the animal. They have a life that goes well or badly for them, and that life ends at slaughter, whether the meat is healthy or not, local or not. The moral question isn't "which diet is best for me?". It is: do I have a good enough reason to do this to someone who feels? Someone for whom it was "never about the animals" can start there, now.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hidden premise | Assumes health and the environment were the only possible reasons |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "vegan" for a diet, and draws a conclusion about an ethical position |
| Epistemological | From exaggeration to the opposite | Exaggerated promises were false, so a plant-based diet must be inadequate |
| Moral | Leaving out the third party | Decides by weighing only one's own interests, without counting the animals' |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they changed their mind, but about the animals there was no view to change.** The question was never examined.
2. **Distrusts documentaries, but trusts the next guru.** The new diet usually comes from sources of the same kind.
3. **Swapped the environmental reason for local meat.** The data show that the type of food weighs far more than the distance.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains N10 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority bias | One documentary or influencer decides the way in, and another decides the way out | General literature on heuristics |
| Confirmation bias | After deciding to go back, only the studies favorable to meat are remembered | General literature on confirmation bias |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I stopped believing in the reason that led me to a practice, there is no other reason to keep it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone who didn't drive drunk only for fear of the fine | With no enforcement, they could drive drunk | The main reason was the other people on the road |
| Someone who treated employees well only because it was profitable | If it stopped being profitable, they could mistreat them | No one would accept this: they always deserved respect |
| Someone who stopped smoking near their children only because of the smell on their clothes | With the smell solved, they could start again | The children's health was the reason they had yet to see |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that a diet adopted for health should be judged by health, and that changing your mind in the face of new information is a sign of honesty. It's right that a lot of vegan outreach exaggerates: it promises cures, weight loss and boundless energy, and people who come in through those promises leave when they fail. That is a fair criticism of the movement: promoting veganism as a miracle diet produces people who give up.

But this shows the reason was weak, not that no strong one exists. The central motive was never the body of the one who eats. It was always the body of the one who is eaten, and that motive still stands, waiting to be considered for the first time.
