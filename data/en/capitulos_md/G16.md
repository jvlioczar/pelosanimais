# G16. "A carnivore, paleo or keto diet is healthiest"

**Thesis:** the evidence for the carnivore diet is weak, short term and based mostly on personal accounts. Large long-term studies point the other way: in a study of more than 15,000 adults followed for about 25 years, those who replaced carbohydrates with animal protein and fat had higher mortality, and those who replaced them with plant sources had lower mortality. Losing weight in the short term isn't the same as living longer.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The carnivore diet cured my illness. Our ancestors ate only meat. Carbs are the villain."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Low-carbohydrate diets can lead to weight loss and better blood sugar control in the short term.
2. **P2.** Many people report health improvements when adopting carnivore or ketogenic diets.
3. **P3.** Our hunter-gatherer ancestors ate a lot of meat.
4. **C.** So a meat-based diet is the healthiest.

P1 is supported by short-term studies. P2 describes personal accounts, not controlled evidence. P3 is debatable and, even if true, wouldn't tell us what's best today (C4). The problem lies in the conclusion: long-term studies don't support it.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because P2 and P3 don't support a conclusion about long-term health.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people think eating only meat is good for you. But when scientists follow thousands of people for many years, they see that people who eat more fruit, vegetables, beans and grains tend to live longer. Varied food, full of plants, is what helps the body most.

## 4. Short answer

The evidence for the carnivore diet is weak and short term: it comes mostly from personal accounts and surveys of enthusiastic volunteers. Large long-term studies point the opposite way. In a study of more than 15,000 adults followed for about 25 years, published in *The Lancet Public Health* in 2018, replacing carbohydrates with animal protein and fat was linked to higher mortality, and replacing them with plant sources, to lower mortality.

**Question to ask back:** "Is there any long-term study, with thousands of people, showing that meat-based diets make people live longer?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes very different kinds of evidence:

| Kind of evidence | What it shows | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal accounts | People felt better | Weak: no controls, no follow-up |
| Short-term studies of low-carb diets | Weight loss and better blood sugar | Moderate, but only in the short term |
| Large long-term cohort studies | Diets with more meat and fewer plants linked to higher mortality | Strong for association |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Personal accounts aren't controlled evidence.** People who feel good on a diet tend to talk about it; those who feel unwell usually quit quietly. Changes such as cutting ultra-processed food and sugar also explain some of the improvements.

**2. Long-term studies point the other way.** In the ARIC study, with 15,428 adults followed for about 25 years, the lowest mortality was linked to a moderate carbohydrate intake. Replacing carbohydrates with animal protein and fat was linked to higher mortality; replacing them with plant sources, to lower mortality.

**3. Processed and red meat have known risks.** The WHO's cancer agency classifies processed meat as carcinogenic and red meat as probably carcinogenic (G17).

**4. What works is quality, not meat.** Many of the benefits attributed to low-carb diets come from cutting sugar and ultra-processed food, which can be done on a plant diet.

**5. Our ancestors didn't eat only meat.** Hunter-gatherers had very varied diets, with plenty of plants, roots and fruit, depending on the environment.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Seidelmann et al., 2018, *Lancet Public Health*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30122560/) | Replacing carbohydrates with animal protein and fat was linked to higher mortality; with plant sources, to lower mortality | 15,428 adults followed for about 25 years, plus a meta-analysis of other studies; an observational study |
| [IARC, press release no. 240 (2015)](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf) | Processed meat is carcinogenic; red meat is probably carcinogenic | Also used in D4 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: observational studies don't prove causation

The most serious version points out that studies like ARIC are observational, based on food questionnaires, and don't prove that meat causes mortality.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The evidence for the carnivore diet is even weaker.** If observational studies aren't enough, personal accounts are even less so.
2. **Several lines of evidence converge.** Cohort studies, biological mechanisms and trials on saturated fat point in the same direction.
3. **The body of evidence favors plants.** Plant-rich diets have the largest body of favorable evidence, from several kinds of study.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if a meat-based diet brought some short-term personal benefit, that wouldn't justify the animals' deaths. And since long-term studies don't support that benefit, the moral question returns to the center.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in G16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Anecdotal evidence | Personal accounts against long-term studies |
| Epistemological | Confusing short and long term | Weight loss treated as longevity |
| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Selects short-term studies and ignores cohorts |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | Uses ancestors as the model |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts personal accounts as proof, but rejects observational studies.** The standard of evidence is selective.
2. **Uses ancestors as a model only for diet.** In nothing else.
3. **Ignores that hunter-gatherers ate many plants.** The model itself doesn't confirm the diet.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains G16 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation bias | Seeks only information that confirms a taste for meat | General literature on confirmation bias |
| Survivorship bias | Only those who adapted to the diet tell their story | Linked to D4 |
| Rationalization through the 4Ns | "Natural" and "necessary" | [Piazza et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25865663/) |
| Overconfidence (Dunning-Kruger) | A little reading on nutrition produces more certainty than the experts have | [Kruger and Dunning, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone reports getting better on a diet, it's the healthiest".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fad diets in general | Every diet with positive accounts would be the best | They all have positive accounts |
| Vegan diets | They'd also be the healthiest, because there are accounts | The argument settles nothing |
| Extreme fasting | It would be healthy, because some report feeling well | No one concludes this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There are truths here: low-carb diets can help with weight loss and blood sugar control in the short term, and cutting sugar and ultra-processed food is good for you. Many people really do feel better when they change their diet, and those experiences deserve attention.

But what long-term studies show is that the healthiest path runs through more plants, not more meat. And no personal benefit answers the question about the animals.
