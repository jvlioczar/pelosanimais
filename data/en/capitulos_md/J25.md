# J25. "A party with only vegan food excludes people who eat meat"

**Thesis:** it's the other way round. People who eat meat also eat rice, beans, salad, pasta, fruit and sweets without animal ingredients; vegans don't eat meat. A vegan menu is the only one everyone present can eat, so it's the most inclusive. Nobody goes hungry because there's no meat, and nobody is forced into anything: the party simply doesn't offer one kind of food. Often the refusal doesn't even come from the food, but from the word "vegan": research shows that the label by itself lowers acceptance of dishes people would happily eat, and that there is prejudice against vegans. Refusing out of prejudice is disrespectful to people who chose a more ethical life, without animal exploitation. And it isn't the same as saying vegans disrespect people who eat meat: on one side there is an imposition on victims, the animals; on the other, there isn't.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Making the school party vegan-only is an imposition. People who eat meat are left out."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** At a party or group event, the menu should respect the preferences of the majority.
2. **P2.** Most people eat meat.
3. **P3.** A vegan-only menu ignores that preference.
4. **C.** So a vegan-only menu excludes people who eat meat.

P1 is debatable: at a group event, the fairest criterion is that everyone can eat, not that the majority eats what they prefer. P2 is true. P3 is true, but ignoring a preference isn't excluding: people who eat meat can still eat everything that's served.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1 and in the step from "not having the preferred food" to "being excluded".

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "A party without meat isn't a party." |

| Disguised version | "The state can't ban what I eat." (which is E2) |

## 3. Answer for children

At a party with egg-free chocolate cake, popcorn and veggie hot dogs, everyone can eat. If there were only food with meat, some friends would have nothing to eat. The party where everyone can eat is the one that includes the most people. Sometimes someone won't even try the food just because they heard the word "vegan", and that isn't fair to whoever planned the party thinking of everyone and of the animals.

## 4. Short answer

People who eat meat can eat vegan food, but vegans can't eat meat. That's why a vegan menu is the only one everyone can eat: it's the most inclusive, not the one that excludes. Nobody goes without food, nobody is banned from anything outside the event, and the party simply didn't offer one kind of dish. Often the rejection is of the label, not the food: research shows that the word "vegan" lowers the choice of dishes people would accept under another name. Refusing out of prejudice disrespects people who chose to live without exploiting animals. And that isn't equivalent to vegans "disrespecting" people who eat meat: only one side imposes something on victims, the animals.

**Question to ask back:** "At the vegan party, who was left with nothing to eat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Menu | Who can eat everything | Who is left without options |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat only | People who eat meat | Vegans, vegetarians, people with some religious or health restrictions |
| Mixed | People who eat meat | Sometimes those who don't, for lack of dishes or cross-contamination |
| Vegan only | Everyone | Nobody goes without food; those who refuse usually refuse the label, not the food |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Not having your favorite dish isn't exclusion.** Excluding means stopping someone from taking part. People who eat meat take part and eat normally at a vegan party.

**2. Vegan food is the most compatible.** It also suits vegetarians, many people allergic to milk or eggs, and several religious restrictions, such as those of people who don't eat pork or beef.

**3. Nobody loses a right.** Outside the party, everyone still eats what they want. The event just chose a menu, as it always does.

**4. Many everyday dishes are already vegan.** Rice, beans, salads, pasta with tomato sauce, fruit, popcorn and many sweets have no animal ingredients.

**5. Often the refusal is of the label, not the food.** People who say they'll go hungry at a vegan party often eat, on other days, the same dishes without noticing they're vegan. Research suggests the problem is often the word. In an experiment with more than 7,000 US adults, published in 2024, a basket of food without meat or dairy was chosen by 20% of participants when called "vegan" and by more than 40% when called "healthy" or "sustainable". Another study, from 2017, found prejudice against vegetarians and vegans: people who eat meat rated them as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups commonly targeted by prejudice, and rated vegans more negatively than vegetarians. These studies come from Western countries and don't measure any particular party, but they show that rejection can come from an idea about vegans, not from the taste. Refusing a dish out of prejudice is disrespectful to whoever made it and to people who chose a more ethical life, without animal exploitation.

**6. "But vegans disrespect meat eaters too" is a false equivalence.** The usual reply is that vegans disrespect non-vegans when they turn down meat or criticize eating it. But the two situations aren't the same. Someone who eats meat suffers no harm at a vegan party: they eat normally, and nothing is taken from them outside it. Eating meat, on the other hand, involves an imposition on victims, the animals, who are bred and killed without being able to refuse. Disagreeing with a practice that harms others isn't the same as looking down on someone for not causing that harm. Treating the two as equal is a false equivalence, and answering a criticism with "you too" is the [tu quoque fallacy](../falacias.html#tu-quoque). None of this entitles either side to treat the other with contempt; it only shows that the two complaints don't weigh the same.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: a party is also about tradition and pleasure

The most serious version says the barbecue or the meat dish is part of the party's tradition, and that removing it changes the meaning of the celebration.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Party traditions change all the time.** Menus change with the times, the place and who is there.
2. **Some people's pleasure doesn't outweigh everyone's participation.** At a group event, making sure everyone can eat matters more than serving one group's favorite dish.
3. **The moral question remains.** For the animals, a party with meat isn't a neutral tradition.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question isn't whether the party became less traditional, but whether it's fair for animals to be killed for a celebration that can happen without that, and with everyone at the table.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Excluding" used to mean "not offering the favorite dish" |
| Logical | Reversal | Calls excluding the only menu everyone can eat |
| Moral | Preference treated as a right | The majority's taste becomes the event's obligation |
| Logical | False equivalence | Equates refusing vegan food with refusing meat, though only one side involves victims |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Talks about inclusion.** And defends the menu that leaves some people with nothing to eat.
2. **Talks about imposition.** And accepts a meat menu being imposed on those who don't eat it.
3. **Says they'll go hungry.** And can eat everything that was served.
4. **Eats vegan dishes every day.** And only refuses them when they're called "vegan".

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J25 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo | The usual menu seems neutral, and a different one seems like an imposition | General literature on decision-making |
| Loss aversion | Losing one dish weighs more than gaining others | General literature on loss aversion |
| Label effect | The word "vegan" lowers the choice of a dish that would be accepted under another name | [Sleboda et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2023.102217) |
| Prejudice against vegans | The food is rejected because of the image of those who advocate it | [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a menu without the dish I prefer excludes me".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A party without soda | It would exclude people who like soda | Nobody would say that |
| A peanut-free party, because of an allergic guest | It would exclude people who like peanuts | It's usually seen as inclusion |
| A vegan party | Everyone can eat | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that food is part of the tradition and pleasure of parties, that menu changes can bother people, and that the choice should be talked through.

But a vegan menu leaves nobody without food, and it's the only one everyone can share. It includes rather than excludes.
