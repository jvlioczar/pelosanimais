# B3. "They had a good life and a quick death"

**Thesis:** the argument combines two promises that are rarely kept and that those who repeat them can almost never check. And even when they are kept, they don't justify the conclusion. A good life gives no one the right to take it, and a quick death is still a death. No one would accept the sentence as a justification for killing a dog or a person.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The animals had a good life and died without noticing. Better than dying of old age in the wild."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** What matters to an animal is the quality of its life and the absence of suffering.
2. **P2.** An animal raised with good welfare and slaughtered without pain had a life worth living and a death without suffering.
3. **P3.** In the wild, many animals die of hunger, disease or predation, with great suffering.
4. **C.** So raising and slaughtering with good welfare does not harm the animal, and may even be better for it.

P3 is true: life in the wild involves a great deal of suffering (see Beyond veganism). The problem lies in P1, which leaves out the loss of life, and in P2, which describes a rare ideal.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1 (B2) and P2 (B1 and B4). The comparison in P3 is a **false dichotomy**: the alternative to the slaughterhouse isn't the wild, it's not breeding animals to kill them.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone says: "Your kitten has lived very well, so now he can be killed." Would you agree? No, because he could still live many happy years. Having a good life is no reason to take it away. It's precisely because it's good that it's worth going on living.

## 4. Short answer

First, how do we know the life was good? Those who say it have almost never seen the farm the meat came from, and about three quarters of farmed land animals live in intensive systems. But even if it had been good, a good life doesn't give the right to take it: if it was good, death takes something valuable from the animal. Would you accept being killed young because you had a good life until then? And a quick death is still the death of a young animal that had many years ahead of it. As for nature, the comparison doesn't hold: the alternative to the slaughterhouse isn't being eaten by a wolf, it's not being bred to die. And notice that no one would accept that sentence about a dog.

**Question to ask back:** "Would you accept someone killing your dog by saying he had a good life and a quick death? And is what he would lose any different from what the cow loses?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument combines two promises and a comparison:

| Part | What it claims | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good life | The animals lived well | About three quarters of farmed animals live in intensive systems (B4) |
| Quick death | They died without noticing | Stunning fails, and some permitted methods cause pain (B1) |
| Comparison | Better than nature | The real alternative is not breeding them, not releasing them into the wild |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. "They had a good life" is a hasty conclusion.** Those who say it rarely know which farm the animal they ate came from, and have almost never seen it. An animal's welfare also can't be read from the landscape: it depends on health, pain, fear and the chance to behave according to its own nature, things that are hard to assess even for specialists. And the odds that the sentence is true are low: about three quarters of the world's farmed land animals live in intensive systems (B4). The sentence usually describes the picture on the package, not a known life.

**2. Even if it had a good life, that increases the loss.** If the animal's life was good, death takes something valuable from it. The promise of a good life is an argument against killing, not for it (B2). The exercise is worth doing: would anyone accept being killed young and painlessly on the grounds that they had a good life until then? Or accept living a comfortable life knowing it was planned to end when it suited someone else?

**3. A better life doesn't license use.** It doesn't follow that the solution would be to keep the animal alive, in a reasonable life, and go on exploiting it. Saying that death is a harm doesn't make the rest acceptable. Keeping animals for use involves controlling where they live, with whom, when they breed and what is taken from them. And in practice, farming for milk, eggs or wool also usually ends in slaughter, once the animal stops paying its way (B5). A better life reduces one harm; it doesn't license the others.

**4. The animals are killed young.** In commercial production, broiler chickens are slaughtered at a few weeks old and pigs at about five months, long before the natural end of their lives.

**5. A quick death isn't guaranteed either.** Even on the best farms stunning can fail, and some permitted methods cause pain before unconsciousness (B1).

**6. The comparison with nature is a false dichotomy.** That leaves the last part of the sentence, that it would be better than dying in the wild. It compares slaughter with an option that isn't on the table. Farmed animals wouldn't be released into the wild: they simply wouldn't be brought into existence to die (B7). Wild animal suffering is real, but it isn't the alternative to slaughter.

**7. No one applies the sentence to humans or dogs.** A happy life has never been a justification for cutting it short.

**8. The question isn't whether it could have been, but whether it was.** Peter Singer, in *Practical Ethics*, notes that, even outside factory farms, raising animals usually involves castration, separating mothers from their young, hot-iron branding, transport and slaughter, and he doubts that raising animals without suffering is feasible on the scale needed to feed cities. For the buyer, he says, the relevant question isn't whether meat *could* have been produced without suffering, but whether the meat in front of them *was* (ch. 3). "They had a good life" usually answers the first question and assumes the answer to the second (point 1). Singer was writing about the United States and Europe in the early 1990s; current data on intensive farming are in the table in (c).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: a net positive

The most serious version says that, for animals who would not otherwise have existed, a good life followed by a painless death is a net positive: they gain a life worth living. It would be better for them to exist this way than not to exist.

The most respected defender of this position in practice is the animal scientist Temple Grandin, who spent her career designing facilities and audits to reduce suffering in handling and slaughter (B1). For her, those who raise animals for food owe them a decent life and a painless death; with both conditions met, farming would be acceptable (*Animals Make Us Human*, 2009).

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Bringing someone into existence gives no right to kill them.** Parents who give life to a child don't gain the right to take it (B7).
2. **A net positive doesn't require an early death.** If the animal's life is good, the balance would be even greater if it lived longer. Death doesn't improve the sum; it only cuts it short.
3. **The counter-reply only applies to the exception.** For most animals, life is not good, and the balance, even by this criterion, is negative.
4. **Grandin's two conditions are this chapter's two promises, and the answer stays the same.** A decent life doesn't license taking it (point 2 of (b)), and a painless death is still the loss of everything that would have come after (B2). The merit of Grandin's work lies in reducing real suffering, not in showing that death stops being a harm.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Slaughterhouse or the wild, as if there were no other option |
| Logical | Non sequitur | From "it had a good life" it concludes "it can be killed" |
| Epistemological | Generalizing from the ideal case | Takes the exception as the rule |
| Moral | Reducing harm to suffering | Forgets the loss of life |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wouldn't accept the sentence for their own dog.** A pet's good life is a reason to care for it longer.
2. **Mourns the early death of those who lived well.** Among humans, a good life cut short is seen as a tragedy.
3. **Uses nature as a comparison only here.** No one justifies other practices by saying they'd be worse in the wild.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B3 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Optimism bias | Assumes the animals eaten had the life shown in the ads | Linked to B4 |
| Meat paradox | The image of a good life eases the tension between liking animals and eating them | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| Willful ignorance | People avoid learning how the animals really lived and died | Linked to E12 |
| Duration neglect | A whole life is summed up as one image of pasture and a quick end, without counting how long each part lasted | [Fredrickson and Kahneman, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.1.45) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a good life followed by a quick death makes death acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dogs and cats | They could be killed after a few years of good life | Almost no one accepts this |
| Humans | A happy person could be killed painlessly | Absurd |
| Rescued animals | Sanctuaries could slaughter them after a while | Contradicts the very reason sanctuaries exist |

## 11. What the argument gets right

A life with good welfare is much better than a life in confinement, and a painless death is much better than a painful one. It's also true that nature holds enormous suffering, and that idealizing wild life would be a mistake.

But none of this turns death into a favor. If the animal's life is good, the best thing to do for it is to let it live, without turning it into a resource.
