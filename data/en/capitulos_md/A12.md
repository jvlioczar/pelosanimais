# A12. "It's natural to put our own first"

**Thesis:** partiality lets us prefer; it doesn't let us exploit. We may save our own child first in a fire, but that doesn't authorize us to hurt the neighbor's child so ours can have a snack. The argument swaps a case of tragic conflict, where only one can be saved, for an everyday practice where there's no conflict at all: eating animals saves no human. Even defenders of partiality, such as Mary Midgley and J. Baird Callicott, admit it has limits: the strength of an interest can outweigh closeness.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My family first, then everyone else. It's natural to look after our own first."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We have special duties to those close to us: family, friends, community.
2. **P2.** The species is our widest community, and solidarity among humans is one of those special bonds.
3. **P3.** Those with a special bond to us may be given priority over those without one.
4. **C.** So we may prioritize human interests over animals' interests.

P1 is true, and moral philosophy takes it seriously: an ethics demanding total indifference between your own child and a stranger would be unrealistic. The problem lies in P2 and, above all, in what the conclusion authorizes.

The argument is **valid** if the conclusion is "we may give priority in conflicts". It fails when the conclusion becomes "we may use outsiders as we please", which is what the practice requires.

## 3. Answer for children

Everyone loves their own family most, and that's good. If your mum and a stranger needed help at the same time, you'd help your mum first. But loving your family more doesn't let you hurt other people's families. It's the same with animals: loving people more doesn't let anyone hurt a little pig just because he isn't part of our family.

## 4. Short answer

Preferring our own is normal and legitimate in real conflicts: in a fire, I can save my child before a stranger. But preferring isn't the same as being allowed to exploit. No one thinks loving your own child authorizes hurting the neighbor's child. And eating animals isn't a tragic conflict: no human is saved when you pick chicken over beans. Picking the beans, on the other hand, spares the lives of individuals who feel, and leaves less suffering in the world.

**Question to ask back:** "Does loving your own family authorize you to hurt other people's families?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument merges two different things:

| What partiality permits | What partiality doesn't permit |
| --- | --- |
| Giving priority to those close to us when we can't help everyone | Harming outsiders to get a smaller benefit |
| Devoting more time and resources to those we love | Treating outsiders as resources |
| Saving your own child first in a fire | Hurting the neighbor's child so ours can play |

Priority applies in situations of **tragic conflict**, where only one can be helped. Consuming animals isn't one of them: on one side is the animal's life, on the other a menu preference.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Preferring isn't exploiting.** Partiality is a permission to favor those close to us, not a license to harm those far away. Parents who love their children don't thereby gain the right to attack other children.

**2. There's no conflict.** Eating animals saves no human. The comparison with the fire, where only one can be saved, doesn't describe a supermarket choice (F3). And the asymmetry is clear: on the chicken side, no human gains anything the beans wouldn't give; on the bean side, sentient individuals are no longer bred to die. One choice protects no one; the other spares someone.

**3. The species isn't a family.** Special bonds arise from real relationships: living together, care, dependence. The species is a biological category grouping billions of strangers. Many people have a stronger bond with their own dog than with any human on another continent.

**4. The same logic has already justified the worst.** "Our own first" has been used to justify excluding foreigners, other ethnic groups and other groups. The circle of "our own" has always been drawn by those inside it.

**5. The argument concedes the essential point.** If animals need to be put "after", then they're in the queue. That already admits their interests count, and the question becomes how to weigh them, not whether they can be ignored.

**6. Preferring one's own species isn't a clear natural fact.** The psychologist Lewis Petrinovich, in *Darwinian Dominion* (1999), argued that every social animal favors its own species and that human interests should therefore prevail over comparable interests of other species. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane (2010, pp. 37–38) replies in three ways. The description is doubtful: animals live and associate with other species, and fight members of their own, even killing them. Even if it were a natural fact, that wouldn't make it right, since many forms of violence are also natural (C1). And if belonging to a biological group authorized preference, it would also hold for race and sex, which almost no one accepts.

**7. Affections are real, but they don't settle on their own what we owe.** In *Practical Ethics*, Peter Singer discusses a review of *Animal Liberation* that urged accepting partiality toward one's own species the way one accepts the universe, since fighting it might dry up the source of all affection. He answers with a question: some people are more attached to their cat than to their neighbours; would those who ground morality in affection accept that such a person should save the cat from a fire first? And whoever said yes would hardly want to stand beside those who argue for preferring their own race on the same grounds. Ethics, he concludes, doesn't ask anyone to give up their affections; it asks that, when we act, we weigh the claims of those affected with some independence from what we feel for them (ch. 3). In the chapter on rich and poor, he examines "looking after our own" and grants it an element of truth: families and communities that look after their members achieve, through ties of affection, what would otherwise take a huge bureaucracy. So he allows a small preference for those close to us, but denies that distance or membership of a community makes a crucial difference to what we owe people in need (ch. 8). Singer is discussing human poverty there; the transfer is this guide's. Partiality may decide whom to save when only one can be saved (F3), but it doesn't license harming those outside the circle to obtain something minor.

**8. You can't get an ought from an is.** From the fact that we favor those close to us, the argument concludes that this is right. But a fact, by itself, doesn't say what is right: reaching a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, and that premise is what needs defending. This leap is [Hume's law](../falacias.html#hume-s-law-from-is-to-ought); see also [Describing is not evaluating](../comece-aqui.html#descrever), in Start here.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the human moral community and the mixed community

The most serious version doesn't talk about family. It says morality arose among humans to regulate life in common, and it's reasonable for members of that community to have stronger duties to each other. Animals would be protected by weaker duties, but wouldn't have the same priority.

The philosopher Mary Midgley gave this idea its most careful form. For her, nearness and kinship matter to morality, because we are bond-forming creatures, not abstract intellects; total impartiality would be unrealistic. Midgley doesn't use this to exclude animals: she defends strong protection for them, within a "mixed community" of humans and domestic animals, and notes that real conflicts between the two sides are rare. But she accepts that, where there is conflict, humans may come first (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 81–82).

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Weaker duties are still duties.** Even on this view, causing suffering and death for the pleasure of taste doesn't pass the minimum filter.
2. **The moral community has always expanded.** It once excluded women, foreigners and enslaved people, and each expansion was later recognized as a correction (A22).
3. **Those who can't take part are already protected.** Babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities aren't party to any agreement, and no one accepts leaving them with "weaker" duties (A21).
4. **Priority in conflict isn't the case under discussion.** Even if granted, it doesn't apply to a practice without conflict.
5. **Species changes the content of a duty, not its priority.** Midgley argues that we need to know someone's species to know what we owe them, but not their race or sex. The philosopher Elisa Aaltola (2005) replies that sometimes we do need to know someone's race or sex, for example in matters of health or historical redress. And knowing what someone needs doesn't say who comes first: a hen needs different things from a human, and that doesn't make her interests less important (Cochrane, 2010, p. 82).
6. **What holds in personal life doesn't hold in state policy.** Political theorists distinguish first-order partiality, each person's toward family and friends, which almost everyone accepts, from second-order partiality, that of state decisions, which should be as impartial as possible. A judge can't favor their own child. So even those who accept partiality in private life have no basis for defending it as a public rule (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 82–83).
7. **Defenders of partiality themselves pull back.** The environmental philosopher J. Baird Callicott proposed an order of priorities by closeness: family, then distant humans, then domestic animals, then wild ones. Faced with the objection that this would permit spending on luxuries for those close to us while strangers starve, he granted that the strength of the interest also counts, and that hunger beats luxury. Cochrane notes that this concession brings Callicott close to the impartial position he wanted to avoid (2010, p. 84). Applied to animals, it weighs against the argument: an animal's life is a strong interest, and the taste of a dish a weak one.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept that family authorizes attacking others.** Loving your children has never justified hurting the neighbor's children.
2. **Loves their own dog more than strangers.** Real bonds don't follow the species boundary.
3. **Condemns "our own first" among humans.** The same phrase, applied to nationality or ethnicity, is recognized as prejudice.
4. **Prioritizes where there's no conflict.** No one stops helping a human by not eating meat.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "those who are our own may use those who aren't for smaller benefits".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Families | Parents could hurt other people's children for the benefit of their own | No one accepts this |
| Nations | Foreigners could be exploited by nationals | It's the logic of colonialism |
| Ethnic groups | The majority group could use the others | Recognized as racism |
| Species | A stronger species could use us | Whoever rejects this rejects the premise |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Partiality is a legitimate part of moral life. Loving your own family more, caring first for those nearby and giving priority to those close to you in real conflicts are things any reasonable ethics has to accommodate.

But partiality only authorizes preferring when we can't help everyone. It has never authorized harming those outside the circle for lesser gains. Animals may not be part of our family, and that doesn't give us the right to treat them as food.
