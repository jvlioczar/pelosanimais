# K10. "Vegans will always be a minority"

**Thesis:** being a minority doesn't make a position wrong, and the number of people who agree with something doesn't decide whether it's just. Many moral changes began with minorities that seemed to have no chance. And you don't need to be a majority to reduce suffering: each person who stops buying animal products reduces demand, and animal protection laws have been passed without a vegan majority. Calling a vegan world utopian confuses the feasibility of a total change with present duty: perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared, but that doesn't change what each person should do today, and every reduction in exploitation is worth it for its own sake.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You'll always be a minority. The world will never go vegan, so why bother?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegans are a small part of the population, and that has changed little over the decades.
2. **P2.** Social change only happens when the majority comes on board.
3. **P3.** It's unlikely the majority will become vegan in the near future.
4. **C.** So veganism won't change anything, and it isn't worth adopting.

P1 is true today almost everywhere. P3 is plausible. The problem lies in P2 and the conclusion: important changes happen without a majority, and the question of what's right doesn't depend on how many agree.

The argument is **invalid** as a moral objection: from a position being rare it doesn't follow that it's wrong or useless.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes only a few children in the class think it isn't right to leave a classmate out of a game. They're few, but they're right, and often the others end up changing their minds. Being few doesn't mean being wrong. And even if the others don't change, whoever doesn't leave the classmate out is already doing the right thing.

## 4. Short answer

Being a minority doesn't make something wrong, and being with the majority doesn't make something right. Many moral changes began with a few people who seemed to have no chance. And you don't need to be a majority to reduce suffering: each person who stops buying animal products lowers demand, and animal protection laws have been passed without a vegan majority. Perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared, and no one concludes from that that committing crimes doesn't matter: your duty doesn't depend on everyone fulfilling it. The animals don't need a majority for the question to apply to you: is it fair to make them suffer and die?

**Question to ask back:** "If something is unjust, does it stop being unjust because few people oppose it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes two questions:

| Question | Type | Depends on the number of supporters? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is it just to exploit animals? | Moral | No |
| Will veganism become the majority? | Prediction | Yes, by definition |

The second question is interesting, but it doesn't answer the first. When the objection takes the form "a vegan world is utopian", the confusion is the same: it mixes the feasibility of a total, immediate change with each person's duty today.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Popularity doesn't decide what's right.** That a position is held by few doesn't show it's wrong. It's the appeal to the majority (D2).

**2. Change begins with minorities.** Many moral and legal changes, such as ending practices once accepted by the majority, began with small groups and were treated as utopian before they happened. In experiments on social conventions, a committed minority of about 25% managed to change the group's norm. It's a laboratory result, not a prediction, but it shows norms can tip without a majority.

**3. You don't need a majority to reduce harm.** Each person who stops buying animal products reduces, on average, demand and the number of animals bred (K1). The effect exists even if veganism never becomes the majority.

**4. Laws change before the majority does.** The European Union banned battery cages for laying hens in a 1999 directive, and the United Kingdom recognized the sentience of several kinds of animals in law in 2022, without a vegan majority in either place.

**5. The prediction may be wrong.** Technologies such as cultivated meat and precision fermentation may make products free of exploitation cheaper and more common. There's no guarantee, but there's no guarantee of the opposite either (K11). And the change is possible in principle: a global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%.

**6. Every reduction is a legitimate step.** A perfect world isn't needed to justify each step. Crime has never disappeared, and no one concludes that it isn't worth refraining from it or trying to reduce it. Reducing road deaths, hunger or violence is worth it even though none of them will ever reach zero. Demanding the ideal world before recognizing any progress is the [nirvana fallacy](../falacias.html#nirvana-fallacy): discarding the best possible because it isn't perfect. Each animal spared is a real individual who no longer suffers and dies, not a fraction of a project that would only count if completed.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: without a majority, suffering stays almost the same

The most serious version says: even if each vegan reduces demand a little, as long as the majority eats meat, billions of animals will keep being killed. The individual effort would be almost irrelevant next to the scale.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Almost irrelevant isn't irrelevant.** For each animal that isn't bred, the difference is total.
2. **The argument works against any cause.** No individual ends hunger or poverty alone, and helping still makes sense (K1).
3. **The moral question remains.** Even if it were useless, taking part in something unjust would still be taking part in something unjust.
4. **Individual duty is attainable.** Whether distant goals frustrate or mobilize people can be debated, but that's a strategic question. Each person can stop paying for exploitation today, even if the world doesn't change.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if vegans were always a minority, the question would be the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die for the pleasure of taste? The number of people who agree doesn't change the answer, and the question about the world's future doesn't replace the question about the present.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to popularity and normality | Uses the number of supporters as a criterion of truth |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either veganism becomes the majority, or it changes nothing |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If the ideal world isn't reached, no step would be worth it |
| Epistemological | Prediction taken as certainty | Treats the future as already decided |
| Moral | Confusing effectiveness with justice | From the chance of winning it concludes what's right to do |
| Moral | Duty conditional on universal adherence | It would only make sense to act if everyone acted |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Admires those who championed unpopular causes in the past.** But uses unpopularity against veganism.
2. **Votes, donates or helps without changing everything alone.** And demands that veganism change everything at once.
3. **Recognizes that social norms change.** And assumes this one never will.
4. **Does the right thing on other matters, even if others don't.** Doesn't steal, although theft has never disappeared.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a position is a minority one, or if the goal will never be reached by everyone, it's wrong or useless".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unpopular causes of the past | Those who championed them as a minority would have been wrong | History judges otherwise in many cases |
| Donations | Giving would be useless, because most people don't give | Each donation helps someone |
| Any social change | None could ever begin | Every change starts with a few |
| Ending violence | No one would need to stop being violent, because crime has never disappeared | No one accepts this |
| Road safety | Reducing deaths wouldn't be worth it, because they'll never reach zero | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that vegans are a minority today, that an entirely vegan world seems distant, that deep cultural change is slow and uncertain and that the majority probably won't become vegan in the near future. It's also reasonable to think about strategies that reduce suffering even without mass adoption, such as laws, technologies and institutional change.

But being a minority doesn't decide what's right, and you don't need to be a majority to make a difference. Each animal that isn't bred to die is a real difference.
