# H15. "What matters is how food is grown, not whether it comes from animals"

**Thesis:** how food is produced really does matter: soil, pesticides, biodiversity and working conditions vary a great deal from one producer to another. But "how was it produced" and "what was produced" are two questions, and one doesn't replace the other. You can choose plants and also care about how they are grown. The data show that, for emissions and land use, the type of food weighs more than the production method. And neither question answers the third: what happens to the animal.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The deeper issue isn't cutting out animal foods. It's looking at how food is grown, what happens to the soil and what model of agriculture you're funding. I'd rather have meat from a good farmer than soy from a monoculture."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The production method determines decisive impacts: soil health, pesticide use, biodiversity, working conditions.
2. **P2.** Those impacts vary widely within the same type of food: there is well-produced meat and there are badly produced vegetables.
3. **P3.** If the production method is what weighs most, whether a food is of animal or plant origin is secondary.
4. **C.** So the focus should be the farming system we fund, not the exclusion of animal foods.

This is a typical argument from advocates of regenerative agriculture, and P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3. For emissions and land use, one of the largest surveys ever made on the subject found the opposite: the type of food weighs more than the production method. And for the animal, the origin of the food isn't a detail: it's the difference between living and being killed.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Looking after the land is very important, and there are better and worse ways of growing food. But these are two different questions: "was the land well cared for?" and "did any animal have to die for this food to exist?". You can answer both well at the same time: eat plants that were grown with care.

## 4. Short answer

I agree that how food is grown matters, a lot. But that isn't in competition with what we eat: I can choose plants and also choose how they are grown. When the two factors are compared, the type of food weighs more. In Poore and Nemecek's study, with data from almost 39,000 farms, the lowest-impact beef still emits about ten times more, per gram of protein, than the highest-impact peas. And even in the best system in the world, the animal is still killed.

**Question to ask back:** "If we can look after the soil and also not kill animals, why would I have to choose only one of the two?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument swaps one question for another, when the three below sit side by side:

| Question | What it is about | Does answering one answer the others? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How was the food produced? | Soil, pesticides, water, labor | No |
| What type of food is it? | Emissions and land needed per calorie or gram of protein | No |
| Was anyone killed to produce it? | What happens to the animal | No |

A good production system answers the first question well. The argument concludes from this that the other two have stopped mattering.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. These are two questions, and one doesn't replace the other.** Knowing that a food was well produced doesn't tell you how much land and how many emissions that type of food requires, and knowing that doesn't tell you whether anyone was killed. Those who care about the farming system are right to ask "how?". They aren't right to stop there.

**2. It's a false dichotomy.** The argument compares well-produced meat with badly produced vegetables, as if those were the only options. There is a combination it leaves out: well-grown plants. Agroecology, organic farming, agroforestry and so-called veganic farming (which does without manure and other animal inputs and even has its own certification) show that looking after the soil doesn't require raising animals (H9).

**3. In the data, the type of food weighs more.** The study by Joseph Poore and Thomas Nemecek, published in the journal *Science* in 2018, brought together data from about 38,700 farms. It confirms that the production method matters: impact can vary up to 50-fold among producers of the same product. But it concludes that the impacts of the lowest-impact animal products typically exceed those of vegetable substitutes. In Our World in Data's presentation of these data, beef from the 10% of producers with the lowest impact emits about 9 kg of CO₂ equivalent per 100 g of protein; peas from the 10% with the highest impact, about 0.8; tofu from the 10% with the highest impact, about 3.5.

**4. "Good" systems with animals usually use more land.** Free-ranging, pasture-raised animals grow more slowly and take up more area per kilo produced. The gain for the local soil comes with a cost in land that doesn't show in the photo of the farm (H3, H8).

**5. "The model you fund" includes what the animal eats.** Those who buy animal products also fund the crops that feed the animals, and most of the world's soy becomes feed (H1, H16). Eating plants directly is the simplest way of funding less cropland, not more.

**6. The question about the animal still stands.** Healthy soil, a beautiful farm and a dedicated farmer don't change the fate of those raised to be killed (B2, B5).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: in an integrated system, the animal is part of caring for the land

The most serious version says: on a small, diversified farm, hens and ruminants recycle nutrients, keep weeds down and cut the need to buy inputs. A monoculture vegetable, with pesticides and bare soil, can do more harm to that piece of land than an animal in such a system. The right criterion would be the system, not the category of food.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The comparison is crossed.** It puts the best system with animals next to the worst plant system. The fair comparison is best with best, or typical with typical (H16).
2. **Nutrient cycles close without animals.** Green manures, compost and rotation with legumes do that work; manure only returns part of what the animal ate (H9).
3. **This system feeds few people.** The report *Grazed and Confused*, coordinated at the University of Oxford, calculated that solely grass-fed animals supply about 1 gram of protein per person per day worldwide, against 32 grams from all animal sources and 49 grams from plants.
4. **The "integrated" animal is killed too.** Being part of caring for the land doesn't change how it ends.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Suppose a farm takes perfect care of the soil, the water and the people who work on it. Even so, the animals there are raised to die young. "How is the food grown?" is a good question, but it isn't the deepest one when the food was someone. For the animal, what matters is what happens to it, and that depends on whether or not the food comes from animals.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in H15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either you look at the production method, or you look at the origin of the food |
| Logical | Selective comparison | Compares the best meat with the worst vegetable |
| Logical | Question swap | Answers "how was it produced?" in place of "was anyone killed?" |
| Epistemological | Ignoring the order of magnitude | Treats variation between producers as greater than the difference between types of food |
| Moral | Excluding those who pay the price | Judges the system by the soil and the consumer, not by the animal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't always buy from the "good farmer".** In restaurants, at parties and in ready-made products, the meat almost never comes from the system being defended.
2. **Doesn't apply the criterion to plants.** If how food is grown is what matters, well-grown plants should be the obvious choice.
3. **Says diet doesn't matter, but defends one.** Putting grass-fed meat at the center of the plate is also a choice of diet.
4. **Looks at what it funds, but not at the feed.** The "good farmer's" hens and pigs also eat grain from crops.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what matters is how something is produced, not what is produced or who is harmed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An organic farm using child labor | There would be no problem, because the soil is well cared for | No one accepts this: the growing method doesn't erase the victim |
| Timber from old-growth forest cut "with careful management" | Felling would be the same as planting | What is produced counts too |
| Organic tobacco | It would be a good choice, because of how it is grown | The product still does harm |
| Well-grown plants | They would be the best option on both criteria | This is the conclusion the argument avoids |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets a lot right. The production method matters: the same data that show the advantage of plants show differences of up to 50-fold between producers of the same food. Degraded soil, excessive pesticide use and precarious labor are real problems, including on farms that supply vegans. And asking "what model of agriculture am I funding?" is better than looking only at the "vegan" label on a product.

But the concession ends where the argument uses a good question to erase the others. Caring about how food is grown and leaving animals off the plate are choices that add up. And however good the system is, it doesn't answer what matters most to the one inside it: the animal.
