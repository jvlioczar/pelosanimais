# L9. "I'll eat double to make up for you"

**Thesis:** it isn't an argument; it's an emotional reaction. Psychology calls it reactance: when people feel their freedom is threatened, they tend to do the opposite of what's asked. The phrase says nothing about the animals; it says something about the discomfort of whoever says it. And the one who pays the "make-up" isn't the vegan, it's the animal.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I'll eat some bacon in your honor. For every vegan, one more steak."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

There's no strong version. The phrase is a provocation, not an argument. It can express:

1. **Irritation** at feeling judged.
2. **Defense** of one's freedom of choice.
3. **Humor** to ease an uncomfortable conversation.

None of these readings offers a reason to eat animals.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes, when someone tells us not to do something, we feel like doing it even more, just to provoke them. But in this case, the one who gets hurt isn't the person who asked, it's the animals. Provoking doesn't change what's right.

## 4. Short answer

That isn't an argument; it's a provocation. Psychology calls it reactance: when we feel our freedom is threatened, we tend to do the opposite. But the one who pays the "make-up" isn't me; it's the animals. If the goal is to annoy me, there are ways that don't involve anyone's death. And whenever you want to talk about the animals for real, I'm here.

**Question to ask back:** "If it weren't to provoke me, what do you think of what happens to the animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The phrase has no argumentative structure:

| What the phrase expresses | What it says about the animals |
| --- | --- |
| Irritation | Nothing |
| Defense of freedom | Nothing |
| Humor | Nothing |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It isn't an argument.** There are no premises or conclusion about the moral question.

**2. It's reactance.** Feeling one's freedom threatened produces a tendency to do the opposite of what's suggested.

**3. The victim is the animal.** The provocation is aimed at the vegan, but the harm falls on the animals.

**4. It reveals discomfort.** Feeling the need to "make up" for it shows the question bothered the person, which is a starting point for conversation.

**5. Do-gooder derogation explains part of the reaction.** Those who feel judged tend to react against the group that seems to judge them (L1).

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) | Meat eaters disparage vegetarians when they anticipate being judged by them | Helps explain the reaction |
| [Brehm, theory of psychological reactance (1966)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675534/) | Threats to freedom produce a motivation to restore it, often by doing the opposite | Academic Press; the link is to an open-access review by Steindl and colleagues (2015) |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: it's just a joke

The most serious version says the phrase is only humor, with no real intention to eat more meat, and that taking it seriously shows a lack of humor.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **If it's a joke, it isn't an argument.** And the moral question is still unanswered.
2. **Humor can close the conversation.** Often, that's exactly what it's used for.
3. **The joke has a real target.** The animals.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The provocation is a way of not talking. But the question remains: setting aside irritation with vegans, is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the choice is personal.** And makes it a response to someone else.
2. **Wouldn't eat more meat to annoy other groups.** Only vegans.
3. **Reveals the question bothers them.** By feeling the need to make up for it.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L9 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Psychological reactance | Feeling one's freedom threatened produces the opposite behavior | General literature on reactance |
| Do-gooder derogation | Irritation with those who act morally | [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone asks me not to do something, I may do more of it to provoke them".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environmentalists | Polluting more to provoke them would be acceptable | No one seriously defends this |
| Anti-smoking activists | Smoking more to provoke them | Harms oneself |
| Vegans | Eating more meat to provoke them | Harms the animals |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The phrase reveals something real: many people feel pressured or judged when the subject is meat, and react with humor or provocation. Animal advocates should understand this reaction and avoid feeding it.

But provocation isn't an answer. And when the irritation passes, the question about the animals is still there, waiting.
