# L9. "Hitler was a vegetarian"

**Thesis:** it's the reductio ad Hitlerum: trying to discredit an idea by associating it with a hated figure. It's a poorly built association, meant to discredit, and it proves nothing: Hitler also painted, and that doesn't make artists bad people; he didn't smoke, and that doesn't make cigarettes good. And the historical question is more complicated than the slogan: he adopted a meat-free diet inconsistently, from the 1930s and mostly for health reasons, there are accounts of him eating meat at various times before the war, and he certainly wasn't vegan, since he ate eggs and dairy. The Nazi regime, in fact, pressured German vegetarian associations, and the main one dissolved in 1935.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Hitler loved animals. The Nazis passed animal protection laws."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Hitler and the Nazi regime expressed concern for animals.
2. **P2.** That same regime committed terrible crimes against humans.
3. **P3.** So concern for animals can coexist with contempt for humans, and isn't a sign of virtue.
4. **C.** So veganism has no moral superiority.

P1 and P2 have a historical basis, with debates. P3 is a reasonable observation: concern for animals doesn't guarantee virtue. The problem is that none of this says whether it's right to eat animals.

The argument is **valid** to show that no one is good just for caring about animals. It fails as an objection to veganism.

## 3. Answer for children

Some very bad people did ordinary things, like drinking water, painting pictures or liking dogs. That doesn't make those things bad. What matters is whether an idea is right, not who else thought something similar. Hurting animals without needing to doesn't become right just because a bad person also didn't eat meat.

## 4. Short answer

Even if Hitler had been vegetarian, that would say nothing about veganism. Hitler also painted, and that doesn't make artists bad people. It's the so-called reductio ad Hitlerum: associating an idea with a hated figure to avoid discussing it. And the history is more complicated: he gave up meat inconsistently, mostly for health reasons, there are accounts of him eating meat at various times, and he certainly wasn't vegan, since he ate eggs and dairy. What matters is whether the arguments about the animals are good.

**Question to ask back:** "Hitler didn't smoke. Does that make cigarettes a good choice?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument tries to transfer a person's reputation to an idea:

| What the argument does | Why it doesn't work |
| --- | --- |
| Associates veganism with Hitler | An idea doesn't inherit the character of those who practice it |
| Picks one trait among many | Hitler also painted, and no one concludes anything about artists |
| Says "vegetarian" and concludes about vegans | Hitler ate eggs and dairy: he wasn't vegan |
| Uses disputed historical information | Even if true, it would be irrelevant |
| Suggests concern for animals is suspect | Many animal advocates have also been human rights advocates |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the reductio ad Hitlerum.** Associating an idea with a hated figure doesn't show it's wrong.

**2. It's guilt by association.** An idea doesn't become wrong because of who practices it (J22). It's a poorly built association, meant to discredit ([guilt by association](../falacias.html#guilt-by-association)).

**3. It proves nothing, because it proves too much.** Hitler also painted: he twice tried to get into the Academy of Fine Arts in Vienna (1907 and 1908) and, for a few years, lived by selling watercolors and paintings of the city's buildings. No one concludes from this that painting is bad or that artists are bad people. Picking, from a hated person's many habits, precisely the one you want to discredit says nothing about that habit.

**4. The historical question is more complicated than the slogan.** Accounts of Hitler's diet before the Second World War are inconsistent, and several mention meat, such as sausages and liver dumplings. He began avoiding meat inconsistently from the 1930s, mostly because of digestive and health problems; in the last years of the war, accounts point to a meat-free diet, and the image of an austere vegetarian leader was also cultivated by propaganda. Historians still debate whether "vegetarian" accurately describes his diet over his lifetime.

**5. He certainly wasn't vegan.** Even in his meat-free periods, Hitler ate eggs and dairy, such as cottage cheese, and wore leather. The word "vegan" was only coined in 1944, when the Vegan Society was founded in the UK. The argument talks about a "vegetarian" and tries to conclude something about vegans.

**6. The regime was no friend of organized vegetarianism.** Germany's main vegetarian association, the Deutscher Vegetarierbund, had its magazine stopped in 1933 and dissolved in February 1935 under pressure from the regime, which accused vegetarian groups of promoting pacifism; several groups of the life-reform movement were banned or forced to dissolve. Accessible sources on the details are limited, but the overall picture is not that of a vegetarian regime.

**7. The Nazi regime used animals in experiments.** Its supposed concern for animals was selective and instrumental.

**8. Concern for animals doesn't require contempt for humans.** A small neuroimaging study even suggested the opposite, that vegans and vegetarians react more strongly to human suffering; it's modest evidence, but nothing links concern for animals to contempt for people (L21).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: concern for animals can be misanthropic

The most serious version says that in some cases concern for animals goes hand in hand with contempt for humans, and that the Nazi example shows this risk.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Any value can be distorted.** That doesn't make the value wrong.
2. **Most animal advocates also defend humans.** And empathy isn't zero-sum (L21).
3. **The question about the animals remains.** No historical figure answers it.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Associating veganism with Hitler is a way of avoiding the conversation. The moral question is the same, whoever ate or didn't eat meat: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Reductio ad Hitlerum | Associates the idea with a hated figure |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Judges the idea by who practiced it ([see fallacy](../falacias.html#guilt-by-association)) |
| Logical | Equivocation | Slides from "vegetarian" to "vegan", which aren't the same thing |
| Epistemological | Disputed historical claim | Presents as fact something that is debated |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't use the argument for other habits.** Hitler didn't smoke either, and no one defends cigarettes for that reason.
2. **Accepts today's animal protection laws.** Which exist in almost every country.
3. **Likes pets.** Like many people, including Hitler.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a hated figure practiced something, that practice is bad".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not smoking | It would be bad, because Hitler didn't smoke | Absurd |
| Liking dogs | It would be suspect, because Hitler had dogs | Absurd |
| Painting pictures | Artists would be bad people, because Hitler painted | Absurd |
| Building highways | It would be bad, because the Nazis built motorways | Absurd |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that concern for animals doesn't make anyone virtuous, and that violent regimes have used animal protection rhetoric selectively. No one should assume that someone who cares for animals is automatically good.

But that says nothing about whether it's right to eat animals. The question needs to be answered with arguments, not associations.
