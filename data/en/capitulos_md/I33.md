# I33. "Buying a pedigree dog or cat doesn't hurt anyone"

**Thesis:** commercial breeding of dogs and cats treats the animals as a product, and the mothers pay the highest price: breeding females spend their lives producing litter after litter, often in kennels, and are discarded when they stop producing; dogs rescued from these operations show much higher levels of fear. Puppies are chosen for their looks, in a status culture often based on aesthetics, and several breeds have been shaped in ways that harm their health: in bulldogs and French bulldogs, more than 80% of litters are born by caesarean section. A Royal Veterinary College study of more than 4,000 pugs found almost twice the odds of having a disorder in a year compared with other dogs, and 54 times the risk of obstructive airway syndrome. Meanwhile, shelters are full of animals waiting to be adopted.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I bought from a responsible breeder, with a pedigree. Adopting is lovely, but I wanted a purebred. What's the harm?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Responsible breeders take good care of their animals.
2. **P2.** Breeds have predictable temperament and size, which helps people choose the right animal.
3. **P3.** Whoever buys gives the animal a good home.
4. **C.** So buying a purebred animal doesn't hurt anyone.

P1 holds for some breeders. P2 holds in part. P3 is true for the animal bought, but it leaves out the breeding females, breed-related health problems and the animals waiting in shelters.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails by omission: it only looks at the animal that comes home.

## 3. Answer for children

Some dogs were bred to have very flat little faces because people find it cute. But that face makes it hard for them to breathe. And the mother dogs at breeders often spend their lives shut away, having puppy after puppy, until they're no longer useful for selling. A dog isn't worth more or less because of its breed or its looks. Meanwhile, many dogs and cats at shelters are waiting for a family.

## 4. Short answer

It does hurt. Commercial breeding treats dogs and cats as a product, and the mothers pay the highest price: breeding females spend their lives producing litters, often in kennels, and are discarded when they stop producing. When you choose by breed, you choose by looks, often for status, and several breeds have been shaped in ways that harm their health. A Royal Veterinary College study of more than 4,000 pugs found almost twice the odds of having a disorder in a year and 54 times the risk of serious breathing problems compared with other dogs. Buying keeps that demand going, while shelters are full of animals waiting to be adopted. Is a look worth the suffering of those mothers and those puppies?

**Question to ask back:** "If the animal is part of the family, why does it have to be chosen from a catalogue of breeds?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument only looks at the animal that comes home:

| Who is affected | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| The puppy bought | Usually gets a good home |
| The breeding female | Successive litters, difficult or caesarean births; in commercial breeding, a life in kennels and being discarded when she stops producing |
| Breeds with extreme traits | Health problems tied to the selected looks |
| The buyer | Chooses by looks, often for fashion and status |
| Shelter animals | Left without the home that went to the bought animal |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Selected looks come at a price.** The Royal Veterinary College study, using veterinary records of 4,308 pugs and 21,835 dogs of other breeds in the UK, found about 1.9 times the odds of having at least one recorded disorder in a year, and about 54 times the risk of brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome. The authors concluded that the pug can no longer be considered a typical dog from a health perspective.

**2. The breeding female is the invisible part, and the most cruelly treated.** In commercial breeding, especially in so-called puppy mills, females are typically bred every heat cycle, regardless of their health, often confined to cages or pens with little contact, exercise or care; when production drops, they are usually discarded, according to a report by veterinarians linked to animal protection. The buyer rarely sees them, because the puppy is handed over far from there. The marks remain: in a study of more than a thousand dogs that had been breeding stock in commercial operations in the United States and were later adopted, compared with pet dogs, the odds of very high levels of fear were several times greater, and compulsive behaviors, such as circling, and health problems were also more common. Even outside these operations, selection for looks weighs on the mothers: in a British survey, more than 80% of litters of bulldogs, French bulldogs and Boston terriers were born by caesarean section, because the puppies' broad heads and the mother's body make natural birth difficult.

**3. "Responsible breeder" is a variable label.** There are careful breeders, but there is no single standard, and the same demand that sustains the good ones sustains the bad ones.

**4. Judging an animal by breed is judging by looks.** A breed doesn't say whether a dog or cat deserves a home; it only says what it looks like. In a British study of 1,427 owners, appearance was the factor that weighed most in buying pugs, bulldogs and French bulldogs, and the breed's health weighed less than for owners of other breeds. Often, the breed works like a brand: fashion, social media and celebrities turn a muzzle shape into a status symbol, and the high price becomes part of the appeal. It's a culture that values the animal for what it displays, not for who it is.

**5. Buying competes with adopting.** Each animal bought is one home fewer for a shelter animal, and shelters have dogs and cats of every size and temperament, including purebreds. The numbers are large: in the United States, shelters took in more than 6.5 million dogs and cats in 2023, and about 690,000 were euthanized.

**6. The animal isn't a product.** Choosing by looks, as from a catalogue, follows the same reasoning as breeding animals for consumption: the animal exists to satisfy a preference.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: buying from an ethical breeder avoids the worst problems

The most serious version says that ethical breeders run health tests, limit the number of litters, avoid extreme traits and take the animal back if the owner can't keep it.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It improves breeding, but doesn't answer the shelters.** While animals wait to be adopted, breeding more to sell is still a choice.
2. **It doesn't reach breeds with extreme traits.** No health test fixes a face selected to be flat.
3. **It still treats the animal as a product.** The breeding happens because someone pays for a look.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question isn't whether the animal bought will be loved, but whether it's fair to breed animals to sell while others wait for a home, and to shape their bodies to the point of harming their health. Those who love animals can start at the shelter.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I33 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Omission | Considers only the animal bought |
| Epistemological | Trust in the label | "Pedigree" and "responsible breeder" are taken as a guarantee of health and welfare |
| Moral | Animal as product | The desired look outweighs health and the other lives involved |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the animal is family.** And chooses it by breed, like a product.
2. **Finds flat faces cute.** And wouldn't accept a trait that made a child's breathing hard.
3. **Loves dogs and cats.** And ignores the ones waiting at the shelter.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I33 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Baby schema | Infant-like traits, such as a short muzzle and big eyes, attract and hide the health cost | [Glocker et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1439-0310.2008.01603.x) |
| Motivated reasoning | Wanting the breed makes the breeding female and the shelter invisible | General literature on motivated cognition |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the animal bought is well cared for, breeding for sale doesn't hurt anyone".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Puppy mills | They would be acceptable if the buyer cared well | Criticized by vets and animal protectors |
| Breeds with traits that prevent normal breathing or birth | They would be acceptable | Some countries restrict breeding for these traits |
| Animals bred for consumption and treated well until slaughter | They would be acceptable | See B3 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that there are careful breeders, that the animal bought usually gets a good home, and that knowing an animal's temperament and size helps people choose well. It's also true that many shelters have rules that make adoption hard.

But none of these points answers the breeding females, the traits that harm health and the animals waiting at the shelter. Adopting solves all three at once.
