# N16. "Sentientists want to get rid of forests"

**Thesis:** it's largely a straw man, but not entirely. What the ethics of wild animal suffering proposes in practice is research (welfare biology), help in cases where we already know how to act (vaccination, rescue in disasters, contraception in place of culling) and caution. Destroying a habitat causes enormous, immediate suffering to those who live in it. But there is a real, contested position that the accusation distorts: some authors in the field, such as Luciano Carlos Cunha, Oscar Horta and Brian Tomasik, argue for *not expanding or recreating* natural areas (reforesting open land, rewilding), because that would multiply the births of animals who, for the most part, die soon after being born. Not expanding isn't the same as destroying what exists, and Cunha doesn't propose destroying. At least one author, Tomasik, went further and argued that habitat loss generally reduces suffering. These theses are debated in the field and contested by conservationists, by climate policy and by those who doubt that these lives are bad on balance. Uncertainty is precisely the reason not to do anything irreversible.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You want to pave over nature to end suffering. That's ecocide with good intentions."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Sentientists claim that, in nature, most animals die shortly after being born, and that suffering therefore probably outweighs wellbeing.
2. **P2.** If the goal is to reduce suffering, and wild life contains more suffering than wellbeing, the most direct route is less wild life.
3. **P3.** There are authors in this field who have drawn exactly that conclusion.
4. **C.** So sentientism, taken seriously, calls for the end of forests and ecosystems.

P1 describes a real thesis, defended among others by the Spanish philosopher Oscar Horta, and also a contested one: Zach Groff and Yew-Kwang Ng (2019) corrected an error in Ng's 1995 model and concluded that the balance is uncertain, and Heather Browning and Walter Veit (2023) argue that wild lives may be far more positive than is assumed. P3 is partly true: one author argued for habitat loss, and a few others argue for not expanding natural areas, which is different from destroying them. The problem lies in P2, which jumps from the diagnosis to the most drastic remedy, and in the conclusion, which attributes to the whole field what one essay argued.

The argument is **invalid**: from "there is a lot of suffering in a place" it doesn't follow that "the place should be destroyed".

## 3. Answer for children

When we find out that lots of people in a neighborhood are sick, no one thinks of knocking the neighborhood down. We send doctors, medicine and vaccines, and we study why people are getting sick. It's the same with the animals in the forest. Knowing that many of them suffer is a reason to study and to help, not to knock down their home.

## 4. Short answer

I understand where that picture comes from, but it distorts what is being proposed. The organizations that work on this don't propose cutting down any forest. The real proposals are to study how wild animals live, to vaccinate, to rescue in floods and fires, to use contraception instead of killing. Destroying a habitat kills and injures millions of animals on the spot. There is philosophical discussion about how much suffering there is in nature, but recognizing a problem isn't wanting to blow up the place where it happens. Some authors argue against creating new natural areas, such as new forests, and that thesis is contested; but not planting isn't cutting down.

**Question to ask back:** "When someone says there's a lot of suffering in a poor region, do you take them to mean they want to destroy the region, or to help the people who live there?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes three kinds of claim:

| Kind | Example | Who holds it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diagnosis | There is a great deal of suffering in nature, and it matters | The whole field |
| Theoretical question | In certain habitats, does suffering outweigh wellbeing? | Some philosophers, with different answers |
| Practical proposal | Research, help where we already know how, act with caution | The organizations and researchers in the area |
| Contested proposal | Don't expand or recreate natural areas (without destroying existing ones) | Cunha (2022), Horta and Tomasik; contested by conservationists, by climate policy and by part of the field itself |

"Getting rid of forests" isn't in any of these rows as a proposal. The argument gets there by attaching to the second row a remedy the field doesn't prescribe, or by reading the fourth ("don't plant new forests") as if it were "cut down the existing ones".

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The real proposals are different.** The organization Animal Ethics collects the forms of help already practiced: rescue in disasters, treatment of the injured, care of orphans and vaccination. Oral vaccination of foxes against rabies helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe (N12). Wild Animal Initiative says "we focus on generating scientific research" and names, as a promising area, more humane ways of controlling populations (contraception in place of culling is the most discussed example). Neither proposes eliminating habitats.

**2. Destroying habitats causes immense, immediate suffering.** Fire, starvation, dehydration and displacement are terrible ways to die. WWF estimated that nearly 3 billion animals were killed or displaced by the Australian bushfires of 2019 and 2020. Anyone concerned with the suffering of wild animals has there a direct reason against destruction.

**3. A theoretical question isn't a practical proposal.** In 2010 Oscar Horta argued that, since most species produce far more young than survive, suffering probably prevails in nature. His conclusion was that intervening to help animals may be justified, not destroying where they live. In 2016 the American essayist Brian Tomasik went further: he wrote that habitat loss in general reduces suffering and should be "encouraged rather than opposed". It's honest to acknowledge that this text exists. But the rest needs saying: it is a reply to Yew-Kwang Ng, the founder of welfare biology, who argued for environmental preservation as a measure in animals' favor; Tomasik himself admits that burning or paving an area "usually causes severe short-run suffering", lists exceptions and says that evaluating any policy "requires detailed analysis"; and the thesis isn't the program of any organization in the field.

**4. Uncertainty calls for caution.** No one knows how to measure reliably the wellbeing of most wild animals, or how to predict the effects of large changes in an ecosystem. The philosophers Nicolas Delon and Duncan Purves go so far as to argue that, today, we have no way of predicting whether broad interventions would reduce or increase suffering. Faced with an irreversible action and deep uncertainty, the reasonable course is not to act that way and to study first. That is why the field's central proposal is research (N9).

**5. The human test.** There are regions of the world with hunger, epidemics and very high infant mortality. Saying so isn't proposing to destroy those regions or to prevent people from being born there. The response we give is aid, vaccines, sanitation and research. Applying a different logic to animals (either accept the suffering or destroy everything) creates for them a dilemma we would never accept for humans (N8).

**6. The ones cutting down forests today are another industry.** Cattle pasture was responsible for about 41% of tropical deforestation between 2005 and 2013 (H1). Sentientists are among those who most oppose that industry. The accusation points the wrong way.

**7. The philosopher David Pearce proposes the opposite of destroying nature.** Pearce argues that life in forests, jungles and oceans is dominated by predation, hunger, disease and painful deaths of billions of animals, and that the romantic idea that "wild nature is perfect" ignores the enormous amount of physical and psychological pain they suffer there. The way out he imagines is not to wipe out habitats: it is that the Earth could keep recognizable forms of nature, including large animals, in reserves and parks managed with technology to eliminate cruelty, predation and agony. In *Reprogramming Predators* (2009), he argues that a biosphere without suffering is technically feasible, and discusses means such as immunocontraception and genetic engineering. These are speculative and controversial ideas, and worth discussing, but they show that the aim is to preserve the nature we know with less pain, not to tear it down.

**8. There is, however, a real thesis about not expanding nature, and it needs to be stated.** In the book *Razões para ajudar* (Reasons to Help, 2022, section 8.2, pp. 236–243), the philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha, whom this guide cites in other chapters, argues that natural areas should not be expanded or recreated, for example by reforesting open land or restoring desertified areas. The argument runs like this: where vegetation appears, populations of animals appear who have many young, almost all of whom die early; creating that environment means creating the conditions for an immense number of short, painful lives. He draws on Horta (2010) and Tomasik (2015). Cunha presents this as a form of prevention that requires no intervening, only refraining from certain interventions (p. 237). Hiding this position would leave this chapter open to the charge of omitting what authors in the field itself write.

**9. Not expanding isn't destroying.** Cunha doesn't propose cutting down existing forests. For the good of the animals already living in them, he says, it may be enough not to destroy what remains (p. 238). But he also leaves a point open: in a note (n. 137, p. 241), he says it is uncertain whether human interventions that destroy ecosystems have a negative balance for sentient beings, because they also prevent the birth of animals who would suffer; and he adds that this doesn't mean approving them, since there may be better options. Later on (pp. 278–279), he gives the example of buildings put up in green areas, which kill animals but reduce births, and says this doesn't justify that kind of killing, only shows that leaving everything as it is also has a cost. These are sensitive passages, which don't license the caricature, but which an honest reader should know about. The distinction that remains is clear: not creating new areas is one thing; destroying existing ones, with the immediate suffering described in point 2, is another.

**10. The climate objection and Cunha's reply.** Reforestation and ecosystem restoration are among the climate mitigation measures assessed by the IPCC, and studies such as Hayek and colleagues (2021) estimate that letting native vegetation return on land freed from livestock farming would remove a great deal of carbon from the atmosphere. Cunha replies (pp. 241–243) that there are other means against climate change, such as energy efficiency and renewable energy, that different kinds of vegetation affect animals differently and the least harmful ones should be chosen, and that a climate reason would justify, at most, the expansion needed for that purpose. It's a real disagreement, and climate policy today runs the opposite way from his view.

**11. Cunha himself asks for caution in conversation.** He acknowledges that the thesis sounds absurd to someone who has never heard of wild animal suffering, and says it's not a good idea to open the subject with it (p. 241). That is another reason why "they want to get rid of forests" distorts: it takes some authors' most controversial point, swaps "not expanding" for "destroying" and presents it as the program of the whole field.

**12. Who contests the thesis.** Conservationists and climate policy, which see ecosystem restoration as a good. Part of the field itself: Wild Animal Initiative focuses on research and treats as uncertain the question of which populations have net-negative lives, which calls for study before any policy on population size. Those who doubt the assumption that these lives are bad on balance: Groff and Ng (2019), who corrected Ng's 1995 model and concluded that the balance is uncertain, and Browning and Veit (2023), who argue that welfare in the wild may be far more positive than is assumed and that this calls for even more caution before intervening. And sentientists who hold rights theories, for whom displacing or killing individuals is wrong even if it improves some balance (E7). The non-expansion thesis depends heavily on a suffering-focused ethics, like Tomasik's, and on empirical premises that are still open.

**13. The best-known sentientist wrote in defense of old-growth forests.** In *Practical Ethics* (ch. 10), Peter Singer criticizes cutting down or flooding old-growth forests for short-term gains: the loss is permanent, falls on all future generations, and no invested income can buy back the link with the past that a forest represents. He also counts as a cost the deaths by drowning or starvation of the animals living in a flooded area. In the same passage, however, he touches on the question this chapter discusses: he asks whether the lives of animals in the wild contain more pleasure than pain, and grants that at that point the idea of calculating benefits becomes almost absurd, without that permitting the loss to be ignored. Doubt about the balance of wild lives exists within sentientism (points 8 to 12), but Singer's conclusion was in favor of preservation.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the logic leads there

The most serious version says: it doesn't matter what the organizations propose today. An ethics should be judged by what it implies. If all that counts is the balance of suffering, and nature's balance is negative, then less nature is better, and one day someone with power and technology will act accordingly. Tomasik's essay would show where the idea points.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The implication depends on premises no one knows to be true.** We would have to know that most wild animals' lives are bad on balance, that there is no better way to help them and that destruction wouldn't cause even more harm. None of the three is established.
2. **Sentientism isn't a single theory.** It says who matters, not how to calculate. There are sentientists who defend rights, and for them killing and displacing individuals is wrong even if the balance improves (E7). For those who think in terms of balance, the suffering caused by destruction goes into the sum.
3. **The same reasoning would work against any ethics that cares about suffering.** Anyone who wants to reduce human suffering could also, "by the logic", be accused of wanting the end of humanity. No one accepts that caricature, because there is another path: helping. That path exists for animals too (N12).
4. **Where the counter-reply is right, it is right about a specific position.** The logic of the balance leads some authors, such as Cunha, Horta and Tomasik, to argue for not expanding natural areas, and leaves the balance of destruction open (points 8 and 9). That is a debatable thesis within the field, with critics inside and outside it (point 12), not the inevitable implication of taking sentience seriously.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Straw man | Swaps "research and help" for "destroy" |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either accept suffering in nature or get rid of nature |
| Logical | Slippery slope | From recognizing suffering it arrives, with no steps in between, at ecocide |
| Epistemological | Hasty generalization | Takes one author's essay as the position of the whole field |
| Logical | Equivocation | Treats "not expanding natural areas" as if it were "destroying existing ones" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the reasoning to humans.** Someone who denounces a region's misery isn't accused of wanting to destroy it.
2. **Worries about forests.** And often funds, on the plate, the main cause of tropical deforestation.
3. **Accuses of recklessness.** A field whose central proposal is to study before acting.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever says there is a lot of suffering in a place wants to destroy the place".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doctors working in an epidemic zone | They would be proposing to wipe out the region | No one concludes this |
| People who study infant mortality | They would be in favor of preventing births by force | Absurd: the answer is care and research |
| People who study wild animal suffering | They would be proposing to cut down the forests | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that the idea of reducing suffering in nature, if applied hastily, simplistically and with power to spare, could cause disasters. It's right that there is at least one well-known text that argues for welcoming habitat loss, and that sentientists shouldn't pretend it doesn't exist. It's also right that authors in the field, such as Cunha, Horta and Tomasik, argue for not expanding or recreating natural areas, and that Cunha leaves open the balance of destroying ecosystems: these are real, contested positions that deserve open debate. And it's right that the field needs to say clearly what it proposes and what it doesn't, because the subject is frightening.

But the limit lies between discussing and proposing. Asking how much suffering there is in nature is a legitimate question, and those who take it seriously have been the first to call for research, humility and small, reversible actions. Standing forests shelter beings who feel, and destroying them would make those beings suffer now, for certain, in exchange for a benefit no one knows would exist.
