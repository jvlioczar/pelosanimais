# J23. "What would we do with the animals if everyone went vegan?"

**Thesis:** no one goes vegan all at once across the whole world. Change is gradual, and with less demand, fewer animals are bred. Farmed animals exist in enormous numbers because they're bred for this (C12). Those who already exist could live in sanctuaries or until the natural end of their lives. And whole economies have already reorganized when an industry built on exploitation came to an end: whaling shrank until it was banned in most of the world, and, in a far graver case, human slavery, which was also an industry, was abolished without economies ceasing to exist. The problem isn't what to do with them; it's that we keep bringing them into the world to die.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Release the cows into the streets? They'd have to kill them all at once."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There are billions of farmed animals in the world.
2. **P2.** Many wouldn't survive if released into the wild.
3. **P3.** If everyone stopped eating meat, there'd be nothing to do with them.
4. **C.** So veganism is impractical or would lead to a catastrophe for the animals themselves.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: change would be gradual, and the animals' breeding is controlled by the farmers.

The argument is **valid** for an instantaneous change. Its **soundness** fails because that change doesn't happen.

## 3. Answer for children

People won't all stop eating meat on the same day. It happens little by little. When fewer people buy meat, farms breed fewer animals. The animals that already exist can live until they're old, some in sanctuaries, which are like homes for rescued animals. This has happened before: many cities used to be lit with whale oil, and when other kinds of light came along, that industry faded away, and life went on.

## 4. Short answer

That won't happen, because no one goes vegan all at once across the whole world: change is gradual. With less demand, farmers breed fewer animals, because breeding is controlled through insemination and hatcheries. The animals that exist can live out their lives, some in sanctuaries. There wouldn't be cows loose in your street; there'd be fewer cows being born to die. And whole industries have ended without catastrophe: whale oil was replaced by kerosene, and commercial whaling was later put under a moratorium, and even human slavery, which was also an industry, was abolished, and economies reorganized. Really, the question isn't what to do with them, but why keep bringing them into the world only to die.

**Question to ask back:** "If hatcheries incubate eggs according to demand, what happens when demand falls?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument imagines a scenario that doesn't happen:

| How the argument imagines it | How change happens |
| --- | --- |
| Everyone stops eating meat at once | Consumption falls gradually |
| Billions of animals are left with nowhere to go | Fewer animals are bred each year |
| The animals would have to be released or killed | Existing ones live out their lives; new ones stop being born |
| A whole industry can't end | Industries built on exploitation have ended, and economies reorganized |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Change is gradual.** Meat consumption shifts over years and generations.

**2. Breeding is controlled.** Chickens hatch in hatcheries and cattle and pigs are born through insemination, in numbers set by demand (C12).

**3. The existing animals can live.** In sanctuaries, on small holdings or to the end of their cycle, while breeding declines.

**4. The question reverses the responsibility.** The problem isn't what to do with the animals if we stop eating them; it's that we keep breeding billions of them to die.

**5. Selectively bred breeds may decline.** Many production breeds have health problems caused by breeding (B8). Breeding fewer of them is no loss to the animals.

**6. Whole industries have ended, and economies reorganized.** Whaling is a close example. In the 19th century, whale oil lit homes and cities, and the whaling fleet of New Bedford, in the United States, reached 329 ships in 1856. After the first commercial oil well was drilled in 1859, kerosene became cheaper, and the whaling industry gradually became obsolete, along with its investments, its know-how and its workers. In the 20th century, the International Whaling Commission adopted a moratorium on commercial whaling in 1982, in force since 1986, and Brazil banned the hunting of cetaceans in 1987. A few countries still hunt whales, but the industry that once lit the world disappeared without economic catastrophe.

**7. Even human slavery, which was also an industry, was abolished.** For centuries, the labor of enslaved people sustained the sugar plantations of the British Caribbean and the sugar and coffee plantations of Brazil. Britain abolished slavery in most of its colonies in 1833, and Brazil did so in 1888, with the Golden Law. The economies went through crises and changes, but reorganized around free labor. The comparison is limited and holds for one point only: the economic transition of a whole industry is possible. It doesn't equate the victims or diminish what was specific about slavery. And it also shows what not to do: Britain paid 20 million pounds to the slave-owners, about 40% of public spending in 1833, and forced the freed people into years of unpaid "apprenticeship", while Brazil's Golden Law, just two articles long, gave the freed people no land, no education and no support at all, with consequences that last to this day. A just transition takes care of those who were exploited, not only of those who exploited them.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some breeds would disappear

The most serious version points out that, with the end of farming, many domestic breeds could disappear, and that this would be a loss of diversity.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Breeds aren't individuals.** What matters morally is the animals who feel, not the continuity of a breed (A10).
2. **Some could continue in sanctuaries.** Without being bred to die.
3. **Many breeds suffer because of selection.** Chickens who can barely walk lose nothing by not being born.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question about the animals' fate is legitimate, but the answer is simple: stop breeding them for slaughter. History shows that whole industries built on exploitation can end, and that what matters is making the transition with care for the victims. The moral problem isn't what to do with them afterwards; it's continuing to bring them into the world to die.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J23 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epistemological | Unrealistic scenario | Imagines an instantaneous change |
| Logical | Reversing responsibility | Treats a consequence of farming as an argument for continuing to farm |
| Moral | Species in place of the individual | Worries about the breed, not about who is born to die |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Knows production follows demand.** For any other product.
2. **Doesn't worry about the animals' fate today.** Which is slaughter.
3. **Doesn't think we must preserve other breeds at any cost.** Many domestic breeds have already disappeared.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if we stop using something, we'd have to destroy what already exists".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pedigree dogs | Ending breeding would require killing existing dogs | No one concludes this |
| Cart horses | Banning carts would require killing the horses | Cities set up adoption programs |
| Whaling | The end of the whaling industry would have ruined economies | The economy reorganized around oil |
| Farmed animals | Ending consumption would require killing them | Breeding simply declines |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The question about the fate of existing animals is legitimate, and a responsible transition needs to answer it, with sanctuaries, public policy and care for the animals already alive.

But the scenario of billions of animals set loose doesn't happen, because change is gradual and breeding is controlled. The best way to care for farmed animals is to stop breeding them to die.
