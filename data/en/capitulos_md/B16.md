# B16. "It's a fair trade: we care for them and feed them, and they give us meat"

**Thesis:** the idea of an agreement between humans and farmed animals, defended in books such as Stephen Budiansky's *The Covenant of the Wild* (1992), assumes a trade. But a trade requires that both parties can accept or refuse, and the animal can't. The same side that "protects" is the one that kills, and the price charged is life itself, in a fraction of the time the animal could have lived. The fact that the species multiplied through domestication doesn't benefit each individual born to be slaughtered. And the most serious academic version, J. Baird Callicott's "domestication contract", condemns intensive farming, where almost all meat comes from.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "We give them food, shelter, protect them from predators and care for them when they're sick. In return, they give us meat. It's a fair deal, and nature is much worse."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Farmed animals receive food, shelter, protection from predators and veterinary care.
2. **P2.** In the wild, they would suffer from hunger, disease and predators.
3. **P3.** A relationship in which each side gives something and receives something is a fair trade.
4. **C.** So breeding animals and killing them for food is a fair trade.

P1 is partly true, and depends a lot on the farming system. P2 compares with an alternative that isn't on the table: these animals wouldn't live in the wild, they simply wouldn't be bred. P3 is false when one side can neither consent nor refuse.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

A trade is when both sides agree, like swapping stickers. The cow didn't agree to anything: nobody asked her, and she can't say no. And in the end, the one who cared for her is the one who takes her life. That isn't a trade, it's a decision made by only one side.

## 4. Short answer

A fair trade requires that both sides can accept or refuse, and the animal can't. The one who "protects" is the same one who kills, and the price is life itself, charged long before its natural end. The species having multiplied through domestication doesn't help the individual born to be slaughtered. And the comparison with nature doesn't work: without farming, these animals wouldn't be out there suffering; they wouldn't have been bred.

**Question to ask back:** "If it were a fair trade, could the animal say no?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument calls a trade | Who decides | What the animal gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food, shelter, protection | Only the farmer | Their whole life, as well as freedom of movement and company |
| Veterinary care | Only the farmer, according to economic value | Their body, when slaughter pays more than further care |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. A trade requires consent.** An agreement in which only one side sets the terms, and the other can neither accept nor refuse, isn't a trade. Between humans, we'd call it an imposition.

**2. The protector is the predator.** The argument describes the farmer as the one who protects the animal, but the farmer is the one who kills them. Protecting someone in order to kill them later isn't protection; it's breeding for use.

**3. The price is disproportionate.** What the animal "pays" is their life, usually long before its natural end. No care received beforehand makes up for losing everything that would have come after.

**4. The species isn't the individual.** Domestication multiplied the numbers of cattle, pigs and chickens, but that doesn't benefit each animal who is born. It is the individual who feels, not the species (see A10).

**5. The alternative isn't the wild.** Without farming, these animals wouldn't be roaming free and suffering from predators; they simply wouldn't be bred (see B7).

**6. If there's a trade, animals contribute, and then they count.** A common objection to justice for animals, tied to John Rawls's idea of reciprocity, says they don't contribute to society and so aren't entitled to what it distributes. The fair-trade argument concedes the opposite: animals give meat, milk, eggs, wool and labor. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane notes that animals on farms, in laboratories and in homes are part of how society functions, and that by the contribution criterion they should be included in justice (Cochrane, 2010, p. 56). Whoever accepts the trade can't also say they give nothing (see A22 and A26).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the domestication contract

The most serious version says that at the start of domestication, some animals approached humans of their own accord, because they gained food and protection, and that this shows a kind of consent.

The environmental philosopher J. Baird Callicott gave this idea an academic form: by bringing animals into a "mixed community" with humans, we would have entered an implicit contract with them. Under the contract, we may use and even slaughter them, provided we give them proper pastoral care. Callicott himself concludes that intensive farming breaches this contract and is therefore wrong.

The journalist Michael Pollan gave the idea its most widely read version, in a *New York Times Magazine* essay that replies directly to Peter Singer ("An Animal's Place", 2002), taken up again in *The Omnivore's Dilemma* (2006). Pollan says domestication was an evolutionary process, not a political one: some species discovered, by Darwinian trial and error, that they prospered more in alliance with humans, and calling it domination would project a human idea of power onto what is in fact mutualism between species. Cattle, pigs and chickens multiplied, while their wild relatives declined. For Pollan, a species can have interests, and a domesticated animal's good lies in expressing its own nature, which, for these species, doesn't happen apart from humans. He grants that industrial farming is an evil and that, faced with it, vegetarianism is a reasonable response; but he concludes that the wrong lies in the practice, not the principle, and points, as a model, to farms such as Joel Salatin's Polyface, in Virginia, where each species lives according to its own nature.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Approaching food isn't agreeing to be killed.** An animal scavenging for scraps isn't agreeing to the slaughter of their descendants thousands of years later.
2. **Consent isn't passed down the generations.** Even if an ancestor had chosen something, that doesn't hold for the animal alive today.
3. **Today's domestication is imposed.** Controlled breeding, genetic selection and slaughter aren't the animals' choices.
4. **It's hard to imagine an animal signing this contract.** The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane asks whether any animal would accept a few months of free-range life followed by slaughter, and notes that the benefits of traditional pastoral care are often overstated (Cochrane, 2010, p. 84).
5. **The close relationship the contract assumes doesn't exist.** As the philosopher John Hadley (2007) points out, animals on factory farms and in laboratories live out of sight. Most people only come into contact with them once they're on the plate, not as members of a community they share (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 84–85).
6. **By Callicott's own standard, almost all meat is condemned.** If intensive farming breaches the contract, and that's where most meat, milk and eggs come from, the contract doesn't defend ordinary consumption.
7. **Pollan's mutualism is between species, and the one who dies is the individual.** That the species has multiplied doesn't benefit the animal slaughtered young (point 4 of (b) and A10). Pollan argues that species have interests, but the one who feels fear, pain and pleasure is the individual, and it's their life that is taken.
8. **An evolutionary process isn't an agreement.** If domestication was "evolutionary, not political", as Pollan says, then no one consented to anything: describing the result as a fair trade lends selection the language of a contract.
9. **Pollan himself condemns almost all the meat people eat.** He grants that industrial farming is an evil, and that's where most meat, milk and eggs come from (B4). And he proposes that slaughterhouses have glass walls, believing that many practices would disappear if the public could see them, which suggests that much of the practice depends on not being seen.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Talks about protecting the animal.** And is the one who kills them.
2. **Talks about a deal.** And only one side sets the terms.
3. **Says they care for the animal's health.** And the care lasts only while the animal is worth more alive.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I support and protect someone, I can take their life in return".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pet dogs and cats | It would be fair to eat them | Almost no one accepts that |
| Humans under guardianship | It would be fair to exploit them | No one accepts that |
| Caring without killing | It's what sanctuaries do | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that many farmers feed and care for their animals, that life in the wild involves a lot of suffering, and that domestication multiplied these species.

But a trade requires that both sides can refuse, and the price charged is the animal's life. Caring for someone doesn't give the right to kill them.
