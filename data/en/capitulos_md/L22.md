# L22. "Comparing animal farming to slavery or the Holocaust is offensive"

**Thesis:** there are good reasons on both sides. For the comparison: many situations imposed on animals are, descriptively, similar to those humans suffered under slavery and in the Holocaust (commodification, confinement, forced labour, violence, separation of families, mass killing), and Jewish writers and Holocaust survivors have made the comparison themselves. Those who reject it on principle often start from a speciesist premise: that bringing humans and animals together always degrades the humans. Against using the comparison: it can hurt descendants and survivors, risks instrumentalizing human suffering, and can drive people away from the cause. Structural analogy isn't moral equivalence, and the comparison can be valid; how, where and by whom it's made matters. And the case for animals doesn't depend on it.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You trivialize the Holocaust. That comparison is racist."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Slavery and the Holocaust were atrocities against human beings, whose memory must be respected.
2. **P2.** Comparing these atrocities with the treatment of animals can suggest the human victims are worth no more than animals.
3. **P3.** That suggestion offends descendants and survivors, and reproduces the historical dehumanization they suffered.
4. **C.** So these comparisons are offensive and should be rejected.

P1 is true. P2 and P3 point to a real risk: in many situations, these comparisons hurt. But P2 only works as an objection of principle if comparing with animals is, in itself, degrading; and that assumes animals don't matter morally, which is precisely what's at issue. The bigger problem is concluding from this that the question of animals can be dismissed.

The argument is **valid** as a criticism of a way of communicating. It fails when used to reject the moral question about animals.

## 3. Answer for children

Some very sad things happened to people in history, and it's important to remember them with respect. Some people notice that things done to animals today, like locking them up, separating mothers from their babies and killing many at once, look like things that were once done to people. Others are hurt by that comparison. So whoever makes it needs to be very careful and respectful. And animals deserve care for their own sake, with or without a comparison.

## 4. Short answer

You're right to ask for care: these comparisons can hurt. But pointing out similarities of mechanism, such as turning sentient beings into commodities, confining them, separating families and killing them en masse, isn't saying the victims are worth the same. Jewish writers and Holocaust survivors have made comparisons of this kind themselves. And refusing every comparison usually assumes that bringing humans and animals together is always degrading, which only holds if animals don't matter. The structural comparison can be valid; the manner, the setting and respect for the affected communities are what decide whether it helps or hurts. But the defense of animals needs no analogy at all: confining and killing those who feel, without need, is wrong in itself.

**Question to ask back:** "Setting the comparisons aside, what do you think of what happens to the animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes a legitimate criticism of form with a rejection of content, and hides a premise:

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can these comparisons hurt? | Yes, and they should be used with care, and sometimes avoided |
| Are the situations imposed on animals descriptively similar? | In several mechanisms, yes; there are also important differences |
| Is comparing mechanisms equating victims? | Not necessarily: analogy isn't equivalence |
| Is rejecting every comparison a neutral position? | It often assumes that bringing humans and animals together is degrading, a speciesist premise |
| Does the question of animals depend on these comparisons? | No |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Analogy isn't equivalence.** Comparing structures, such as objectification, property and the denial of interests, isn't claiming the victims are worth the same or that the suffering is equal.

**2. Refusal on principle usually starts from a speciesist premise.** Saying the comparison offends because it "equates people with animals" assumes that being placed beside an animal is, in itself, a degradation. That's only true if animals don't matter morally, which is exactly what the comparison questions. Someone who accepts that a cow's suffering counts doesn't see the comparison as an insult to the human victims, but as the denunciation of the same kind of treatment.

**3. The descriptive similarities exist.** Defenders of the comparison point to recurring mechanisms: sentient beings turned into property and commodities; freedom restricted to a minimum; forced labour and reproduction; routine violence; young separated from their mothers, like calves in dairy production; and killing on an industrial scale, justified by the idea that the victims are worth less. There are also differences, which critics rightly point out: the Holocaust sought to exterminate a people out of ideological hatred, and Black slavery created a racism whose consequences still structure societies today; animal farming kills in order to use, not out of hatred. An honest comparison shows both.

**4. Jewish writers and survivors have made the comparison.** The Jewish writer Isaac Bashevis Singer, born in Poland and winner of the 1978 Nobel Prize in Literature, wrote in the story "The Letter Writer" (1968) that for animals the world is an "eternal Treblinka", a phrase that gave its title to Charles Patterson's book *Eternal Treblinka* (2002). Alex Hershaft, a survivor of the Warsaw ghetto, founded the Farm Animal Rights Movement and publicly compares the treatment of farmed animals to the Holocaust, though he says he doesn't equate the two. On slavery, Marjorie Spiegel discussed the comparison in *The Dreaded Comparison* (1988). This doesn't oblige anyone to make it, nor does it silence descendants who feel offended, but it shows the comparison isn't, in itself, disrespect for the victims.

**5. There are good reasons against using it, and they should be taken seriously.** In 2004, PETA's German branch planned the campaign "The Holocaust on your plate", with photos of concentration camp prisoners beside confined animals. At the request of leaders of the Central Council of Jews in Germany, German courts banned the posters as an affront to the victims' dignity, a decision upheld by the Federal Constitutional Court; in 2012, the European Court of Human Rights found that the ban didn't violate freedom of expression, taking German history into account. PETA itself, in the United States, apologized in 2005 for the pain caused, without withdrawing the comparison. Critics point to three risks: hurting descendants and survivors; using others' suffering as a campaign tool, without the consent of those who lived it; and, in the case of slavery, reopening a specific wound, because Black people were compared to animals for centuries to justify their oppression. There is also the strategic risk: a comparison that shocks can drive away precisely those one wanted to persuade.

**6. Criticizing the form doesn't refute the content.** If the comparison is inappropriate in a context, the answer is not to use it there, not to dismiss the question of animals.

**7. The case for animals stands without analogies.** Sentience, the needlessness of the harm and moral consistency are enough (A8).

**8. How, where and by whom matters.** The structural comparison can be valid and still be misused. Making it respectfully, without images that expose the human victims, listening to the affected communities and weighing whether it helps or hinders in that context, is a duty for those who defend animals.

**9. Peter Singer, the philosopher, used the comparison for something else.** Peter Singer (no relation to Isaac Bashevis Singer) doesn't equate victims in *Practical Ethics*: he compares how hard a choice is. He says that ceasing to support factory farming is less difficult than it would have been for a white Southerner in the United States to defy the traditions of his society and free the people he enslaved (ch. 3). In the preface, he reports that a German organization, distancing itself from him, passed a motion holding that the uniqueness of human life rules out any comparison with other living beings, and he replies that banning comparisons between species would be philosophically indefensible. But he also shows the limit of strong metaphors: in the chapter on ends and means, he cites one of the British founders of the Animal Liberation Front, Ronnie Lee, who described human domination over animals as a *Reich*, and an anti-abortion activist who spoke of more deaths than Hitler caused. He notes that these comparisons explain why those activists feel justified, but don't answer who has the right to decide which means are acceptable (ch. 11; see K8). A comparison can shed light on the structure of a harm without licensing any response to it.

**10. Not every mention is a comparison.** Slavery or the Holocaust often appear in an argument only as a counterexample to a principle. When someone says rescuing animals is wrong because it's illegal, pointing out that hiding Jews from the Nazis was also illegal merely shows that "illegal, therefore wrong" is false; the philosopher Nathan Nobis notes that this doesn't compare the victims (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 79; see K8). For those who want to discuss the comparison itself, Nobis recommends *The Holocaust and the Henmaid's Tale: A Case for Comparing Atrocities* (2005), by Karen Davis, founder of the organization United Poultry Concerns, who defends the legitimacy of comparing atrocities and discusses the conditions under which it can be done.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the harm of the comparison outweighs the benefit

The most serious version says that even if the analogy is structurally valid, it hurts so much and alienates so many people that it shouldn't be used, at least without the support of the communities whose history is invoked.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It may be right about strategy in many contexts.** Many animal advocates agree and avoid these comparisons, or make them only with great care.
2. **It's about communication, not about the animals.** The moral question remains.
3. **It doesn't show the comparison is false.** An analogy can be true and misused; criticizing the use doesn't erase the similarities.
4. **There are other ways to speak.** Animal suffering can be described on its own terms.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even without any historical comparison, the moral question remains: animals feel, and we make them suffer and die without need. Is that fair?

The principle being tested is: "if a comparison offends, the question being compared can be dismissed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exaggerated political comparisons | Political questions could be ignored | No one concludes this |
| Medical analogies | Diseases compared to wars would stop mattering | Absurd |
| Animals | The question of animals could be ignored | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

This objection is right on important points. Comparisons with slavery and the Holocaust can deeply hurt people and communities still living with the consequences of these atrocities, and animal advocates have used them disrespectfully, as courts and PETA itself acknowledged. Care here is a duty.

But the objection goes wrong when it treats every comparison as an insult. The structural similarities exist, and were pointed out by Jewish writers and by survivors. Refusing them on principle usually assumes that animals don't matter. The comparison can be valid; how, where and by whom it's made is what decides whether it respects the human victims. And the defense of animals still stands with or without it.
