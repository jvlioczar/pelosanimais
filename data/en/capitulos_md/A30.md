# A30. "The sentience criterion is anthropocentric"

**Thesis:** the accusation says the sentience criterion just swaps one human trait for another: instead of reason, it picks the capacity to feel, which we also have. But the criterion doesn't consider animals because they resemble us. It considers any being that can be harmed or benefited, and that's why it considers humans too. It gives no special weight to our species, leaves out animals that probably don't feel, and would include non-animal or non-organic minds if they felt. Starting from our own case to recognize other minds is a starting point for knowing, not a rule about who matters.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You only value sentience because humans are sentient. Deep down, you're still putting human beings at the center."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Anthropocentrism picks a typically human trait and gives consideration to other beings according to how much they resemble us in that trait.
2. **P2.** Sentience is a human trait, and we only recognize it in others by analogy with our own case.
3. **P3.** The sentience criterion gives consideration to those who have that trait, that is, to those who resemble humans in that respect.
4. **C.** So the sentience criterion is anthropocentric, and "sentiocentrism" is just animalism in disguise: it includes animals because they are like us.

P1 describes many forms of anthropocentrism well, and P2 has a true part: we know other minds from our own. The problem lies in P3, which confuses the reason for the criterion with the fact that humans meet it.

The argument is **valid** in form, but P3 is false: the criterion doesn't ask whether a being resembles humans, but whether things can go better or worse for it.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine a firefighter who saves people and cats from a fire. They don't save the cats because cats look like people. They save them because cats also get burned and feel pain. If a being from another planet showed up who felt pain, the firefighter would save it too, even if it looked like neither a person nor a cat.

## 4. Short answer

I get the suspicion: a lot of ethics has put human beings at the center and called that neutral. But here the order is the reverse. It's not that animals matter because they resemble us. It's that we matter because we can be harmed, and everyone who can be harmed matters for the same reason. That's why sponges are animals and are probably left out, while a digital mind or an extraterrestrial that felt would be in. If humans felt nothing, the criterion wouldn't even include us.

**Question to ask back:** "If a being showed up that looked nothing like us but felt pain, do you think its pain wouldn't count?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Question | Anthropocentrism | Sentience criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What counts? | Resemblance to humans | The possibility of being harmed or benefited |
| Do humans get special weight? | Yes, they are the measure | No, they count like any being that feels |
| Are all animals in? | Those most like us count more | No: animals without a nervous system are probably left out |
| Are non-animal beings in? | No | Yes, if they feel, whether plants, machines or extraterrestrials |
| What if humans didn't feel? | They'd still be at the center | They wouldn't be included |

The last row is the decisive test. An anthropocentric criterion starts from humans and measures others by them. The sentience criterion starts from a question that doesn't mention humans: is there someone there for whom things can go better or worse?

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The order of reasons is the reverse.** The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha shows that defenders of the criterion don't begin by saying humans matter and then look for something similar in animals. They say humans matter because they feel, and that the same holds for every being that feels. The criterion would still pick out who can be harmed even if humans weren't sentient ([Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 2](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v6/02-is-the-criterion-of-sentience-anthropocentric-at-bottom.html)).

**2. Reason and sentience aren't equivalent traits.** Classic anthropocentrism picks traits such as reason or language. Those traits don't say who can be harmed: a being can suffer without reasoning like us. Sentience, by contrast, is precisely the condition for something to be good or bad for someone ([vol. VI, ch. 1](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v6/01-what-is-relevant-for-knowing-to-whom-we-owe-consideration.html)). Choosing reason is choosing by resemblance; choosing sentience is choosing by what is at stake.

**3. It isn't animalism.** All sentient beings known so far are animals, but that's a fact about the world, not the criterion. Sponges have no nervous system, and jellyfish have a non-centralized one; so, according to Cunha and the organization Animal Ethics, they probably don't feel and are left out. If it were ever discovered that a plant or a fungus feels, the criterion would include it (F8) ([vol. VI, ch. 3](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v6/03-does-the-criterion-of-sentience-discriminate-against.html)).

**4. Non-organic minds would be in.** The same goes for robots, AI systems or digital beings that came to feel, and for sentient extraterrestrials. What a being is made of matters as little as its species (N21). Brian Tomasik (2015) argues that, if they exist, sentient digital minds may become very numerous, and that animal advocates should take this seriously. A criterion that includes beings with no bodily resemblance to us can't be a measure of resemblance to humans.

**5. Epistemic anthropocentrism isn't moral anthropocentrism.** It's true that we start from our own case: we infer other beings' pain by analogy with ours, and that method has limits, discussed for centuries as the problem of other minds (Avramides, SEP). That is an anthropocentrism of knowledge, and it calls for care in both directions, against exaggerating similarities and against missing ways of feeling unlike ours (A7). But moral anthropocentrism, the idea that only humans matter or matter more for being human, doesn't follow from it. The philosopher Tim Hayward (1997) notes that there is an element of human perspective in any ethic, since humans formulate it, and that the moral defects are usually better described as speciesism and human chauvinism.

**6. The criterion gives humans no extra weight.** The usual form of the criterion is equal consideration of similar interests: a pain of the same intensity weighs the same, whoever has it (Gruen and Monsó, SEP; see the page [Antispeciesism and sentientism](../alem/antiespecismo.html)). This doesn't mean all beings have the same interests; it means belonging to the human species adds no weight. An anthropocentrism that doesn't favor humans isn't anthropocentrism.

**7. The accusation has been made before, and some disagree.** Cunha cites the philosopher Tom Regan (1981) as an example of this criticism, made in the debate on environmental ethics. Biocentric and ecocentric views hold that every living being, or ecosystems, have value of their own, even without experience (Brennan and Lo, SEP). The debate is legitimate, but it's a different one: it asks whether only experience matters, and doesn't show that the sentience criterion measures beings by humans.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: choosing experience is already choosing from our point of view

The most serious version says: even if the criterion doesn't ask about resemblance to humans, the idea that only those with experiences matter reflects what we, beings who feel, value. A tree can also grow well or badly, and whoever says that doesn't count would be imposing our perspective.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Good for someone isn't the same as good in a certain way.** One can say a tree is in good condition, just as a car can be. But without experience there's no one for whom that is better or worse; as Cunha puts it, there is only a living body. This distinction doesn't depend on being human: it holds for any being that feels.
2. **Formulating an ethic is always the task of whoever formulates it.** Every ethic is thought up by humans, including biocentric ones. If that were enough to make it anthropocentric, no ethic would escape, and the accusation would no longer distinguish one position from another (Hayward, 1997).
3. **The counter-reply changes the question.** It doesn't show that the criterion favors humans; it shows there's disagreement about whether beings without experience have value of their own. That disagreement exists and is debated, but it doesn't support the charge of anthropocentrism.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A30 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | [Affirming the consequent](../falacias.html#affirming-the-consequent) | "If the criterion were anthropocentric, it would include humans; it does; so it's anthropocentric" |
| Logical | [Equivocation](../falacias.html#equivocation) | Uses "anthropocentrism" sometimes to mean knowing from our own case, sometimes to mean only humans matter |
| Epistemological | Confusing extension with criterion | Since every known sentient being is an animal, concludes that the criterion is being an animal |
| Moral | [Tu quoque](../falacias.html#tu-quoque) | Answers the critique of speciesism by saying sentientism makes the same mistake, without showing that it does |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accuses the criterion of putting humans at the center.** But the criterion gives humans no weight for being human.
2. **Calls sentientism animalism.** But it leaves out sponges, which are animals, and would include sentient machines, which aren't.
3. **Says starting from our own case taints ethics.** But every ethic, including the one making the accusation, is formulated by humans.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a criterion that humans meet, and that we recognize from our own case, is anthropocentric".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicine | Treating animals' pain in veterinary care would be anthropocentric, because pain was studied first in humans | No one thinks this |
| Biocentrism | Valuing every living being would also be anthropocentric, because humans are alive | The accusation turns against whoever makes it |
| Humans | Protecting a baby from pain would mean valuing it only for resembling adults | The reason is that the baby suffers, not the resemblance |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that many ethics have put human beings at the center and called that impartial, and that we should be wary of criteria that happen to favor whoever formulates them. It's also right that we know other minds from our own, and that this can lead us to exaggerate similarities or miss ways of feeling very different from ours.

The mistake is concluding that the sentience criterion measures beings by humans. It measures by what can be good or bad for someone, and so it would include any being that feels, whatever its form.
