# M2. "I was vegan for my health, and I changed my mind"

**Thesis:** someone who adopted a plant-based diet for health or for the environment is right on one point: if that was the only reason and the person changed their mind about the facts, no reason was left. That is why indirect arguments are fragile. But there is more than one path. Many people arrive with several reasons, and the animals may be among them; what tends to vary is how much weight that reason carries and whether it stays strong over time. For veganism, the reason concerning animals is the center, and it is the one that does not depend on any nutrition study. In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, health was the only motivation cited by a majority of those who gave up (58%), which is compatible with the idea that, for many, the reasons tied to animals were not the strongest or did not last.

**Family:** M. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I went vegan because of a health documentary. Then I read more and saw it wasn't quite like that. It was never about the animals. Now I eat everything, in balance."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** I adopted a plant-based diet because I believed it was the healthiest (or the best for the environment).
2. **P2.** After informing myself better, I changed my mind about those facts.
3. **P3.** Without that reason, there is no other reason to keep the diet.
4. **C.** So I have no reason to continue.

P1 and P2 are sincere accounts, and P2 may even be partly correct: many health promises made in the name of veganism are exaggerated. The problem lies in P3: there may be a reason that was left out of the count, the animals, and health facts do not refute it. In some cases it was never examined; in others it existed and was one of several reasons, but it was not strong enough or lost strength over time.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound** for everyone: P3 is only true if the reason concerning animals did not exist or stopped existing, and that is a question for each person that health facts do not answer.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone who stopped pulling the cat's tail because they heard that cats bring bad luck. One day they find out the bad luck story is nonsense. Can they go back to pulling the cat's tail? No, because the real reason was always something else: it hurts the cat. With animals that become food it's similar. Even if a person changes their mind about health, the animals' reason is still there.

## 4. Short answer

That makes sense: if the reason was only health and you changed your mind, the diet lost its footing. It may also be that you had more than one reason and that the one about animals existed but was not the strongest, or lost strength over time. That is human and common. Veganism itself is the reason about animals, and it does not change with revisions in nutrition. On the facts: a well-planned plant-based diet is adequate, according to the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States, but it isn't a miracle cure or the only way to be healthy. If the diet no longer convinces you today, it is worth asking what is left of the reason about animals and whether there is something of it you want to keep.

**Question to ask back:** "Besides health, did the animals ever become part of your reasons? How did that reason hold up over time?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The same plate can have very different reasons behind it, and they hold up differently:

| Reason for the diet | What it depends on | What knocks it down |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health | Nutrition studies and how the person feels | A new study, a new fad diet, feeling unwell (M1) |
| Environment | Calculations of emissions and land use | The belief that local or "regenerative" meat solves it (H11) |
| Animals | Their feeling pain and having a life to lose | Only proof that they don't feel, which doesn't exist (A1) |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The argument is coherent, and it shows how fragile indirect reasons are.** Someone who stops eating animals only for a benefit to themselves can turn back when the benefit looks smaller. This guide keeps indirect arguments separate for exactly that reason: health and the environment are good additional motives, but they aren't the heart of the matter.

**2. A diet and veganism can look the same.** Veganism is the refusal, as far as is possible and practicable, to exploit animals: in food, clothing, entertainment and testing (L22). Someone who adopted a plant-based diet only for health or for the environment was following a diet, and that is not a criticism: it is a description. Someone who had more than one reason, including the animals, was on the path to veganism, even if that was the weakest of their reasons. In that case the person may have left the diet without re-examining whether the reason about animals still stood. So "I used to be vegan and I know what it's like" (M10) describes, depending on the case, the experience of a diet or of a conviction that did not last.

**3. The data fit the account.** In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, the only motivation cited by a majority of former vegetarians and vegans was health (58%). Among those who continued, five motivations passed the halfway mark: health (69%), animal protection (68%), disgust at meat (63%), the environment (59%) and taste (52%). In a 2013 study of 292 vegetarians, published in the journal *Appetite* by Hoffman and colleagues, those with ethical motivation had stronger conviction and had kept the diet longer than those motivated by health. There is a caveat: in a 2022 Faunalytics study that followed 222 people for six months, the type of main motivation made no difference to success over that period. What counted most was the level of commitment.

**4. The health facts, with no exaggeration either way.** The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States stated in 2016 that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are healthful and adequate at all stages of life. In its 2025 update, which covers only adults who aren't pregnant or lactating, it maintained that these patterns can be nutritionally adequate and offer long-term benefits. "Well planned" includes supplementing B12. None of this makes a plant-based diet a cure for everything, and no one needs it in order to be healthy: anyone who heard those promises in a documentary is right to be wary (L24). The mistake is to conclude the opposite from the exaggeration (G16).

**5. The environmental facts.** Buying local meat changes little: transport accounts for less than 10% of emissions for most foods. In the data compiled by Our World in Data, the lowest-impact beef emits about 9 kg of CO2 equivalent per 100 g of protein, against 0.8 kg for the highest-impact peas (H11).

**6. The reason about animals does not depend on health facts.** A diet can be swapped when the science changes. The animal's situation in the slaughterhouse doesn't change with any nutrition review. Someone who had this reason as one among several, or as a reason that lost strength, can reassess its weight now, apart from the others.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: I thought about the animals and wasn't convinced

The most serious version says: it's not that I never thought about it. I considered the argument about animals and it didn't convince me. My reason was health, that's legitimate, and no one can force me to have another.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's legitimate as far as the diet goes.** No one should keep a health diet they have stopped believing in. But in that case the conversation moves elsewhere: it becomes about the animals, and the chapters are different ones (A1, B2, B1, B3).
2. **"I wasn't convinced" calls for an argument.** Animals feel pain and fear. For that not to count, one has to point to a relevant difference between their suffering and ours. Species alone isn't that difference.
3. **Changing your mind about nutrition doesn't change the facts about animals.** The two questions are independent. You can think a plant-based diet isn't the healthiest and still conclude that one shouldn't kill someone who feels when there is an alternative.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Health and the environment are reasons about the one who eats and about the planet. The third party involved is the animal: they have a life that goes well or badly for them, and that life ends at slaughter, whether the meat is healthy or not, local or not. The moral question isn't "which diet is best for me?". It is: do I have a good enough reason to do this to someone who feels? Someone who had that reason but it grew weaker, or who had not yet considered it, can come back to it now, calmly.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hidden premise | Assumes health and the environment were the only possible reasons |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "vegan" for a diet, and draws a conclusion about an ethical position |
| Epistemological | From exaggeration to the opposite | Exaggerated promises were false, so a plant-based diet must be inadequate |
| Moral | Leaving out the third party | Decides by weighing only one's own interests, without counting the animals' |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Changed their mind about health, but the reason about animals does not depend on it.** It was not always re-examined.
2. **Distrusts documentaries, but trusts the next guru.** The new diet usually comes from sources of the same kind.
3. **Swapped the environmental reason for local meat.** The data show that the type of food weighs far more than the distance.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M2 | Evidence |

| Authority bias | One documentary or influencer decides the way in, and another decides the way out | General literature on heuristics |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I stopped believing in the reason that led me to a practice, there is no other reason to keep it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone who didn't drive drunk only for fear of the fine | With no enforcement, they could drive drunk | The main reason was the other people on the road |
| Someone who treated employees well only because it was profitable | If it stopped being profitable, they could mistreat them | No one would accept this: they always deserved respect |
| Someone who stopped smoking near their children only because of the smell on their clothes | With the smell solved, they could start again | The children's health was the reason they had yet to see |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that a diet adopted for health should be judged by health, and that changing your mind in the face of new information is a sign of honesty. It's right that a lot of vegan outreach exaggerates: it promises cures, weight loss and boundless energy, and people who come in through those promises leave when they fail. That is a fair criticism of the movement: promoting veganism as a miracle diet produces people who give up.

But this shows the health reason was weak, not that every reason was. Veganism has at its center the reason about animals, the body of the one who is eaten, and it still stands for anyone who wants to examine or strengthen it.
