# M2. "Suffering and death are part of nature"

**Thesis:** being part of nature doesn't make something good or indifferent for the one who suffers. Diseases, parasites and death in childbirth are also part of human nature, and that was never a reason not to treat them. The absence of a culprit matters for judging agents, not for measuring the victims' harm.

**Family:** M. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's just how nature is. Some eat others, some get sick, others die. It's part of it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** It only makes sense to speak of moral evil when there's a responsible agent.
2. **P2.** In nature, suffering is caused by processes without intention: predation, disease, climate.
3. **C1.** So natural suffering isn't a moral evil.
4. **C2.** And what isn't a moral evil gives us no reason to act.

P1 and P2 are acceptable if we're talking about blame: a hurricane is guilty of nothing. The problem lies in C2, which jumps from the absence of a culprit to the absence of a victim.

The argument is **invalid**: from "no one is to blame" it doesn't follow that "no one was harmed".

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "Nature is cruel, that's how it is." |
| Everyday conversation | "Nature is neither good nor bad." |

| Academic version | "Natural suffering is morally neutral, because it doesn't result from choices." |
| Disguised version | "It's the circle of life." (which is C7) |

## 3. Answer for children

Catching a cold is part of life, right? Even so, when you're sick, someone looks after you, gives you medicine and a warm drink. No one says "leave it, it's natural". What's natural can hurt just the same. And when it hurts, we help if we can.

## 4. Short answer

Disease is also part of nature, and we still treat it. No one leaves a child with an infection untreated because bacteria are natural. That no one is to blame for suffering doesn't make it any less bad for the one who suffers. The question is whether we can help without causing more harm.

**Question to ask back:** "If a disease is natural, would you stop treating it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two questions:

| Question | Depends on | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is anyone to blame? | There being an agent who chose | An arsonist is to blame; lightning isn't |
| Was anyone harmed? | There being a victim who suffered | A forest burned by lightning has as many victims as one burned by an arsonist |

For the one who suffers, the origin of the suffering doesn't change what they feel.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Neutral for whom?** Nature has no intentions, but animals have experiences. The suffering of an animal eaten alive by parasites isn't neutral for it.

**2. Natural isn't a criterion of good.** In the essay *Nature*, John Stuart Mill observed that nature does, every day, things for which a human being would be imprisoned or hanged. Copying nature was never a good moral guide (C1).

**3. We already intervene in the natural all the time.** Vaccines, antibiotics, glasses, anaesthesia, shelter from the cold: much of civilization consists of reducing natural suffering.

**4. The human test.** Faced with an epidemic or an earthquake, no one says "it's part of nature, let's not help". The absence of a culprit was never a reason to deny help to humans. Denying it to animals for the same reason is using a criterion we apply only to them.

**5. There's no agent, but there's someone who can help.** Ethics asks not only who caused something, but also what we, who can act, ought to do.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mill, *Nature* (1874) | Nature kills, tortures and destroys all the time; imitating it isn't a moral criterion | Published in *Three Essays on Religion* |
| [Animal Ethics, "Why wild animal suffering matters"](https://www.animal-ethics.org/wild-animal-suffering-matters/) | Suffering matters to the one who suffers, whatever its cause | |
| [*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VII](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v7/15-if-nature-is-not-evil-is-natural-suffering-neutral.html) | Discusses whether natural suffering is neutral and whether nature's indifference releases us from helping | Chapters 13, 15 and 16 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: suffering has a function

The most serious version says pain is useful: it teaches animals to avoid danger, and natural selection depends on it. Ending natural suffering would destroy something that gives meaning to animals' lives.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Function isn't wellbeing.** Pain serves the survival of genes, not the good of the individual. For the young animal that dies on its first day, it teaches nothing.
2. **We accept relieving pain that has a function.** The pain of childbirth and of a fracture have functions, and we still use anaesthesia.
3. **No one proposes ending all pain at once.** The serious proposal is to reduce avoidable suffering with research and caution (M5, M10).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | From the natural it concludes the acceptable |
| Logical | Non sequitur | From "no one is to blame" it concludes "no one was harmed" |
| Moral | Confusing blame and harm | Measures the victim's harm by the cause's intention |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts treating the natural in humans, but not in animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Takes medicine.** And the disease was natural.
2. **Supports humanitarian aid in disasters.** Earthquakes and droughts have no culprit.
3. **Takes their own animal to the vet.** Without saying its illness "is part of it".

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M2 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appeal to nature | The natural seems good or inevitable | Linked to C1 and C7 |
| Omission bias | Not acting seems less serious than acting | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |
| Intention bias | Harm only seems serious when someone meant to cause it | [Tomasik, "Intention-based moral reactions distort intuitions about wild animals"](https://briantomasik.com/intention-based-moral-reactions-distort-intuitions-about-wild-animals/) |
| Just-world hypothesis | If suffering is part of the order of things, it seems no one is wronged by it | [Lerner and Miller, 1978](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.85.5.1030) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "suffering that's part of nature doesn't need to be relieved".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human diseases | We shouldn't treat infections, cancer or malaria | No one accepts this |
| Childbirth | Anaesthesia would be a mistake | Some people once said so, and lost the debate |
| Natural disasters | We shouldn't help victims | Humanitarian aid exists precisely for this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that nature isn't a moral agent and that it makes no sense to blame lions or viruses. It's also right that pain has important biological functions and that tampering with it requires great care.

But the question isn't whether nature is to blame. It's whether we, who can act, have reason to help those who suffer. And the natural origin of suffering has never, in any other context, been a reason not to help.
