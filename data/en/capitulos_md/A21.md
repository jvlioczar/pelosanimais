# A21. "Animals aren't persons"

**Thesis:** "person" is a concept we created, and that we already apply to companies, ships and rivers. Using it to decide who may be killed is choosing the definition that produces the desired conclusion. By the classic criterion of personhood, Locke's, human babies aren't persons either, and some animals come closer to the criterion than they do.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Animals aren't people. People are people, animals are animals."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Only persons have full moral status and can hold rights.
2. **P2.** A person is a self-aware being, able to recognize itself as the same over time, to make plans and take on responsibilities.
3. **P3.** Animals aren't persons in this sense.
4. **C.** So animals don't have full moral status and may be used.

P2's definition comes from John Locke, who described a person as a thinking, intelligent being, with reason and reflection, able to consider itself as the same thinking thing in different times and places. It's a **psychological** definition, not a biological one: it doesn't say "member of the human species".

There's also the **legal version**, used in courts: a person is whoever can hold rights and duties. This was the criterion adopted by the majority of the New York Court of Appeals in the case of Happy the elephant, in 2022.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because P2 and P3 can't both be true without excluding human babies, and because P1 makes a definition we chose into the source of moral value.

## 3. Answer for children

Words are invented by us. "Person" is one of them. Did you know that, in the law, a company is also a "person"? It has no body and feels nothing, and still it can have rights on paper. So calling someone a person isn't what decides whether they can feel pain. What decides it is having a body and a mind that feel. A pig has those.

## 4. Short answer

This argument settles the question by the choice of word. If "person" means self-awareness and plans for the future, then newborn babies aren't persons either, and elephants, great apes and dolphins come closer to the criterion than they do. If "person" means a legal holder of rights, remember that companies are legal persons and that the Whanganui River, in New Zealand, was granted legal personhood in 2017. Personhood is a tool we grant, not a fact of nature.

**Question to ask back:** "Does a company feel pain? Then why is it a person and the pig isn't?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The word "person" is used in at least three senses, and the argument switches between them as convenient.

| Sense | What it means | Who's included |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biological | A member of the species *Homo sapiens* | All humans, no animals. But then "person" is just another name for species, and the argument becomes A8 |
| Psychological (Locke) | A self-aware being that recognizes itself over time and makes plans | Typical adult humans and, probably, great apes, elephants, crows and dolphins. A newborn is left out |
| Legal | An entity to which the law assigns rights and duties | Humans, companies, ships, foundations and, in some countries, rivers and animals |

This is the book's clearest case of the **definist fallacy**: the definition that produces the desired answer is chosen, and then the answer is presented as a discovery. None of the three senses does the work the argument needs.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. In the psychological sense, it proves too much.** By Locke's criterion, a three-month-old baby isn't a person: they don't recognize themselves as the same being over time or make plans. Whoever holds P2 must accept that killing a baby wouldn't harm a person, or abandon the criterion.

**2. In the psychological sense, it includes animals.** Elephants recognize their own image in a mirror (Plotnik, de Waal and Reiss, 2006). Scrub jays (*Aphelocoma*) remember what they hid, where and how long ago, adjusting their behavior to the type of food, which has been called episodic-like memory (Clayton and Dickinson, 1998). If that's the criterion, it doesn't divide species the way the argument wants.

**3. In the legal sense, personhood is a tool, not a fact.** Companies are legal persons and feel nothing. In 2017, New Zealand recognized the Whanganui River as a legal person, with legal representatives, through the Te Awa Tupua Act. In 2022, Ecuador's Constitutional Court recognized individual animals as subjects of rights (see A22). The law grants personhood when it decides to protect something; the question is whether there's reason to protect, not whether the label has already been granted.

**4. The Happy case shows the argument in action, and its fragility.** In 2022, the New York Court of Appeals denied, by 5 votes to 2, the habeas corpus sought for Happy the elephant, holding that the writ applies only to human beings and that a person is someone who can bear duties and social responsibilities. In dissent, Judge Rivera observed that humans without full rights have been able to use habeas corpus, and that the majority failed to explain why an autonomous being of another species couldn't; Judge Wilson recalled that the writ was historically extended to enslaved people, who also had no recognized duties.

**5. The moral question doesn't depend on the word.** Even if we agree to call only humans persons, there's still a being who feels pain, fear and pleasure. Sentientism doesn't need the concept of person: it only needs someone for whom things can go well or badly.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: person as a moral concept, not a label

The serious reply says "person" isn't an arbitrary label: it marks the kind of being whose life has special value, because it has projects, projects itself into the future and can lose more than pleasure by dying. Stronger protection falls on these beings, which would explain why killing a human is worse than killing a fish. Philosophically, this appears in Jeff McMahan's time-relative interest account.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** Even if killing a person is worse than killing an animal, it doesn't follow that killing an animal is permitted, still less for the pleasure of a meal.
2. **It grades without giving a number.** Saying an animal's life is worth less requires saying how much less, and comparing it with what's gained in exchange. No one does that calculation when ordering a steak.
3. **It still excludes babies.** The reply gives less protection to exactly the humans least projected into the future, and most people reject that consequence.
4. **Suffering doesn't depend on personhood.** Even if accepted, the argument concerns death, not pain. Confining, mutilating and terrifying a being is still harm, whether or not it's a person.
5. **The concept's history counsels caution.** "Person" has been denied to women and to enslaved people, and in each case the denial came after the practice it served to justify.

| Epistemological | Outdated empirical premise | Ignores evidence of self-awareness and memory in many species |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts persons that don't feel.** Companies, foundations and, in some countries, rivers are legal persons with no sentience at all.
2. **Calls their own dog a member of the family.** Many people treat their pet as someone, with a name, a personality and a will of its own, and deny this to the pig.
3. **Protects those who aren't persons in the psychological sense.** Babies and people with advanced dementia remain fully protected.
4. **Condemns cruelty.** Punishing someone who tortures an animal presupposes that someone is being tortured, not just an object damaged.
5. **Uses the word as both argument and conclusion.** "Isn't a person" is presented as a reason for treating it a certain way, when it really just describes the treatment already given.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever isn't a person may be used".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newborn babies | They could be used. By the psychological criterion, a baby doesn't recognize itself over time or make plans, so it isn't a person | Whoever rejects this conclusion is admitting that sentience, not personhood, is what protects |
| People with advanced dementia | They'd lose status as they lost memory and plans. The psychological continuity that defines a person gradually unravels | Medical practice and the law do the opposite and reinforce protection |
| Human groups already excluded from the category | Denying them the status of person would be enough to permit their use. This move has a history: women and enslaved people have been legally treated as non-persons | In each case, the denial came to justify a practice that already existed |
| Companies and rivers | If personhood decides, companies would have more moral protection than a chimpanzee, because they're legal persons | Shows the label doesn't track the capacity to suffer |
| Artificial intelligence | A system granted personhood by law would come to be worth more than a sentient being without it | Reinforces that personhood is a legal tool, not a moral criterion |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The concept of person isn't philosophical rubbish. It captures something real: beings with self-awareness, memory of themselves and plans for the future may lose more by dying, and can be harmed in ways other beings can't, such as by humiliation or broken promises. Legal personhood is also a complex instrument, involving representation, procedures and responsibilities, and extending it requires careful institutional design, not just good intentions.

The mistake is turning this concept into the gateway to moral consideration. None of it answers the question that matters: is there someone there for whom what we do is good or bad? If there is, calling that someone a person or not is a decision about vocabulary, not about what we owe them.
