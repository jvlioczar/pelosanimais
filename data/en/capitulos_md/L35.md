# L35. "Defending animals is middle-class moralism"

**Thesis:** the charge bundles four different claims, and none of them holds up. That only the rich care about animals is false: in India, about 39% of adults say they are vegetarian, and in the United States people with lower incomes describe themselves as vegetarian more often. That the social background of those who defend an idea decides whether it's right is a fallacy, the same one that would discredit the abolition of slavery and workers' suffrage. That animal advocates have hidden motives can't be proven. And that the cause diverts from the fight against human exploitation assumes compassion is a resource that runs out, when history shows many people who fought for both. The criticism gets one thing right: the movement must take care not to speak only to those who can pay.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's middle-class moralism. People with real problems have no time to worry about animals."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The defense of animals is carried out mostly by middle- and upper-class people.
2. **P2.** A cause defended mostly by one class expresses that class's interests and way of life.
3. **P3.** The energy spent on animals is taken away from the fight against the exploitation of workers and the poor.
4. **C.** So the defense of animals is a class moralism that gets in the way of the struggles that matter.

The classic version comes from the Marxist philosopher Howard L. Parsons, who in 1977 described animal protection societies as products of the well-off classes and as a diversion of energy from radical social change. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane examines this charge and breaks it into parts (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 102–105).

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, which is false as a general description, at P2, which judges the idea by the background of those who defend it, and at P3, which treats compassion as a finite resource.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine a girl at school asks everyone not to hurt the neighborhood cat. A classmate says: "She only says that because she lives in a big house." But the cat feels pain just the same, wherever the girl lives. And lots of kids who live in small houses also look after animals. What matters is whether hurting the cat is right or wrong.

## 4. Short answer

I understand the suspicion: sometimes veganism shows up in pricey products and shops in rich neighborhoods. But it isn't a cause of the rich: in India, close to 4 in 10 adults are vegetarian, and in the United States people who earn less say they're vegetarian more often than people who earn more. And even if it were a cause of the rich, that wouldn't tell us whether making animals suffer is right or wrong. Many people have fought for workers and for animals at the same time.

**Question to ask back:** "If most vegans were poor, would factory farming become a problem for you?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

Cochrane shows that the charge mixes several claims, which need to be assessed one by one (Cochrane, 2010, p. 103):

| What the sentence says | What it usually defends |
| --- | --- |
| "Middle-class stuff" | That only the rich care about animals |
| "Bourgeois moralism" | That an idea's social origin decides whether it's right |
| "Rich people's guilt" | That animal advocates have hidden motives |
| "It diverts from the real struggle" | That attention to animals is taken away from humans |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It isn't a cause of the rich alone.** In India, about 39% of adults say they are vegetarian, and 81% follow some restriction on eating meat, according to the Pew Research Center (2021). In the United States, Gallup found in 2023 that 7% of lower-income people say they are vegetarian, against 4% in the middle-income group and 3% in the upper-income group. And worldwide, meat per person tends to rise with countries' income. Vegetarianism isn't veganism, and these figures don't measure activism; but they show that refusing meat isn't a habit confined to an elite. Cochrane adds that strong animal protection movements exist in developing countries such as India (Cochrane, 2010, p. 103).

**2. The origin of an idea doesn't decide whether it's right.** Even if every animal advocate were rich, that wouldn't make false the claim that animals suffer and that their suffering matters. Cochrane points out that if the campaigns for the abolition of slavery, for workers' votes or for women's emancipation had come only from the middle class, they wouldn't have been wrong for that reason (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 103–104). Judging a claim by who defends it is the genetic fallacy. The same goes for Parsons's reminder that some Nazis were fond of animals (L9).

**3. Hidden motives can't be proven.** The charge that animal advocates really want to show off their superiority or protect their privileges can't be checked, because no one can get inside anyone's head. There is vanity and opportunism in every movement, profession and cause, and that isn't enough to condemn a whole movement (Cochrane, 2010, p. 104).

**4. Compassion isn't a finite resource.** The idea that caring for animals takes care away from humans assumes moral concern runs out. History shows the opposite. In nineteenth-century Britain, William Wilberforce, who fought slavery, was one of the founders of the society that would become the RSPCA; Lord Shaftesbury, behind the laws that limited factory work, campaigned against cruelty to animals; and the Humanitarian League, founded by Henry Salt, counted the socialist playwright George Bernard Shaw among its members (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 101–102). John Stuart Mill and Gandhi also extended their compassion across several causes (Cochrane, 2010, p. 105). Today, many animal advocates also work for human rights (L21).

**5. Real conflicts are rare.** There can be hard choices when the vital interests of humans and animals clash. But the most extreme suffering is in intensive farming, and a society can tackle it without sacrificing anyone's vital interests (Cochrane, 2010, p. 105). Given the scale, about 88 billion land animals slaughtered for meat in 2024, according to FAO data compiled by Our World in Data, the problem is far from a whim.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the problem is the structure, not sincerity

The most serious version doesn't say vegans are insincere or that animals don't matter. It says: the exploitation of animals and that of workers have the same root, production for profit, and a campaign centered on consumer choices and moral reform leaves that structure intact. Time and political energy are limited, and the priority should be changing the structure (K5).

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's no longer the charge of moralism.** If the root is shared, animals are part of the problem, not a distraction from it. There are left-wing authors who treat animals as exploited by the same system, such as Katherine Perlo and Barbara Noske (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 105–106).
2. **One doesn't prevent the other.** Changing what one eats takes no time from any political struggle, and many movements combine both fronts (J20).
3. **The debate is internal to the left itself.** Cochrane, for example, accepts that capitalism has intensified animal suffering but doubts it's the cause, since earlier modes of production also exploited animals (Cochrane, 2010, p. 107). Authors such as Perlo and Noske see the link differently. Whatever the answer, the question about animals still stands.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Those who defend animals may be rich or poor, sincere or vain, left-wing or right-wing. None of this changes what happens on a farm or in a slaughterhouse. The question is whether it's just to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to, and it deserves an answer whoever asks it.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L35 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges the cause by the class of those who defend it |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Takes some wealthy advocates for the whole movement |
| Logical | False dilemma | Assumes caring for animals rules out caring for humans |
| Moral | Dodging the question | The advocates' class replaces the question about animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Claims to speak for the poor, but ignores the poor who don't eat meat.** In India and in many communities, refusing meat is a popular tradition, not an elite fashion.
2. **Rejects judging people by their origin, but judges an idea that way.** The same political tradition that criticizes class prejudice uses class to dismiss the cause.
3. **Criticizes exploitation, but accepts the largest one in numbers.** Billions of animals a year are left out of the count.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a cause defended mostly by one class is just that class's moralism".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abolition of slavery | Many abolitionists were middle class, so the cause would be moralism | The cause was just, whoever defended it |
| Women's suffrage | The first leaders were largely middle class, so it would be just their interest | The right came to apply to all women |
| Environmental protection | It would be just a concern of people with free time | The poorest are usually the most affected by environmental harm |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that veganism sometimes appears as a luxury market, in expensive products and in language far from people with little money, and that this pushes people away. It's also right that changes in livestock farming affect workers, and that a just transition has to think about them. The movement gains by showing that the basis of plant-based eating is cheap and by allying with other struggles.

But none of these points shows that animals don't matter. The class of those who defend a cause doesn't tell us whether it's just, and the animals' suffering remains the issue.
