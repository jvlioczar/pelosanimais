# L17. "Vegans defend animals, but they support abortion"

**Thesis:** veganism has no position on abortion: there are vegans on both sides, and the phrase generalizes. It's also a tu quoque: even if a vegan were inconsistent, that wouldn't justify exploiting animals. And the two cases have different structures. In abortion there is a conflict with a person's body, which doesn't exist in eating animals; and, by the criterion of sentience, the question about the fetus is when it begins to feel, a point on which science still disagrees. For slaughtered animals, that doubt doesn't exist. This chapter does not say which position on abortion is correct.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You cry over a chick and support killing babies. If life matters, why not the life of the fetus?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegans say we shouldn't kill beings who can suffer when it can be avoided.
2. **P2.** A human fetus is, or will be, a being capable of suffering.
3. **P3.** Many vegans support abortion rights.
4. **P4.** People who apply a principle only when it suits them don't deserve to be taken seriously.
5. **C.** So the vegan defense of animals can be dismissed.

P1 is true. P2 depends on the stage of pregnancy, and it's the point where science is consulted. P3 is true of many vegans and false of others. The central problem lies in P4 and the conclusion: the consistency of the speaker doesn't decide whether what they say about animals is true.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it generalizes in P3, treats two structurally different cases as the same, and draws a conclusion about animals from a flaw in those who defend them.

## 3. Answer for children

This is a grown-up conversation about a hard subject, and grown-ups think different things about it. Among people who don't eat animals, some think one way and some think another. One thing doesn't change: animals feel pain and fear, and that stays true whatever anyone thinks about other subjects.

## 4. Short answer

Veganism is about the use of animals and has no position on abortion. There are vegans for and against legal abortion, as in any group. And even if I were inconsistent, that wouldn't change what happens to animals. The two cases are also different: in abortion there is a conflict with a person's body, and science debates when the fetus begins to feel. With a pig or a chicken there is no comparable conflict, and no one seriously doubts that they feel. If what worries you about the fetus is its capacity to suffer, we share a reason.

**Question to ask back:** "What, for you, makes someone's life matter morally? Does that reason apply to a pig or a hen?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats as equal two cases that differ on decisive points:

| Point | Abortion | Eating animals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there a conflict with another person's body? | Yes: pregnancy takes place in the pregnant person's body | No |
| Does the being in question feel? | It depends on the stage of pregnancy; science disagrees about the timing | Yes, beyond reasonable doubt, for mammals and birds |
| Does the vegan movement have a position? | No | Yes |

Recognizing these differences doesn't settle the abortion debate. It only shows that a person can answer the two questions differently without contradiction.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Veganism has no position on abortion.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding, as far as is possible and practicable, exploitation of and cruelty to animals. It says nothing about abortion. There are vegans for and against legal abortion. There are even books by animal advocates examining whether the two positions fit together, such as *Beating Hearts* (2016), by the American legal scholars Sherry Colb and Michael Dorf, which argues that they do; the book has drawn criticism, including from people who share its defense of animals.

**2. It's a tu quoque.** Pointing to an inconsistency in the speaker doesn't refute what was said. If a vegan were inconsistent, the conclusion would be that they should revise one of the two positions, not that animals may be exploited (L11).

**3. Abortion involves a conflict that doesn't exist on the plate.** Pregnancy takes place inside a person's body. One strand of philosophy holds that this weighs decisively, even if the fetus has a full right to life: that is the 1971 argument of the American philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson. Another strand disagrees, such as the American philosopher Don Marquis, for whom abortion deprives the fetus of a valuable future. The guide doesn't settle that dispute. What matters here is that eating animals involves no comparable conflict: no one needs a pig's body to sustain their own life or autonomy when there are alternatives.

**4. By the criterion of sentience, the question about the fetus is empirical, and science disagrees.** The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), the British professional body, concluded in 2010 that the fetus cannot feel pain before 24 weeks, for lack of connections to the cerebral cortex. In its December 2022 review, it concluded that pain perception before 28 weeks is "unlikely". Taking a different view, the psychologist Stuart Derbyshire and the clinician John Bockmann, who state that they hold divergent views on abortion, argued in 2020 in the *Journal of Medical Ethics* that neuroscience cannot definitively rule out pain before 24 weeks, and that an "immediate and unreflective" pain may be possible from as early as 12 weeks; they themselves note that some evidence points to a later stage. On mammals and birds, by contrast, the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) speaks of strong scientific support for conscious experience.

**5. The reverse version doesn't settle things either.** "If you're against abortion, you should be vegan" has the same form: it demands consistency. It has force for those who oppose abortion because of the fetus's capacity to suffer, since that reason applies to animals too. But many people oppose it for other reasons, such as the fetus's potential (A19), membership of the human species (A8) or faith (A17), and those reasons need to be discussed one by one. As an attack on the person, the reverse version is as weak as the original.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: those who give fish the benefit of the doubt should give it to the fetus

The most serious version says: vegans ask for caution when there is doubt about the sentience of fish and insects (A2). If some scientists allow for fetal pain from 12 weeks, the same caution should apply to the fetus. Not applying the principle here would be a double standard.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a good argument within the abortion debate.** A vegan who uses the precautionary principle has to take that question seriously, and many do. That's different from showing that the defense of animals is wrong.
2. **Caution weighs differently when another body is involved.** Sparing a fish out of caution costs nothing to anyone's body. In pregnancy, caution has a cost for a person, and it's that conflict that divides philosophers and societies. The guide doesn't say how it should be resolved.
3. **The counter-reply strengthens the criterion.** It only works if the capacity to suffer matters. Whoever uses it has already accepted the premise they need in order to look at the pig, the cow and the chicken.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Abortion is one of the hardest topics in ethics, and serious people disagree about it. None of that changes what happens to animals. They feel, they are killed by the billions, and in their case there is no conflict of bodies that makes the decision tragic. Whatever your position on abortion, the question remains: what justifies making an animal suffer and die when there is an alternative?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L17 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Tu quoque | The vegan's supposed inconsistency becomes a reason to ignore animals |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Attributes to all vegans a position veganism doesn't have |
| Logical | False analogy | Treats a case with a conflict of bodies and one without as the same |
| Epistemological | Certainty where there is disagreement | Talks about the fetus as if science were unanimous on when it feels |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Demands consistency from vegans.** But if a life that feels matters, that applies to the person demanding it too.
2. **Says the fetus's capacity to suffer matters.** And doesn't consider it for animals who undoubtedly suffer.
3. **Treats vegans as a bloc.** And wouldn't accept all meat eaters being treated that way.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone who defends a cause seems inconsistent on another issue, the cause can be dismissed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People against abortion and in favor of the death penalty | Opposition to abortion could be dismissed | Those who use L17 wouldn't accept that |
| People who defend children and don't donate to fight hunger | The defense of children could be ignored | Absurd |
| Vegans, whatever their position on abortion | The question of animals could be ignored | The same mistake |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right to demand consistency: anyone who says the capacity to suffer is what matters has to apply that criterion to every case, including the fetus at the stages when pain is possible, and can't treat the scientific doubt as if it didn't exist. It's also right that some vegans talk about abortion carelessly, or use the reverse version to attack religious people.

But that consistency is a task for each person, not an objection against animals. Concern for a being who may feel and concern for beings who certainly feel come from the same place. Whoever takes the first seriously has one more reason, not one fewer, to take the second seriously.
