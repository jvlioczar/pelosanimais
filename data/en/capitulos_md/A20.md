# A20. "Humans have intrinsic value; animals have instrumental value"

**Thesis:** saying only humans have value in themselves is asserting the conclusion without proving it. When you ask what gives humans that value, the answer is some trait, such as reason or self-awareness, that isn't present in all humans and is present, to some degree, in many animals. Even philosophers in the Kantian tradition, such as Christine Korsgaard, now conclude that sentient animals are ends in themselves too.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Only human beings have dignity. An animal is a means, not an end."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some beings have intrinsic value: they are valuable in themselves and may not be used merely as means.
2. **P2.** That value comes from rationality, from the capacity to act morally and give oneself a law, as Kant argued.
3. **P3.** Only humans have that capacity.
4. **C.** So only humans have intrinsic value, and animals may be used as means.

The strong version has the greatest philosophical tradition of any in the catalog. Kant held that only rational beings are ends in themselves, and that our duties regarding animals are indirect: we shouldn't be cruel to them because that makes us cruel to humans.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3, which return to the problems of A1 and A11, and in a circularity: value is defined precisely by the trait one wants to use to exclude.

## 3. Answer for children

If you had a pet, would it be important only because you like it, or because it has a life of its own that's good for it? It eats, plays, gets cold and gets frightened, and all of that matters to it, even when nobody's looking. That's what being valuable in itself means. Farm animals also have a life that's theirs.

## 4. Short answer

Saying only humans are valuable in themselves is repeating the conclusion. When you ask why, the answer is usually reason. But babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities don't reason, and they don't lose dignity because of it. What they all have is a life that can go well or badly for themselves, and many animals have that too. Even Kantian philosophers, such as Christine Korsgaard, now argue that sentient animals are ends in themselves.

**Question to ask back:** "What exactly gives a baby intrinsic value that a pig doesn't have?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument has a circular structure:

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Why do only humans have intrinsic value? |
| Answer | Because they're rational (or have a soul, or dignity) |
| Next question | Why does that trait, and not the capacity to suffer, define value? |
| Usual answer | Because it's what humans have |

The criterion is chosen so as to produce the conclusion. It's **begging the question**.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It doesn't protect all humans.** If intrinsic value comes from rationality, babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities wouldn't have it. No one accepts that, and attempts to get around it fall into the problems of A11 and A12.

**2. The Kantian tradition itself is revising the point.** Christine Korsgaard, one of Kant's leading interpreters, argues in *Fellow Creatures* (2018) that all sentient animals should be treated as ends in themselves: they have a good of their own, things can go well or badly for them from their own point of view, and that's what gives us reason to respect them.

**3. Having a good of one's own is what matters.** An object only has value for someone. A sentient being has a life that matters to itself. Treating it as a mere instrument ignores exactly what distinguishes it from a chair.

**4. Kant's indirect duties don't convince even those who accept them.** If the only problem with cruelty to animals were that it makes us cruel to humans, torturing an animal in secret, with no effect on anyone, wouldn't be wrong. Almost everyone thinks it is.

**5. A being's value doesn't depend on what it offers.** "An animal is worth what it gives us" describes how society uses it, not what it is. The same sentence was once said about enslaved people.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: dignity is a human concept

The most serious version says "dignity" and "intrinsic value" are concepts built to regulate relations among humans, within a community of moral agents, and that applying them to animals changes the meaning of the words. Animals might deserve care, but not the same kind of respect.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The name doesn't matter; the fact does.** One can argue about whether the word "dignity" fits. There's still someone for whom things go well or badly.
2. **It includes humans who aren't agents.** Babies take part in no moral community and receive full respect (A7).
3. **"Deserving care" already rules out exploitation.** If animals deserve care, breeding them to be killed for the pleasure of taste doesn't pass that test.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Logical | Begging the question | Defines value by the trait one wants to use to exclude |
| Logical | Proves too much | Would exclude humans without rationality |

| Moral | Value measured by usefulness | Confuses what the animal offers with what it is worth |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Protects babies and people with intellectual disabilities.** Without their having the capacity used as the criterion.
2. **Condemns gratuitous cruelty to animals.** If they were mere instruments, cruelty wouldn't be possible.
3. **Treats their own animal as someone with value of its own.** And not as a useful object.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains A20 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In-group bias | Value is attributed exactly to one's own group | [Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29517258/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "only those with capacity X have value in themselves, and the rest may be used".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Babies | They could be used as means, because they don't reason yet | No one accepts this |
| People with profound intellectual disabilities | They would lose intrinsic value | Contradicts human rights |
| A species more rational than us | It could use us as means | Whoever rejects this rejects the premise |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The idea that some beings are valuable in themselves, and not just for their usefulness, is one of ethics' most important achievements. It protects people against being treated as instruments, and the Kantian tradition gave it a powerful formulation.

The mistake is restricting that achievement to one species. If what makes a being an end in itself is having a life that matters to itself, that condition neither begins nor ends at the human boundary.
