# G20. "Eating animals is necessary to survive"

**Thesis:** the word "necessary" only means something with a complement: necessary for whom, where and when. For most people who say it, it isn't: they are choosing between products in a shop, and the alternative is on the same shelf. For people living where the alternative doesn't exist, it genuinely is, and in that case nobody is being accused of anything — the standard definition of veganism already says "as far as is possible and practicable". The argument swaps a real necessity, belonging to a few, for a general justification, used by many. And that is exactly why it turns against whoever uses it: if necessity justifies, the absence of necessity condemns.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND · **Note:** this chapter is about necessity. The specific nutrients are in G1, G2 and G4, and the extreme hypothetical is F1

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Human beings have always eaten meat. That's how we survive. Nobody lasts without it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Causing harm is justifiable when it is necessary for survival.
2. **P2.** Eating animals is necessary for human survival.
3. **C.** So eating animals is justifiable.

P1 is accepted by practically everyone, including those who defend animals. The problem is P2, which is false for most people and true for some, and the argument depends on applying to everyone what holds only for those few.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2. And where P2 is true, the conclusion was already accepted: nobody holds that a person should starve.

## 3. Answer for children

In some very cold or very poor places, people have no other food, and then they really do need it. But in a shop full of rice, beans, lentils, chickpeas and nuts, nobody needs it: you can choose. And when you can choose without hurting anyone, that's the right choice.

## 4. Short answer

Necessary for whom? Whoever says it is usually standing in a supermarket with beans, lentils, chickpeas, rice, nuts and tofu on the next shelf. That isn't necessity, it's preference. Where the alternative really doesn't exist, it is necessity, and nobody is accusing those people: the definition of veganism itself speaks of doing what is possible and practicable. The argument takes the exception and uses it as a general permission.

**Question back:** "If one day it became easy and cheap to live without using animals, would you stop? Because for you that day has already arrived."

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

Everything depends on who is speaking. The same sentence is true in one person's mouth and false in another's.

| Situation | Is it necessary? | What follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person with access to a shop, a market or a varied food basket | No | The choice exists, and it needs justifying |
| A person in food insecurity, eating whatever turns up | It isn't a moral choice of theirs, it's a lack of options | The problem is poverty, and blaming them is unfair |
| Arctic peoples and communities without viable agriculture | Yes, today | Nobody proposes otherwise |
| A rare medical condition that rules out a planned plant-based diet | Depends on the case, and is between the person and their clinician | Outside what is being discussed here |

The argument rolls all of this into one sentence, and uses the last two rows to release the first. It is the same move as the desert-island case (F1): a scenario where the rule genuinely doesn't apply is used to dispense with the rule where it does.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The exception doesn't become the rule.** That someone, somewhere, needs it says nothing about those who don't. It is like saying drink-driving is acceptable because somebody once drove drunk to get an injured person to hospital. Necessity justifies where it exists, and only there.

**2. Veganism already has that clause written in.** The most-used definition, from the Vegan Society, speaks of excluding animal exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". It isn't a concession made under pressure: it has been in the text from the start. Anyone raising the Arctic case is describing exactly the situation the definition already covers.

**3. Well-planned plant-based eating is considered adequate by major nutrition bodies.** The position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States, and of other national bodies, is that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are appropriate for all stages of life, including pregnancy, lactation, childhood and old age, with attention to specific nutrients, above all vitamin B12. Some bodies are more cautious: Germany's DGE, in its 2024 review, makes no recommendation for pregnant women, children and older adults, judging the evidence insufficient — a caveat about the state of the research, not a claim that the diet is unworkable.

**4. The argument turns against whoever uses it.** It concedes the principle: causing harm requires necessity. Anyone who accepts it has already accepted that, where there is no necessity, there is no justification. And for the person saying it in a supermarket, there isn't.

**5. "Surviving" gets swapped for "this is how we live".** Nobody in a city with shops is at risk of death from not eating meat. What is at stake is habit, taste, convenience and identity — real things, but not things called survival.

**6. Where the necessity is real, the target is someone else.** A person who eats whatever turns up because they have no alternative isn't who sustains the industry, and blaming them is wrong on its own account (J2). The argument ends up using the situation of the poorest to protect the consumption of those who do have a choice.

**7. History doesn't establish present necessity.** That the species ate animals over the course of evolution explains how we got here, not what is obligatory now. Otherwise any old practice would be necessary (C5, D1).

### (d) The best reply: necessity isn't only biological

The serious version isn't about immediate survival. It says "necessary" includes what is feasible in a real person's life: price, time, access, food culture, health conditions, family support. For many people a planned plant-based diet is theoretically possible and practically difficult, and demanding it would be demanding what they have no way of delivering.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the thesis, and for the better.** It moves from "eating animals is necessary" to "for some people giving it up is hard", which is true and already in the definition.
2. **Difficulty comes in degrees, and so does the answer.** Someone who can't do everything can do part, and that counts.
3. **It doesn't apply to those who invoke it most.** The objection usually comes from people with access and choice, on behalf of people who have neither.
4. **What it asks for is changing the conditions, not the principle.** If the obstacle is price and access, the conclusion is to make the alternative accessible, not to declare the harm justified.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in G20 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalisation | Extends to everyone a conclusion drawn from a few real cases |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Necessary" shifts from "what you'd die without" to "what I'm used to doing" |
| Logical | Appeal to the extreme case | Uses the Arctic and the desert island to settle the supermarket |
| Moral | Human shield | Protects the consumption of those who choose with the situation of those who can't |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It accepts that vegetarians exist and are alive.** Which already refutes universal necessity.
2. **It picks the cut, the brand and the price.** Behaviour of someone with options, not of someone without.
3. **It says it's necessary and eats for pleasure.** It praises the taste, not the survival.
4. **It agrees that causing harm needs justification.** Which is exactly the principle that condemns it when the necessity isn't there.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up G20 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if someone, in some situation, needs to do something to survive, then doing it is justified for everyone".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stealing food | Since some steal so as not to die, stealing would be justified in general | Nobody accepts this |
| Speeding | Since some speed carrying an injured person, speeding would always be justified | Contrary to law and common sense |
| Any harm | Finding one case of necessity would release the whole practice | Empties the idea of justification |
| The argument itself | If necessity justifies, abundance condemns | The consequence its users don't want |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets right something that can't be brushed aside: there are people and peoples for whom the alternative doesn't exist, because of climate, geography, income or lack of access, and demanding of them what is demanded of someone in a supermarket would be unfair and foolish. It also gets right that "possible" on paper and "possible" in someone's life aren't the same thing, and that a cause which ignores this forfeits the right to be taken seriously.

What it gets wrong is turning that truth into a general permission. The person saying the sentence is almost never the person the sentence is about. And the argument concedes the principle that undoes it: if harm needs necessity to be justified, then where there is no necessity, there is no justification.
