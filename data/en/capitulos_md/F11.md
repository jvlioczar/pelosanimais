# F11. "Would you eat animals if someone paid you well?"

**Thesis:** the question tries to show that veganism is just a preference with a price tag, as if "everyone has a price" proved that values are a façade. But the same dilemma can be put about any human cause, and there the answer is never used to prove the cause is false. Refusing on principle is a coherent answer. Accepting can be too: if the money is large enough to save far more animals, or people, than the harm done, accepting is the choice of someone who takes the victims seriously, not of someone who has abandoned them. Either way, what decides is the harm, not a taboo.

**Family:** F. Borderline and hypothetical cases · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** this chapter is about incentives. The cases with no new victim (an animal who died of natural causes, food headed for the bin) are F9 and F10

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If I gave you a million to eat a steak, you'd eat it, right? So it's not that serious."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Someone who takes a moral value seriously doesn't give it up for money.
2. **P2.** Many vegans admit that, for a very large sum, they would eat meat.
3. **C.** So veganism isn't a serious moral matter but a preference with a price.

P2 is true of some people. The problem is P1, which confuses two completely different reasons for accepting an offer: trading the value for the money, or using the money in the service of the same value.

The argument is **valid** if P1 is read strictly. Its **soundness** fails at P1: accepting an exchange that greatly reduces total harm isn't abandoning the concern about harm. It is applying it.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone offers you lots of money to do something wrong. Many people would say "no, that's wrong". But what if, with that money, you could save a thousand puppies or a thousand children? Then the question gets hard — not because the person doesn't care about what's right, but precisely because they do.

## 4. Short answer

The question works for any cause. "Would you cheat on the person you love with your best friend for a million?" "Would you commit violence against a defenceless, innocent person for a billion?" Nobody uses the answer to prove that fidelity or the refusal of violence are a façade. Refusing is a coherent answer. Accepting can be too: if the money saves far more animals, or people, than the harm of one plate, accepting is taking the victims seriously. What decides is the harm.

**Question back:** "Would you do something you think is wrong to a person if you were paid well? If the answer depends on what would be done with the money, then you understand mine."

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

Whoever answers the question can be in one of three positions, and all of them are consistent with veganism.

| Answer | Reason | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "No, not for any amount" | Integrity: some things aren't done for money | That the value is treated as a limit, not a price |
| "Yes, if the money saved far more lives" | The harm prevented is far greater than the harm done | That the criterion is the harm to victims, not the purity of whoever decides |
| "It depends on what I'd have to do" | Eating meat already produced isn't the same as having an animal killed | That the concrete harm matters more than the label |

The question only works as a trap if it assumes the only "sincere" answer is the first, and that any other proves a lack of seriousness. That assumption is the mistake.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The same dilemma applies to any human cause.** "Would you cheat on the person you love with your best friend for a million?", "commit violence against a defenceless, innocent person for a billion?". Some people will say yes, especially if the money could save many other lives. None of these questions is used to conclude that fidelity or the refusal of violence are preferences with a price. If it proves nothing there, it proves nothing here.

**2. Accepting can be the answer most consistent with veganism.** Veganism isn't a rule of personal purity; it is a response to the harm done to animals. If a very large sum would fund, for example, campaigns, rescues or research that spare thousands of animals, agreeing to eat one plate is trading a small harm for an enormous reduction in harm. Someone who accepts for that reason hasn't abandoned the value: they have applied it.

**3. Refusing is coherent too, from another tradition.** Bernard Williams criticised the idea that adding up consequences is enough, arguing that the integrity of the agent also counts: there are things someone won't do even if the summed outcome would be better. Someone who refuses for that reason holds a serious philosophical position, not a stubborn one.

**4. The debate is old and has a name.** "Earning to give" — working at something bad or indifferent to fund good — is discussed in effective altruism, including important criticism about the risk of getting used to what one does. It is a real question of applied ethics, and it applies equally to human and animal causes.

**5. "Everyone has a price" doesn't tell right from wrong.** That someone would do something for money says something about the person and about the size of the offer, and nothing about whether the act is right. If someone accepted money to assault an innocent person, that wouldn't make the assault acceptable.

**6. The hypothetical doesn't describe the asker's own choice.** Nobody pays meat eaters to eat meat. The real choice is the everyday one, with no money at stake, and there the only benefit is the taste (J5). The extreme case doesn't justify the ordinary one.

### (d) The best reply: the money reveals intensity

The serious version doesn't say veganism is false. It says the answer reveals how much the person really values the cause: if they would accept for a modest sum, their conviction would be weak, and weak convictions shouldn't be pressed on others.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It measures the person, not the argument.** That someone has a weak conviction doesn't say whether the conviction is right.
2. **It confuses price with trade.** Accepting in order to save more victims isn't selling the value cheaply; it is using the offer in its favour.
3. **It would work against any cause.** Almost everyone would do something they disapprove of for a large enough sum, and nobody concludes that nothing matters.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in F11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either refuse at any price, or the value is fake |
| Logical | Proves too much | It would discredit any human cause by the same test |
| Logical | Extreme case as rule | Uses a scenario that doesn't exist to settle the everyday choice |
| Moral | Purity confused with harm | Treats veganism as a personal rule rather than a response to what happens to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It doesn't apply the test to its own convictions.** Nobody concludes their own fidelity is fake because some sum would make them cheat.
2. **It admires people who accept something unpleasant to help others.** But calls the vegan who would do the same inconsistent.
3. **It isn't paid to eat meat.** And eats it, which shows the question doesn't describe the real choice.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if you would give up a value for enough money, the value isn't serious".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fidelity | Someone who would cheat on the person they love for a huge sum wouldn't care about that person | Nobody accepts this |
| Violence against the innocent | Someone who would hurt a defenceless person to save a whole city wouldn't oppose violence | The dilemma is tragic, and doesn't prove the value is fake |
| Fighting racism | Someone who would accept an offensive offer to fund anti-racist work wouldn't be anti-racist | Confuses strategy with conviction |
| Health | Someone who would smoke a cigarette for a million wouldn't care about health | The size of the offer changes the calculation, not the value of health |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets something interesting right: extreme cases help reveal what, exactly, a person values. And it is right that some vegans treat veganism as personal purity rather than as a response to harm, which leaves the position fragile against this kind of question.

But the question doesn't prove what it intends. Refusing on principle is a coherent position, and accepting in order to save far more victims is too, and both take the harm seriously. The same test, applied to any human cause, would discredit none of them. And the choice that really matters is the everyday one, where nobody is paying anybody.
