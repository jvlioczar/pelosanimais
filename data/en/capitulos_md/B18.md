# B18. "The problem is suffering, not use"

**Thesis:** this is the most serious version of welfarism, defended by philosophers such as Alasdair Cochrane: most animals would have no interest in liberty as such, and so using them without making them suffer wouldn't be wrong. Even if granted, the thesis doesn't justify what people buy. Almost all use of animals for food ends in death, and death is a harm even without pain; Cochrane himself condemned intensive farming and, in a later book (2012), concluded that animals raised for food have a right not to be killed. "Use without suffering" describes idealized cases, not the real practices behind meat, milk and eggs. And the thesis is contested from two sides: Gary Francione points out that, as long as they are property, animals' interests tend to yield to profit, and Tom Regan argues that every subject-of-a-life has value of their own and can't be treated merely as a resource.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I don't have a problem with using animals. I have a problem with making them suffer. If the cow lives well, what's wrong with taking the milk?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** What makes what we do to an animal wrong is the harm it causes them.
2. **P2.** Most animals don't form or revise a plan of life; for them, liberty matters for the well-being it brings, not in itself.
3. **P3.** So using an animal without making them suffer and without setting back their well-being doesn't harm them.
4. **C.** So using animals isn't wrong in itself; what's wrong is making them suffer.

This is the position defended by the political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane in *An Introduction to Animals and Political Theory* (2010, pp. 67, 108) and developed in *Animal Rights Without Liberation* (2012). For him, keeping a dog or riding a horse doesn't necessarily restrict the animal's agency in a harmful way, and keeping an animal who isn't autonomous differs from enslaving an autonomous human. P1 is accepted by almost every side of the debate. P2 is contested, but has a serious defense. The problem lies in P3 and in the leap from the conclusion to real practices: "harm" isn't limited to suffering, and real use is almost never the harmless use of the premise.

The argument is **valid**. As a thesis about idealized cases, it's a respectable and debated academic position. As a defense of ordinary consumption, it **isn't sound**: P3 ignores the harm of death, and real practices don't meet the condition the thesis itself requires.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone says: "I can take your toy, as long as you don't cry." Even if you didn't cry, the toy would still be taken from you. It's similar with animals: even when an animal doesn't suffer much on the farm, in the end they're almost always killed, and lose the life they still had ahead. Not causing suffering is important, but it isn't everything.

## 4. Short answer

I agree suffering is central. But almost all use of animals for food ends in death, and dying young means losing the whole life still to come, with or without pain. Even the philosopher who defends this idea, Alasdair Cochrane, condemned intensive farming and later concluded that animals raised for food have a right not to be killed. "Use without suffering" describes an imaginary farm. In reality, milk requires separating calves and slaughtering cows, and eggs come from hatcheries that cull male chicks. If the problem is harm, the question is whether what you buy causes harm. It almost always does.

**Question to ask back:** "If the only problem is suffering, do you know of any meat, milk or eggs on sale that come from an animal who wasn't killed or harmed?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument joins a philosophical thesis and a practical conclusion that don't follow from each other:

| Level | What the argument says | What actually follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Philosophical thesis | Using an animal isn't wrong in itself; harm is what's wrong | Can be seriously debated |
| Idealized case | Use without suffering, without death and without setting back well-being would be acceptable | Perhaps, in narrow cases (see F9) |
| Real practice | Buying meat, milk and eggs would be acceptable | Doesn't follow: these practices involve death and harms the thesis itself condemns |

The thesis is contested by abolitionists such as Gary Francione and by rights theorists such as Tom Regan, and Cochrane himself summarizes that challenge in the book (2010, pp. 66–67). But even accepted in full, it doesn't reach the plate.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Death is a harm, even without suffering.** Killing a young animal takes away all the good experiences they still would have had (B2). Almost all use of animals for food ends in early death. In 2010, Cochrane himself said that killing animals to eat them is a hard case, calling for careful consideration of the strength of the interests at stake (p. 144), and in *Animal Rights Without Liberation* (2012) he concluded that animals raised for food have a right not to suffer and not to be killed; milk and eggs would only be acceptable if no animal were killed or made to suffer. The thesis "the problem is suffering" is therefore not the thesis "killing for food is acceptable", not even for the person who formulated it.

**2. Real use isn't idealized use.** Cochrane admits that current production makes animals suffer terribly and presents harmless use as a possibility, not a description (2010, p. 108), and he concludes in favor of ending intensive farming (p. 143). Outside it, the problems remain: free-range animals are also killed young (B4); milk production separates calves and slaughters cows when their yield falls, and egg production culls billions of male chicks a year (B5). Using the imaginary case to defend the real practice is swapping one for the other.

**3. Property pulls use toward harm.** For Francione, as long as animals are property, their interests will be weighed against the owner's economic interest and will tend to lose. Cochrane replies that property isn't the decisive obstacle: wild animals, who belong to no one, are also mistreated; corporations are property and have rights; and no owner has absolute power over what they own (2010, pp. 67–68; see A24). The debate remains open in theory, and other authors, such as the philosopher Eva Meijer, also dispute the idea that animals have no interest in liberty. In practice, the history of welfare rules supports Francione's worry: protections tend to stop where costs begin (B10).

**4. There are interests beyond pain.** For Regan, every "subject-of-a-life" has inherent value that can't be reduced to their usefulness to others, and so can't be treated merely as a means (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 45, 66). Cochrane objects that agency isn't the Kantian autonomy that grounds such respect (p. 67). Even without settling that dispute, premise P2 has a cost: if liberty matters for the well-being it brings, then restricting movement, company and natural behavior harms, because those things are part of well-being. Preference tests show that animals work for space, nests and company (B17).

**5. Whoever judges whether there's harm is whoever profits from the use.** The thesis depends on knowing, case by case, whether a use harms. In commercial practice, that judgment is made by the producer, who gains from the answer "it doesn't harm". This doesn't refute the thesis, but it shows why, on its own, it protects little.

**6. "Just don't be cruel" doesn't answer whether the use is permitted.** Many versions of this thesis only ask that the use be carried out without cruelty. Tom Regan, criticizing what he called the "cruelty-kindness" view, observed that cruelty and kindness concern motives and character: a person is cruel when they lack sympathy for another's suffering or take pleasure in it. The absence of cruelty doesn't guarantee that the act is permitted, just as a kind motive doesn't guarantee it is right (Regan, 1985; cited by Nobis, 2016, p. 14). A caring farmer who dislikes seeing animals suffer can still confine them, separate them from their young and send them to slaughter young. Knowing whether this is permitted requires another question: what the use does to the animal, and whether there is sufficient reason for it (points 1 and 4; see A25).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: harmless uses exist, such as companionship

The most serious version points to concrete cases: a dog living well with a family, a horse ridden with care, a rescued hen whose eggs are left over (F9). If none of that is wrong, then use as such isn't the problem, and what matters is how the animal is treated.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The cases show narrow exceptions, not the rule.** Even if these uses are acceptable, the conditions that make them acceptable, with no death, no sale and no breeding for use, are exactly what's missing from the meat, milk and eggs on the market.
2. **Living with an animal isn't the same as breeding them to kill.** Many animal rights advocates, abolitionists included, adopt rescued animals. What they reject is breeding for consumption, not living together.
3. **The harm criterion cuts both ways.** If what matters is not harming, it condemns everything that harms, and the death of young animals harms.
4. **The practical question is what you buy.** The debate about ideal use can go on among philosophers; the daily decision is about products that come from animals who were killed and harmed.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B18 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Use" shifts from ideal, harmless use to real use, with death |
| Logical | Generalizing from the ideal case | An imaginary case answers for real practices |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the practices | Assumes free of suffering or death what involves both |
| Moral | Harm reduced to suffering | Ignores the harm of deprivation caused by death |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the problem is suffering.** And buys products from systems that cause suffering.
2. **Condemns industrial farming.** And consumes what comes from it, which is almost everything on sale.
3. **Accepts that killing a person painlessly is a grave wrong.** And assumes killing an animal painlessly isn't harm.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "using someone who isn't autonomous, without making them suffer, isn't wrong".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A rescued hen whose eggs are left over | Eating the eggs might be acceptable | A debated case, with narrow conditions (F9) |
| An animal raised free-range and slaughtered young | It would be acceptable | Doesn't follow: death is a harm (B2) |
| A human without autonomy, such as a baby | Could be used for others' ends, as long as painlessly | No one accepts this; shows there are interests beyond pain |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is right to put suffering at the center and to recognize that intensive farming is unjustifiable, which already condemns most of what is produced. It also raises a legitimate philosophical question: whether there are ways of living with animals that don't harm them. There are good arguments on both sides, and the debate among Cochrane, Francione and Regan is far from settled.

But the thesis, taken seriously, is demanding: it accepts only use that doesn't harm, and death harms. In the real practices behind meat, milk and eggs, that condition isn't met. So even those who accept the thesis have reason to reject what's on sale.
