# J19. "Veganism has become big business"

**Thesis:** there is indeed a market for vegan products, with marketing, high prices and meat companies among the owners. In fact, not every plant-based business has vegan owners: many of the big brands of processed plant-based products belong to meat, dairy and general food companies, such as JBS, Nestlé, Danone and Tyson, for which the plant-based line is often just a diversification or niche strategy. But the fact that someone profits from an idea says nothing about whether it is true or false. Veganism is the principle of not exploiting animals, not a product catalog: rice, beans and vegetables aren't a luxury niche. And if the problem is that there's a business, the business of exploiting animals is incomparably larger. In 2024 a single meat company took in more than twice the entire world's retail sales of plant-based alternatives. And if the subject is lobbying or manipulation, the meat and dairy industry is among the most powerful: a 2023 study estimated that, in the United States, animal-source products receive about 800 times more public money and 190 times more lobbying spending than the alternatives. The question isn't who sells, but what happens to the animals.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's just a business now. It's marketing to sell expensive plant burgers to people with money."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There is now an industry of vegan products, with brands, labels, investors and advertising.
2. **P2.** Many of those products are expensive and aimed at higher-income consumers.
3. **P3.** Many of the companies that sell them are the same ones that sell meat, and a vegan label doesn't guarantee an ethical supply chain.
4. **P4.** A movement driven by commercial interests doesn't deserve moral trust.
5. **C.** So veganism needn't be taken seriously as an ethical position.

P1, P2 and P3 are largely true. The problem lies in P4 and the conclusion: the origin or commercial use of an idea doesn't settle whether it is right, and veganism isn't reducible to the products sold in its name.

The argument is **invalid**: from "someone profits from this" it doesn't follow that "this is false".

## 3. Answer for children

Some shops sell T-shirts with a cute animal and a nice slogan just to make money. That doesn't mean caring for animals is a lie. Caring for animals is one thing, selling T-shirts is another. And to stop eating animals, no one needs to buy anything expensive: rice, beans, bananas and carrots will do. Often the company selling the plant burger is the same one that sells meat. Those who sell meat and milk advertise a lot too, even more, and that doesn't stop us from thinking about what happens to the animals.

## 4. Short answer

There is business around veganism, true, and there are expensive products I don't even buy. But an idea doesn't become wrong because someone makes money from it: there's business around health, education and recycling too. Veganism means not exploiting animals, and you can do that with rice and beans. And much of that business isn't even vegan-owned: many famous plant-based brands belong to meat and dairy companies such as JBS, Nestlé and Danone. If profit is the problem, the meat business is far bigger: a single meat company takes in more than twice the world's entire retail market for plant-based alternatives. If the worry is manipulation, advertising exists in every industry, and meat and dairy advertising is far bigger and comes with far more lobbying. In the end, the question isn't who sells, but what happens to the animals.

**Question to ask back:** "If tomorrow no one made a profit from vegan products, would killing animals for food become more right or more wrong than it is today?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The criticism comes from several directions. In Brazil, criticisms like this appear in the comments of figures such as the philosopher and newspaper columnist Luiz Felipe Pondé, who said on a radio show in 2019 that veganism today is "business, like Louis Vuitton, usually for people with money, and that's fine". He added that he isn't against veganism but sees a moralistic streak in it (the subject of L8).

The argument mixes three things under one name:

| What gets called "veganism" | What it actually is | Does the "business" criticism reach it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ethical position | Refusing, as far as possible and practicable, the exploitation of animals | No |
| The everyday diet | Grains, pulses, vegetables, fruit, nuts | No: these are ordinary, cheap foods |
| The plant-based market | Processed plant-based meats, cheeses and milks, many from companies that aren't vegan | Yes, in part |

The criticism applies to the third row and is presented as if it brought down the first.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a genetic fallacy, with guilt by association.** Judging an idea by who profits from it swaps examining the idea for examining its company. There are businesses around vaccines, schoolbooks and solar panels, and none of that shows that vaccinating, studying or using clean energy is a mistake.

**2. Veganism isn't a catalog.** The Vegan Society, the British organization that coined the term, defines veganism as a philosophy and way of living that seeks to exclude, as far as is possible and practicable, the exploitation of animals (L25). Someone who eats rice, beans, potatoes and greens and has never bought a plant-based burger is as vegan as anyone else. A 2021 study of 150 countries found that vegetarian and vegan diets based on ordinary foods were among the cheapest in high-income and upper-middle-income countries (J1; on access, see J2).

**3. The business of exploiting animals is far bigger.** According to the Good Food Institute, using data from the market research firm Euromonitor, worldwide retail sales of plant-based meat, seafood, milk, yogurt, ice cream and cheese totaled 28.6 billion US dollars in 2024, of which 6.1 billion was plant-based meat and seafood. In the same year JBS alone, the world's largest meat company, reported net revenue of 77.2 billion dollars. Beyond Meat, one of the best-known vegan brands, took in 326.5 million. The world produces more than 360 million tonnes of meat a year, and the sector receives far more public money and does far more lobbying than the alternatives (J14).

**4. If the subject is lobbying and manipulation, the meat industry is far more powerful.** The phrase "the vegan industry is trying to manipulate you" assumes a large, influential industry. Marketing exists in every sector, and the plant-based one advertises too; but it's small next to meat and dairy. A 2023 study in the journal *One Earth*, by Stanford researchers, estimated that in the United States animal-source products received about 800 times more public money and 190 times more lobbying spending than the alternatives; in the European Union, about 1,200 times more public money and three times more lobbying. In the United States, mandatory promotion programs, the checkoffs, charge a fee on sales of animals and products to fund advertising and research: the beef checkoff alone, funded by one dollar per head of cattle sold, planned to invest about 38.1 million dollars in 2026, and there are similar programs for dairy and pork. In the European Union, a Greenpeace analysis estimated that 32% of the EU's agricultural promotion budget from 2016 to 2020, about 252 million euros, went to campaigns exclusively for meat and dairy. In Brazil, the Parliamentary Agricultural Front, which represents agribusiness in general and not only livestock, had 358 members of Congress in 2023 and is regarded as one of the most influential caucuses. None of this shows that eating meat is wrong, just as vegan marketing doesn't show that veganism is right. It only shows that, if commercial influence were a reason to distrust an idea, the first to fall would be the idea that we need meat. The question isn't who sells, but what happens to the animals.

**5. Not every plant-based business has vegan owners.** Many of the largest brands of processed plant-based products belong to companies that aren't vegan and that keep profiting from animals. Unilever bought the Dutch brand The Vegetarian Butcher in 2018 and sold it in 2025 to Vivera, which belongs to JBS, the world's largest meat company. In Brazil, the Incrível line, launched in 2019, belongs to Seara, also part of JBS. Nestlé sells its Garden Gourmet line in Europe, and pulled it from UK and Irish supermarkets in 2023, calling it not viable in that market. Danone, one of the world's largest dairy companies, has owned Alpro and Silk since it bought WhiteWave in 2017. Tyson, the American chicken and beef giant, launched Raised & Rooted in 2019, whose first burger blended beef with plant protein. For these companies, the plant-based line is often a diversification or niche strategy, which can be expanded or dropped depending on sales. So the phrase "veganism has become big business" describes, to a large extent, a business of meat and dairy companies. That shows that companies follow demand, not that the demand is fake. Anyone who dislikes funding those companies has two simple ways out: eat food that has no brand or, among similar options, prefer vegan businesses. That is a reasonable preference, which strengthens those already working towards the same goal; but it isn't a rule, because the vegan product from an ordinary company also expands supply and shows that the demand exists.

**6. The vegan label has limits, and vegans know it.** A product can contain no animal ingredients and still have labor or environmental problems in its supply chain. That calls for more attention, not for a return to animal products, which have the same problems plus the killing of the animals (K5).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the market hollows out the cause

The most serious version doesn't say veganism is false. It says that, by becoming a marketable lifestyle, it loses force: a person buys a plant-based burger, feels they've done their part and stops there. The cause becomes a consumer identity for those who can pay, and the animals drop out of the picture.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a criticism of a deviation, not of the principle.** Many vegans make the same criticism. It calls for a veganism centered more on animals and less on products.
2. **Products also reduce harm.** Someone who swaps meat for a plant-based substitute, for whatever reason, stops paying for slaughter. The effect on the animal doesn't depend on the purity of the intention (L18).
3. **Every cause that grows attracts commerce.** It happened with environmentalism, with healthy eating and with rights movements. In no case did the cause become wrong because of it.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Suppose the entire vegan market is opportunistic. Animals still feel pain and fear, and are still killed by the billions. The question about them isn't answered by any company's balance sheet. Anyone who distrusts the market can ignore it completely and still stop eating animals. What justifies killing them is a question that stands with or without a shelf of vegan products.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J19 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges the idea by who profits from it |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Links veganism to luxury brands and meat companies |
| Logical | Equivocation | Calls the plant-based product market "veganism" |
| Logical | Double standard | Sees manipulation in vegan marketing, but not in meat and dairy marketing, which is far bigger |
| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Looks at the vegan sector's profit and ignores the meat sector's |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the rule to what they consume.** Meat is a business too, with much bigger marketing, brands and lobbying.
2. **Credits vegans with the profits of meat companies.** Many of the best-selling plant-based brands belong to companies such as JBS, Nestlé and Danone.
3. **Says "and that's fine".** If there's no problem with its being a business, the fact can't serve as an objection.
4. **Criticizes the price of vegan products.** But doesn't count the rice, beans and vegetables already on their plate.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there's a profitable market around an idea, the idea doesn't deserve to be taken seriously".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar energy | Caring about the climate would be just marketing | No one concludes this |
| The gym and supplement industry | Exercise wouldn't be good for you | The business doesn't change the fact |
| Recycling | Sorting waste would be pointless, because companies profit from scrap | No one concludes this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that there is a market, that it advertises, that many products are expensive and that part of vegan communication looks like a shop window for people with money. It's right that a vegan label doesn't guarantee a clean supply chain and that there is opportunism from companies that go on profiting from slaughter. It's a criticism the movement would do well to hear.

But it reaches the market, not the principle. Ceasing to exploit animals doesn't require buying anything special, and what happens to animals doesn't change according to who profits. If the existence of a business disqualified a practice, the first to fall would be animal farming.
