# K6. "Vegans should fight for laws, not diets"

**Thesis:** it's a false dichotomy. Institutional change, such as laws, rules and agreements with companies, can reach many animals at once, and deserves investment. But diet and laws aren't alternatives: as long as consumers pay for the products, demand sustains animal farming, and laws only pass and last when they have public support. All forms of activism reinforce each other, and personal consistency gives credibility to those calling for change. And those who say change must only be political can be asked the same question: are they supporting the laws they say they prefer? Often, the argument serves to do neither one nor the other (E2).

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Diet is individualism. Do politics instead of pestering people."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Change at scale depends on laws, public policy, court rulings and company commitments.
2. **P2.** These changes reach many animals at once. The European Union banned conventional cages for laying hens from 2012, and cage-free campaigns led many companies to commit to changing their suppliers; a 2019 estimate calculated that these campaigns affected between 9 and 120 years of chicken life per dollar spent.
3. **P3.** Individual consumer choices have a small impact compared with that.
4. **P4.** Activists' time and energy are limited.
5. **C.** So vegans should focus their efforts on politics and institutions, not on diets.

P1 and P2 are true, and the available evidence does suggest that institutional change is one of the most effective forms of activism. P3 is partly true, and so is P4. The problem lies in the conclusion: not eating animals doesn't take time away from political action, laws depend on those who support them, and consumer demand keeps sustaining animal farming.

The argument is **valid** as a strategic argument about where to focus activism. It fails as a personal excuse for eating animals, and it also fails when it treats diet and laws as alternatives.

## 3. Answer for children

You can do both: choose food that doesn't hurt animals and also ask leaders to make laws that protect animals. One helps the other. Laws only change when lots of people care, and people who look after animals every day show others that it's possible to live that way.

## 4. Short answer

Changing laws and rules can help many animals at once, and it's worth doing. But diet and laws aren't alternatives. Not eating animals takes no time away from political action. As long as people buy meat, demand sustains animal farming, and a law that improves cages doesn't stop animals from being raised and killed. Laws also need public support to pass and to last: in Brazil, a Supreme Court ruling against vaquejada, a rodeo-style event, was overturned the following year by a constitutional amendment. And those calling for change are more credible when they live by it. If you think laws are the way, you can support both.

**Question to ask back:** "Would you support laws to protect farmed animals, if they were proposed?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument presents two actions as mutually exclusive:

| Action | What it does | Does it take time from the other? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No longer eating animals | Removes demand, shows change is possible and gives consistency to those who defend animals | No: it's an everyday choice |
| Fighting for laws, rules and agreements with companies | Reaches many animals at once | Not prevented by individual choice |

What the argument gets right is the scale of institutional change. What it gets wrong is treating it as a substitute for personal change, rather than a partner.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a false dichotomy.** Changing one's own consumption and fighting for political change aren't alternatives. Not eating animals is a choice made at meals, and it doesn't take up the time devoted to campaigns, petitions or votes.

**2. Consumers sustain demand.** Laws regulate how animals are raised, but it's purchases that keep them being raised. Even with the ban on conventional cages in the European Union, hens are still exploited and killed, and the animals the law doesn't reach keep existing as long as someone pays for them (K1). Changing the law without changing demand leaves most of the problem in place.

**3. Laws need public support to pass and to last.** In 2016, Brazil's Supreme Federal Court ruled, by 6 votes to 5, that a law from the state of Ceará regulating vaquejada was unconstitutional because of its cruelty to animals. The following year, Congress passed Constitutional Amendment 96, which treats such practices as cultural expressions, and the Court later upheld the amendment. An institutional victory without organized support in society can be undone by those with more support. Without people who care about animals, there's no one to push for new laws or to demand that they be enforced.

**4. Personal consistency gives credibility.** Those who call for laws against the exploitation of animals while still paying for it hand their opponents an easy argument. Those who live by what they defend show that change is possible and that the conviction is serious.

**5. All forms of activism reinforce each other.** One-to-one conversations form the people who later support laws and campaigns; laws and agreements with companies change the environment and make personal change easier; and changes in consumption create a market for alternatives, which makes new policies more viable. That doesn't mean they all weigh the same, and there may be good reasons to concentrate more effort on one of them, but the choice should come from research on what works, not from personal preference. The guide covers this in [Vegans should focus only on institutional activism, changing laws](../stop-saying/vegans-should-focus-only-on-institutional-activism-changing-laws.html), one of the things vegans should stop saying.

**6. The argument often serves to do nothing.** The useful question is whether the person using it supports the legal changes they say they prefer. If they do, great, and nothing stops them from also changing their own consumption (E2).

**7. Individual choice has a real effect.** Each purchase has an expected effect on production (K1).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: focusing on consumption is a trap

The most serious version says that "conscious consumption" talk shifts onto individuals a responsibility that belongs to companies and governments, and that the vegan movement sometimes falls into that trap. If institutions change the lives of millions of animals at once, every hour spent discussing someone's plate would be an hour less where the effect is greatest.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The criticism is of an exclusive focus, not of individual choice.** It may make sense for activists to devote more time to institutions. That doesn't mean they, or anyone else, should keep paying for exploitation.
2. **No longer paying for deaths isn't luxury "conscious consumption".** It's refusing to take part in a harm.
3. **Institutions don't change on their own.** They depend on public support, and that support is made of people who have been convinced, as the vaquejada case shows.
4. **The question to the individual remains.** Even if laws are the main route.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Campaigning for laws for animals is great, and it's consistent with not eating them. The moral question doesn't wait for the laws: until they change, what justifies paying for animals to be killed?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either laws or diet |
| Logical | Practical contradiction | Calls for laws, but opposes them |
| Epistemological | Incomplete model of social change | Assumes laws pass and last without public support |
| Moral | Delegating responsibility | Change would be up to others only |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't support laws protecting farmed animals.** In most cases.
2. **Says the state can't ban what they eat.** While calling for laws.
3. **Makes individual choices on other matters.** Without thinking that prevents politics.
4. **Recognizes that laws depend on support.** But isn't part of that support.

The principle being tested is: "if a change must be political, there's no individual duty".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Racism | No one would need to change personal attitudes, only laws | No one accepts this |
| Pollution | No one would need to reduce their own pollution | No one accepts this |
| Animals | No one would need to change their plate | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Political and structural changes are essential, and the animal movement should invest in them. Protection laws, agreements with companies, an end to harmful subsidies and transition policies can reach far more animals than isolated individual choices, and the available evidence suggests that an institutional focus is one of the most effective forms of activism.

But fighting for laws doesn't excuse personal consistency, and personal consistency doesn't prevent fighting for laws. Laws need people who support them to pass and to last, and consumer demand keeps sustaining animal farming. Each front strengthens the other, and neither justifies continuing to pay for animals to be killed.
