# D6. "Everybody eats meat"

**Thesis:** a practice being popular doesn't prove it's right. Slavery was accepted by the majority for centuries; women's suffrage was once defended by a minority. Every moral change began as a minority position. That almost everyone eats meat explains why it seems normal, not why it would be just.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Almost everyone eats meat. You're a minority. If it were wrong, nobody would do it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The vast majority of people, in almost every culture, eat animals.
2. **P2.** Practices accepted by almost everyone tend to reflect shared moral intuitions.
3. **P3.** Shared moral intuitions are good evidence of what is right.
4. **C.** So eating animals is probably not wrong.

The strong version has something reasonable about it: consensus sometimes points to accumulated wisdom. The problem lies in P3, which history often refutes, especially when the majority benefits from the practice.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3: consensus doesn't guarantee correctness, and it's especially unreliable when it favors those who take part in it.

## 3. Answer for children

Has it ever happened that the whole class laughed at someone and you thought it was wrong? Lots of people doing something doesn't mean it's right. Sometimes the first to notice that something hurts is just one person. And with time, the others can notice too.

## 4. Short answer

Lots of people doing something doesn't prove it's right. Slavery was once accepted by the majority, and the first people who fought for women's suffrage were a minority. Every moral change started with a few. That almost everyone eats meat explains why it seems normal, not why it would be just. Besides, the majority benefits from the practice, and the animals, who pay for it, don't get a vote.

**Question to ask back:** "Can you think of a practice the majority accepted that we now think is wrong?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses three things:

| Claim | Type |
| --- | --- |
| Many people eat meat | Statistical fact |
| So it seems normal | Psychological fact |
| So it's right | A moral conclusion that doesn't follow |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the appeal to popularity.** An idea's popularity isn't evidence that it's true or just. It's one of the best-known fallacies.

**2. History refutes the consensus.** Slavery, the exclusion of women from politics and many forms of discrimination were accepted by the majority for centuries. Change came from minorities who questioned the consensus.

**3. Consensus is suspect when it favors those who take part in it.** Those who eat meat have an interest in eating meat being acceptable. The animals, who pay the price, don't take part in the consensus.

**4. "Normal" is one of the most used justifications.** Research on how people justify eating meat shows "it's normal" among the four most common reasons, alongside "natural", "necessary" and "nice".

**5. Social conformity is powerful.** Classic psychology experiments show people tend to follow the group even when the group is clearly wrong.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Piazza et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25865663/) | "Normal" is one of the four justifications most used for eating meat | The 4Ns: natural, normal, necessary and nice |
| [Asch, *Studies of independence and conformity* (1956)](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0093718) | People tend to follow the group even in the face of clearly wrong answers | *Psychological Monographs*; the experiments began in 1951 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: consensus carries some weight

The most serious version doesn't say consensus proves anything, but that it should count as some evidence, and that whoever goes against the majority needs strong arguments.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It shifts the burden, but not the answer.** The strong arguments exist, and the rest of this guide presents them.
2. **The weight of consensus shrinks when there's self-interest.** A consensus formed by those who benefit is worth less as evidence.
3. **The scientific consensus points elsewhere.** Scientists recognize animal sentience, and nutrition bodies recognize that well-planned vegan diets are adequate.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to popularity | Popularity becomes proof |
| Epistemological | Self-interested consensus | Treats an agreement among beneficiaries as neutral |
| Moral | Normality as justification | The habitual seems right |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't follow the majority on everything.** Almost everyone holds minority opinions on some issue.
2. **Admires those who went against the majority in the past.** Abolitionists and suffragists were a minority.
3. **Would they change their mind if the majority changed?** If the criterion is the majority, yes, which shows it isn't a moral criterion.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what the majority does is right".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slavery in the eighteenth century | It would have been right, because the majority accepted it | No one accepts this |
| Excluding women from voting | It would have been right as long as the majority supported it | No one accepts this |
| Any majority prejudice | It would be just | The principle legitimizes any popular oppression |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that eating meat is nearly universal, and that this makes veganism seem strange to many people. Social consensus also has some practical importance: changing habits is easier when the group changes together.

But how common a practice is explains why it seems normal, not why it would be just. Moral history is full of majorities that were wrong and minorities who saw it first.
