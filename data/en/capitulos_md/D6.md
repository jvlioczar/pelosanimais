# D6. "It's cultural; criticizing it is ethnocentric"

**Thesis:** respecting cultures doesn't mean accepting everything that happens in them. People who use this argument rarely apply it to every practice: almost no one would say child marriage or female genital mutilation are beyond criticism because they're traditional in some places. And cultures aren't fixed blocks: within each one there are people who question their own practices, as the debates over bullfighting in Spain, Colombia and Mexico show, and veganism mostly criticizes the speaker's own dominant culture, not other people's.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You can't judge other cultures. This is part of our identity. That's cultural imperialism."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Different cultures have different values and practices.
2. **P2.** Historically, criticizing other cultures often served to justify domination.
3. **P3.** There's no neutral point of view from which to judge all cultures.
4. **C.** So criticizing cultural practices involving animals is ethnocentric.

P1 and P2 are true and important. Colonialism used "civilization" as an excuse. The problem is moving from there to the idea that no cultural practice can be criticized.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, taken to the extreme, and in the conclusion, which would make it impossible to criticize any practice, including those the person arguing condemns.

## 3. Answer for children

Every place has its own festivals, foods and customs, and it's good to learn about them and respect them. But if a custom hurts someone, we can talk about it, even if it's a tradition. Many places have already changed customs that hurt people or animals, and they're still themselves.

## 4. Short answer

Respecting a culture isn't accepting everything in it. You may well criticize dogfighting or eating dogs in other countries. If you do, you don't really believe no cultural practice can be judged either. Cultures also change from within: Colombia passed a law in 2024 banning bullfighting from 2027, and Mexico City banned wounding or killing bulls in the ring in 2025. Think too of practices that harm women and children, like child marriage and female genital mutilation: they have been or still are cultural in some places, and they are recognized internationally as human rights violations. And veganism mainly criticizes our own culture, the dominant one, yours and mine, not that of some distant people.

**Question to ask back:** "If a practice that harms women and children were cultural, would criticizing it be ethnocentric?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument is applied selectively:

| Practice | People who use the argument tend to say |
| --- | --- |
| Barbecues, rodeos, bullfights, fishing | "It's cultural; don't criticize it" |
| Eating dog meat in other countries | "It's cruel" |
| Cockfighting, dogfighting | "It's cruelty; it should be banned" |
| Female genital mutilation, child marriage | "It's a human rights violation" |

If the argument held, it would hold for all of them. Since it isn't applied to all of them, the real criterion isn't "it's cultural" but "it's my culture".

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Selective relativism.** Almost no one believes every cultural practice is beyond criticism. The argument tends to appear to protect the practices of whoever is arguing, not those of others.

**2. Many societies have already changed their own traditions involving animals.** In 1835 the United Kingdom banned cockfighting and the baiting of bulls and bears with dogs, and cockfighting is now banned in every US state. Bullfighting shows how this debate happens within cultures themselves. In 2010, the Parliament of Catalonia banned it; in 2016, Spain's Constitutional Court annulled the ban, holding that a region may regulate bullfighting but not ban it, and even so there were no more bullfights in Catalonia after 2011, at least up to 2020. Colombia passed a law in 2024 banning bullfighting from 2027, and in 2025 Mexico City banned the use of lances, banderillas and swords and the killing of the bull. Elsewhere bullfighting remains legal, as in most of Spain, in Portugal, in the regions of southern France with an unbroken tradition and in Peru, where the Constitutional Court upheld in 2020 the legal exemption for bullfights and cockfights. In Brazil, the Supreme Federal Court banned the *farra do boi*, in 1997, and cockfighting, for violating the constitutional ban on cruelty (D1). On both sides of these debates are people from the same culture.

**3. Cultures also change where women and children are concerned.** Child marriage and female genital mutilation have been or still are cultural practices in some places, and today they are recognized internationally as human rights violations. According to the WHO, more than 230 million girls and women alive today have undergone genital mutilation, practiced in about 30 countries in Africa, the Middle East and Asia; the organization notes that some religious leaders support abandoning the practice and that many health workers are becoming advocates for this among families and communities. According to UNICEF, child marriage has declined over the past decade but remains widespread: about one in five girls worldwide is married in childhood. Criticizing these practices isn't despising the peoples among whom they occur. As the political scientist Gerry Mackie showed, practices like these tend to end when families and communities themselves decide, together, to abandon them (D5).

**4. Cultures aren't blocks.** Within every culture there are debates, disagreements and change. Many traditions around the world include vegetarianism, respect for animals or meat-free practices.

**5. The criticism is mostly internal.** Vegans mainly criticize the dominant culture of their own country, to which they themselves belong. That isn't ethnocentrism; it's self-criticism.

**6. The criterion is the victim, not the origin.** What matters isn't where the practice comes from, but whether it harms someone who didn't consent.

**7. Not even within a country is there a single attitude.** The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane gives two examples. In the West, it's often said that the Chinese are cruel to animals because of bear bile farms, where bears are kept in tiny cages so their bile can be extracted. But the practice also shocks many Chinese people, and China has old traditions of compassion: Chinese Buddhism has been associated with vegetarianism for centuries, the monk Dao Xuan (596–667) was known for removing lice from his body and placing them where no one would step on them, and the monk Xu Yun (1840–1959) preached to animals (Cochrane, 2010, p. 78). Bear bile farming remains legal in China; World Animal Protection estimates that more than 20,000 bears live on these farms, and other estimates are lower. In the United Kingdom, the 2004 ban on hunting with dogs divided the country: both sides claimed to speak for British tradition (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 79–80). When someone says "it's our culture", the question is: which part of it?

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the problem is who criticizes and how

The most serious version says the problem isn't criticizing, but who criticizes and how: campaigns by rich countries against the practices of poor peoples or minorities reproduce colonial relationships, and veganism sometimes does this.

In political philosophy, the strongest defense of this position is the particularism of the philosopher Michael Walzer, in *Spheres of Justice* (1983): a society is just when it lives faithfully to the shared understandings of its members. Walzer uses precisely the example of eating dogs and cats, taboo in some societies and accepted in others, and concludes that imposing one standard on others would be disrespectful. A more moderate version is the contextual universalism of the political theorist Bhikhu Parekh, in *Rethinking Multiculturalism* (2000): there are universal values, but each society interprets them in its own way (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 76–77).

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It criticizes the form, not the content.** One can defend animals without disrespecting peoples and cultures.
2. **It doesn't apply to those who consume the dominant culture.** A suburban barbecue isn't the practice of an oppressed minority.
3. **The movement itself recognizes this.** Veganism defines itself as avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" (D7).
4. **Particularism shuts down criticism further than anyone accepts.** If every society is just when it follows its own understandings, practices that discriminate against women or minorities would also be beyond criticism. Contextual universalism has a limit: not every practice is a local interpretation of a universal principle. A society that outlaws homosexuality doesn't have its own version of non-discrimination; it follows a different principle. The same goes for practices that wholly ignore animal suffering (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 77–78).
5. **It still has to say which value is the authentic one.** Every society has norms that pull in different directions. To choose between compassion and indifference without appealing to a universal principle, one would have to prove that one of them is more "authentic", and that is usually impossible (Cochrane, 2010, p. 79).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to tradition | Cultural origin becomes a justification |
| Logical | Selective application | The principle only applies to the practices of whoever is arguing |
| Epistemological | Culture seen as a fixed block | Ignores diversity and change within cultures |
| Moral | Selective relativism | Refuses to judge only what is convenient |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Condemns other peoples' cultural practices.** Like eating dogs or whaling.
2. **Accepts the bans on cockfighting and bull-baiting.** Which were cultural practices.
3. **Changes their own culture all the time.** Eating habits have changed enormously in recent decades.
4. **Condemns cultural practices that harm women and children.** Almost no one defends child marriage on the grounds that it's cultural.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "cultural practices can't be criticized by outsiders".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bullfighting, cockfighting | They couldn't be banned | They were banned in several places, by decision of people from the same culture |
| Slavery | It couldn't have been criticized in societies that practiced it | Absurd |
| Child marriage and female genital mutilation | They couldn't be judged | They are recognized internationally as human rights violations |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Concern about ethnocentrism is legitimate and important. History shows cultural criticism was often used to justify domination, and animal advocates should take care not to treat other cultures with contempt or pick easy targets among minorities.

But respecting cultures doesn't make everything that happens within them untouchable. The right question isn't whose practice it is, but who pays for it.
