# D8. "Barbecues bring the family together"

**Thesis:** what brings the family together is the gathering, not the animal. The affection, the conversation, the music and the full table carry on with other food. Many families already hold barbecues with plant-based skewers, and the gathering is still a gathering. The real value of the tradition doesn't depend on someone dying for it to happen.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Sunday isn't Sunday without a barbecue. It's a family tradition. Christmas isn't Christmas without turkey."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Shared rituals, like the Sunday barbecue or Christmas dinner, strengthen family bonds.
2. **P2.** These rituals have characteristic foods that are part of their identity.
3. **P3.** Changing the foods threatens the ritual and, with it, the bonds.
4. **C.** So there are good reasons to keep eating meat on these occasions.

P1 and P2 are true: rituals matter, and food is part of them. The problem lies in P3: what strengthens the bonds is the gathering, and the gathering survives a change of menu.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

What makes Sunday with the family special? Being together, talking, playing, laughing. The food helps, but it isn't what makes a family a family. You can have a party, a barbecue and Christmas dinner with foods that don't hurt any animal, and everyone still stays together.

## 4. Short answer

What brings the family together is the gathering, not the animal. The conversation, the laughter and the full table carry on with other food. Traditions change all the time: holiday menus have changed a lot over the generations. Today there are vegetable skewers, plant-based meats and dishes everyone can eat together. Affection doesn't need anyone to die for it to happen.

**Question to ask back:** "If the barbecue were vegetables and everyone was there, would the family stop being close?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assigns to the animal a value that belongs to the gathering:

| What brings the family together | Does it depend on meat? |
| --- | --- |
| Being together | No |
| Talking and laughing | No |
| Cooking and eating together | No; it depends on there being food, any food |
| The weekly ritual | No; it depends on repetition |
| The smell and taste of the barbecue | Partly, and it can be recreated |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The bond is the gathering.** Families stay close when a member becomes vegetarian, when someone has to change their diet for health reasons or when the menu changes. The bond isn't on the plate.

**2. Traditions change all the time.** Dishes that seem obligatory at celebrations today were once new, and many old dishes have disappeared without families falling apart.

**3. There are alternatives that keep the ritual.** Vegetable skewers, mushrooms, plant-based cheeses and plant-based meats keep the grill, the fire and the gathering.

**4. Some people's affection is paid for by others.** The argument asks that the pleasure of a ritual weigh more than the lives of the animals who die for it.

**5. The argument is used to avoid social discomfort.** Often the real problem is fear of standing out from the group (J13), not the value of the ritual itself.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Piazza et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25865663/) | "Normal" and "nice" are among the justifications most used for eating meat | The ritual combines the two |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: changing the ritual causes conflict

The most serious version accepts that the gathering is what matters, but says that, in practice, refusing the barbecue creates conflict, makes the person feel excluded and can drive the family apart.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The conflict comes from the reaction, not the choice.** A close family can welcome someone who eats differently.
2. **There are ways to take part without conflict.** Bringing a dish for everyone, helping at the grill, talking calmly.
3. **Avoiding conflict isn't a moral justification.** Many important changes began with difficult conversations within families.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to tradition | The ritual becomes a justification |
| Logical | False cause | Attributes to the animal a bond that comes from the gathering |
| Moral | Affection paid for by others | The group's pleasure weighs more than the animals' lives |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **The family stays close when someone changes diet for health reasons.** The bond doesn't depend on the menu.
2. **The rituals have changed before.** Almost every family has dishes it no longer makes.
3. **Values the gathering, not the animal.** No one misses the chicken; they miss the people.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a ritual brings people together, its elements are justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bull-baiting and cockfighting | They'd be justified, because they brought communities together | They were banned in many places |
| Violent hazing rituals | They'd be acceptable, because they create bonds | Many have been banned |
| Any collective practice | It would be justified by the unity it creates | The bond doesn't justify the harm |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Family rituals are precious, and food is part of them. Changing habits tied to affection is hard, and it's understandable that many people resist. Animal advocates should respect that and help keep the gatherings going, not push anyone away.

But what people value in these rituals is each other, not the animal. A tradition can change without losing what matters most.
