# L26. "Vegan documentaries are manipulated"

**Thesis:** some contain real errors and exaggerations, especially on health and on numbers, and that's worth acknowledging: *What the Health* was criticized even by a vegan dietitian, and the "empty oceans by 2048" forecast in *Seaspiracy* was called into doubt by the author of the study it cites. Editing and soundtrack are tools of persuasion. But two things need separating: claims made by interviewees, which can be wrong, and footage shot inside farms and slaughterhouses, which shows standard, legal practices described in the industry's own rules (B10). And the moral conclusion doesn't depend on any film.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's propaganda with sad music. That documentary has already been debunked. Vegan media exaggerates everything."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Vegan documentaries are made by activists, with the aim of persuading.
2. **P2.** Several of them contain factual errors, inflated numbers and emotional editing devices.
3. **P3.** A source that gets things wrong and manipulates isn't reliable.
4. **C.** So what these films say about animals can be dismissed.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and in the conclusion: errors in some claims in some films don't make the footage in others false, nor do they settle the moral question.

The argument is **invalid**: it generalizes from parts to the whole and from the messenger to the message.

## 3. Answer for children

A film can exaggerate in one part and show something true in another. If someone tells a story and makes a number bigger, we correct the number. But if the same film shows a hen stuck in a cramped cage, the cage is real. The best thing is to check each thing, instead of believing everything or believing nothing.

## 4. Short answer

You're partly right: there are documentaries with errors, and I don't defend them. *What the Health* overstated the health claims, and the 2048 figure in *Seaspiracy* doesn't hold up. But camera footage from inside a farm isn't an opinion. Gestation crates, the culling of male chicks and the gas stunning of pigs appear in the industry's own rules. And I don't need any film for the main question: animals feel, and we don't need to eat them.

**Question to ask back:** "Setting aside everything that was exaggerated, is what's left in the footage acceptable to you?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

"Vegan documentary" lumps together very different films, and within each film there are different kinds of content:

| Kind of content | Example | Can it be wrong? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health claims | *What the Health* (2017) | Yes, and several are |
| Projections and numbers | "Empty oceans by 2048", in *Seaspiracy* (2021) | Yes; that one was contested |
| Footage of production practices | *Dominion* (2018), *Earthlings* (2005) | The footage shows what happens; the fair question is whether it's the standard (B10) |
| Editing, narration and soundtrack | All of them | These are tools of persuasion, not evidence |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The errors exist, and acknowledging them is part of the job.** The American vegan dietitian Virginia Messina wrote in 2017 that *What the Health* cherry-picks studies and overstates conclusions: it treats hot dogs and cigarettes as equally dangerous because they're in the same carcinogen category (the category indicates the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk) and suggests a vegan diet cures almost everything. In her view, the film damages the cause's credibility. On *Seaspiracy*, the BBC's fact-checking team spoke to the lead author of the 2006 study behind the 2048 forecast, the researcher Boris Worm, of Dalhousie University in Canada, who said the paper was 15 years old, used data almost 20 years old, and that several regions had been rebuilding stocks.

**2. An error in a claim doesn't erase an image.** What an interviewee says about cholesterol doesn't change what the camera recorded in a shed. *Dominion* was made mainly with hidden cameras and drones on farms and in slaughterhouses in Australia. The hardest scenes show routine procedures.

**3. What shocks is usually in the rulebook.** The European regulation on killing lists among the permitted methods the maceration of chicks up to 72 hours old, carbon dioxide at high concentration for pigs and the electrical waterbath for poultry. In Brazil, Ministry of Agriculture rules allow gestation crates early in pregnancy and the waterbath for poultry (B9, B10). Anyone who doubts the film can read the regulation.

**4. Persuasion isn't lying.** Sad music and editing steer emotion, and it's fair to notice that. But every documentary, every news report and every commercial uses these tools. The useful question is whether what's shown is true and representative.

**5. The industry does propaganda too.** In the United States there are 22 federal programs promoting agricultural products, funded by producers and overseen by the Department of Agriculture; campaigns such as "Got Milk?" and "Pork: The Other White Meat" came out of them. Packaging with cows on pasture and hens roaming free is editing too. Skepticism has to apply to both sides.

**6. The moral conclusion doesn't depend on a film.** The case for animals rests on two things that can be checked without cinema: they feel, and most people can live well without eating them. A bad documentary is a poor messenger, and that doesn't alter the message (L1).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: if they get the checkable things wrong, why trust the rest?

The most serious version says: when a source errs on verifiable points, it's rational to distrust it on the points that can't be verified. The filmmakers had a conclusion ready and chose the worst footage. The ordinary viewer has no way of knowing what's the rule and what's the exception. Given that, suspending judgment is the prudent attitude.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Distrust is reasonable; dismissing without checking is something else.** Distrust should lead to looking for better sources, such as official rules, production data and the scientific literature, and they confirm the practices shown.
2. **Standard practices aren't chosen moments.** A sow in a gestation crate stays there for weeks, not just during the minute that was filmed (B10).
3. **Suspending judgment doesn't suspend consumption.** Someone who says "I don't know what to believe" and keeps buying has already picked a side. When in doubt about a serious harm, the prudent thing would be to abstain.
4. **Selection bias is fixed with transparency.** If the films show only the worst, the industry would only need to open its doors. In several US states the response was to try to ban filming, and courts struck those laws down (B10).

### (f) Back to the moral question

Suppose every vegan documentary were bad and none existed. Animals would go on being confined and killed in the same way, and the question would be the same: is it fair to do this to those who feel, when we don't need to? Films are a way of showing; what matters is what happens to each animal when the camera isn't there.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L26 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | From the errors of some films concludes that all lie about everything |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Rejects the content because activists produced it |
| Logical | Poisoning the well | Calling it "propaganda" excuses one from examining what's shown |
| Epistemological | Selective skepticism | Doubts the films but not the industry's advertising |
| Moral | Changing the subject | The quality of the film takes the place of what happens to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts the other side's propaganda.** Milk and meat commercials also use soundtrack, editing and chosen images.
2. **Trusts documentaries on other subjects.** The demand for perfection appears when the conclusion is uncomfortable.
3. **Says it was debunked without knowing what was.** Usually one claim was debunked, not the whole film.
4. **Doesn't check the rules.** The doubt could be settled by reading what the regulations allow.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L26 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an exposé film has errors or uses emotional devices, the exposé can be dismissed".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Documentaries on forced labor | The exposé would collapse if one number were wrong | The number is corrected and the rest is investigated |
| Films about the tobacco industry | The harm of cigarettes could be ignored because of the soundtrack | The harm is confirmed by other sources |
| News reports on violence | Strong images would be "manipulation" because they move people | The emotion comes from the fact shown |
| Vegan documentaries | The practices of animal production could be ignored | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets a lot right. There are vegan documentaries with false or exaggerated claims, mainly about health and environmental numbers, and they're made to persuade, with editing and music in the service of that. Repeating a wrong figure because it helps the cause is dishonest and costs dearly in credibility. This guide has a whole section, "Stop saying this", devoted to exaggerations that circulate among vegans, because correcting our own mistakes is part of defending animals well. The skepticism of whoever raises the objection is a virtue.

But it has to go all the way. Once the exaggerations are removed, what's left are real, common, legal practices that anyone can check in the rules. And the reason not to eat animals was never a film.
