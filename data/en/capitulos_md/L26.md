# L26. "Veganism is moral puritanism"

**Thesis:** calling a position puritan is giving it a label, and a label doesn't answer an argument. Puritanism polices pleasures that have no victims; veganism is about harm to a third party, the animal. Every morality goes against impulses (the ban on assault does too), and asking people not to kill without need is the minimum of justice, not a quest for purity. The criticism is right on one point: moralism and an obsession with personal purity do exist among some vegans, and that is a problem the movement itself needs to face.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans want to purify the world. It's a morality against human nature, for people who need to feel morally superior."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Part of veganism is lived as a quest for personal purity: lists of what is forbidden, policing of others, the feeling of being on the clean side.
2. **P2.** Moralities of purity tend toward intolerance and deny the human condition, which includes desire, imperfection and death.
3. **P3.** Veganism is a morality of purity.
4. **C.** So veganism should be rejected as puritanism.

P1 is true of some vegans. P2 has support in history. The problem lies in P3: the argument for animals is about what happens to a victim, not about the purity of the eater.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which takes the behavior of some for the content of the idea.

## 3. Answer for children

If someone asks you not to pull the cat's tail, they don't want you to be perfect. They're only asking you not to hurt the cat. With other animals it's similar: not eating animals isn't about wanting to be better than anyone, it's just not wanting them to be hurt.

## 4. Short answer

Puritanism is policing other people's pleasure when nobody gets hurt. Here somebody does: the animal. I don't want to be pure, and I don't think I'm better than you. I just don't want to pay for an animal to suffer and die when I can avoid it. And every morality goes against some impulse: feeling anger is natural, and that doesn't make assault acceptable. There are moralistic vegans, it's true. But the question about animals stays the same with or without them.

**Question to ask back:** "Is asking people not to beat a dog puritanism? So what changes when the animal is a pig?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

In Brazil, this criticism appears in the comments of figures such as Luiz Felipe Pondé, a philosopher and newspaper columnist. In 2019, on a radio talk show, he said he wasn't against veganism but saw in it "a puritan moralistic component". In a December 2020 column he wrote that pure people should never be trusted and that purity is antisocial, and described vegans "in general" as antisocial and authoritarian. English-language readers will know the same criticism from columnists such as Angela Tilby, who wrote in the *Church Times* in 2018 of "the moral puritanism of the vegan movement" and called veganism a kind of secular religion.

The word "puritanism" lumps different things together. It's worth separating them:

| | Morality of purity | Morality of not causing harm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central question | "Am I clean?" | "Was someone hurt?" |
| Typical target | Victimless pleasures | Acts that harm third parties |
| Demands perfection? | Yes | No: it asks for what is possible |
| Example | Condemning dancing or sex | Condemning assault or theft |

The argument for animals belongs in the second column. Some vegans live it as if it were the first, and it's to them that the criticism applies.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Labeling isn't refuting.** Saying a position is puritan doesn't show it's wrong. It's a way of poisoning the well: the listener starts distrusting the vegan before hearing what they say. The question that needs an answer is a different one: do animals feel? Can we live without killing them? (L2)

**2. The subject is the victim, not the purity of the eater.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding exploitation of and cruelty to animals "as far as is possible and practicable". It admits from the outset that perfection doesn't exist (L5). What drives the argument is what happens to the animal, and that doesn't change whether the vegan is humble or arrogant.

**3. Every morality goes against natural impulses.** Feeling anger, wanting what belongs to someone else and favoring one's own relatives are common human impulses, and the rules against assault, theft and nepotism exist to restrain them. Nobody calls those rules a morality "against human nature". Natural isn't a synonym for right (C1).

**4. Not killing without need is the minimum, not the maximum.** Veganism doesn't ask for sainthood, fasting or giving up the pleasure of eating. It asks that the pleasure not cost the life of someone who feels. It's a demand of the same kind as "don't mistreat the dog", which nobody considers puritan.

**5. The charge of moral superiority follows those who question customs.** In 1963, religious leaders called Martin Luther King's actions "unwise and untimely", and in his Letter from Birmingham Jail he also answered the label of extremist. In psychology, Minson and Monin showed that meat eaters rate vegetarians worse when they imagine being judged by them. MacInnis and Hodson found that vegans motivated by animals are viewed more negatively than those motivated by health: what bothers people is the moral message (L1).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: nature kills, and an ethic that refuses death refuses reality

The most serious version, put forward by critics like him, says: nature is the greatest destroyer of lives there is, and life feeds on life. A morality that dreams of a world without death and without blood is a fantasy of innocence, and fantasies of innocence tend to end in intolerance toward those who aren't "pure".

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Veganism doesn't promise a world without death.** It asks us not to cause the harm we can avoid. Recognizing that nature is harsh doesn't oblige anyone to add harshness to it (C1).
2. **The criterion is sentience, not "every form of life".** Animal ethics doesn't say killing a lettuce or a bacterium is wrong; it says the suffering of those who feel counts (A14).
3. **The risk of intolerance is real, but it's a risk of people.** Any moral cause can turn into a hunt for the impure. That recommends humility to those who defend it, not abandoning the question about the victim.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Arguing over whether vegans are puritans is arguing over the character of the speaker. The pig, the cow and the chicken gain and lose nothing from it. The moral question demands purity from no one: they feel, and we can live without killing them. Is avoiding that harm asking too much, or is it the minimum?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L26 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Poisoning the well | The puritan label discredits the vegan before the argument |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Judges the idea by moralistic vegans |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | Whatever goes against natural impulses would be wrong |
| Moral | Confusing purity with justice | Treats a rule against harming third parties as the policing of pleasures |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Criticizes a morality that goes against impulses.** But accepts the rules against assault, theft and lying.
2. **Accuses vegans of thinking they're superior.** And judges vegans from above, as naive or resentful.
3. **Condemns the mistreatment of dogs and cats.** Without thinking that's puritanism.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L26 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do-gooder derogation | Disparaging people who act for moral reasons eases the discomfort | [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) |
| Psychological reactance | The feeling of being policed produces rejection of the message | General literature on reactance |
| Naturalness bias | What goes against custom seems to go against nature | Linked to C1 |
| Stereotyping | The vegan is imagined as a bitter ascetic | [Cole and Morgan, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21361905/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a morality that asks for renunciation and goes against impulses is puritanism, and can be rejected".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not hitting someone when you're angry | It would be puritanism | No one accepts this |
| Not driving after drinking | It would be moralism against pleasure | The rule exists because of the victims |
| Not eating animals | It would be puritanism | The same mistake: here too there are victims |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It accurately describes part of the movement. Some vegans turn the cause into a quest for personal purity: they police traces of ingredients, humiliate people who slip, measure people's worth by their plate and enjoy feeling they're on the right side. That harms them, pushes others away and takes the focus off animals. The guide criticizes that attitude in L1, L5 and K6. Critics like Pondé and Tilby are also right to distrust anyone who presents themselves as pure: the history of moralities of purity justifies the distrust.

But the remedy for moralism is humility, not indifference. The argument for animals doesn't need anyone pure in order to work. It only needs someone who feels, on the other side, and the possibility of not doing them harm.
