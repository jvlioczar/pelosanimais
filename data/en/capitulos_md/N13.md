# N13. "Sentientists defend parasitism"

**Thesis:** the objection says that, if parasites feel and count, helping the host kills many to save one, so sentientists would have to defend parasitism or be inconsistent. But first of all, when the host dies, many parasites die with it: letting the infestation run does not save the parasites. Moreover, death is only a harm to those who are sentient and would have a good future life, and most parasites are probably not sentient. Even if they were, preventing parasitism is not the same as killing those who already exist. Ethics here is controversial, but it is necessary to discuss what the ideas imply.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "So for you the parasite is worth as much as the animal it parasitizes? Then you defend parasitism."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Sentientists hold that every sentient being counts, including in nature.
2. **P2.** Many parasites are sentient, or may be.
3. **P3.** Treating a parasitized animal kills thousands of parasites to save one host.
4. **P4.** If every life counts, the balance is negative, and the sentientist would have to be against treatment.
5. **C.** So sentientism either defends parasitism or is inconsistent.

P1 describes a real position. P2 and P3 have some truth in them: some parasites may feel, and treating a host kills parasites. The problem lies in P4: the balance is not negative, for three reasons the chapter examines. The argument is **invalid**, because the conclusion requires the death count alone to decide, without looking at what happens without treatment.

## 3. Answer for children

Think of a louse on a child's head. If no one takes care of it, the child gets itchy and sick, and the louse won't live long either if its host gets ill. Helping someone who is sick is caring for someone who suffers. And the best thing is to stop animals from getting parasites in the first place, with prevention, before any medicine.

## 4. Short answer

First, when the host dies, many parasites die with it, so letting things run does not protect the parasites. Second, death is only a harm to someone who is sentient and would have a good life ahead, and most parasites are probably not sentient. Third, even when they are, preventing new cases is not the same as killing those who already exist. There are disagreements here, and the ethics of the subject is controversial. But it is worth discussing what each position implies, and that is what the rest of the page does.

**Question to ask back:** "If the host dies, what happens to most of the parasites that lived in it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The objection assumes that treating means killing many to save one. Four questions decide whether that is true:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What happens if we don't treat? | The right comparison is between treating and letting things run, not between treating and a world without harm |
| Is the parasite sentient? | Without sentience, death is not a harm to it |
| If it is, would its life be good? | Death is a harm because of the future good it prevents from being enjoyed |
| Is killing the same as not creating? | Preventing new cases is different from killing those who exist |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. If the host dies, many parasites die too.** Parasitism is not a stable arrangement in which the parasite lives peacefully at the host's expense. Many parasites depend on the host to feed and reproduce: lice, intestinal worms and many other species die with it or soon after. Letting the infestation run does not save the parasites: it often leads to the death of all. There are exceptions, such as fleas and ticks, which can move to another host, and parasitoids, such as certain wasps, whose larvae feed on the host and kill it as part of the cycle. But the premise that treating produces more deaths than not treating is, in many cases, false.

**2. Death is only a harm to those who are sentient.** The Questão de Ética chapter on the subject starts from the idea that death is not bad in itself, but because of the good it prevents from being enjoyed. To count as a harm, it has to affect a being that feels and whose future life is positive. Many parasites are probably not sentient. For them death is not a harm, and treating the sentient host leaves the situation better.

**3. If the parasite is sentient, the life it would have must be assessed.** If it is sentient and has a future life worth living, death is a harm, and it has to be weighed. If its life is not minimally good and cannot be improved, death does not harm it more than continuing to live. It is a case-by-case assessment, not a general rule.

**4. In some cases, the parasite can be removed alive.** There are situations in which the parasite can be taken out without dying. The chapter's example is fly larvae, which only survive if they leave the host's body at a certain stage. In those cases, treating is the only way to prolong their lives.

**5. In the long run, not treating produces more victims.** Most parasites reproduce by producing a large number of offspring. If they are sentient, almost all die early, and this strategy multiplies death and suffering among the parasites themselves. Not treating lets them keep reproducing and infecting new victims. So there are reasons to treat those already infected and, above all, to prevent parasitism in general.

**6. Not creating is not killing.** Even if the death of parasites that already exist were a harm and the right thing were not to treat, preventing new cases, for example by sterilization, would not be rejected: not producing new beings is not the equivalent of killing beings that already exist.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the logic leads to accepting mass death of sentient beings

The most serious version says: if a parasite is sentient and has a good life, killing it on the host's behalf is a real harm, and the logic of sentientism, taken to the end, would justify sacrificing many beings who feel in favor of one. That would be a dangerous rule.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The balance matters, and the chapter discusses it.** The calculation includes what happens without treatment, the kind of life the parasite would have and what multiplies in the long run. Almost always the result favors treatment and prevention.
2. **The rule would apply to humans too.** If care for someone who suffers were forbidden whenever it harms other beings who feel, no one could defend themselves from an aggressor. The answer is to weigh, not to forbid.
3. **The scope is limited.** The discussion applies to cases in which the parasites are probably sentient, which are the minority. In most cases there is no doubt that caring for the host is right.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Sees only "defend the parasite" or "kill it", with no prevention or removal |
| Epistemological | Incomplete count | Counts the deaths from treating and ignores those from not treating |
| Epistemological | Hasty generalization | Treats all parasites as sentient |
| Moral | Focus on death only | Ignores the host's suffering and the life the parasite would have |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Applies to animals a rule no one applies to themselves.** Whoever removes lice, treats worms or uses repellent doesn't stop caring for themselves because of the parasites.
2. **Defends "nature" but wants to keep it as it is, with the suffering it produces.** That is the appeal to nature.
3. **Demands that sentientists answer every extreme case, but doesn't do the same for their own positions.**

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle tested is: "if the parasite feels, it is wrong to kill it to save the host".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person with a serious parasitic infection | Could not be treated if the parasite felt | No one accepts that: the person matters too |
| Someone who defends a victim of aggression | Could not hurt the aggressor | What is discussed is proportion, not defense itself |
| A louse on a child's head | Would have to be left, if it felt | The same reasoning, applied to a common case |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It is right that the death of beings who feel matters, parasites included, and that the subject is hard. It is right to show that an ethics that takes nature seriously has to discuss the uncomfortable cases, not only the easy ones. The ethics of parasitism is controversial, and sentientists themselves disagree on points such as the sentience of each parasite and the value of the life they would have.

But discussing the extrapolations of the ideas shows that the conclusion does not follow: not treating doesn't save the parasites, death is only a harm to those who feel and would have a good life, and preventing is different from killing. The question stays open, and deserves to be discussed without caricatures.
