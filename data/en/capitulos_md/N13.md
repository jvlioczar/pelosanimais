# N13. "We'd have to drop everything to help wild animals"

**Thesis:** the objection assumes that helping wild animals would be each person's job, done with their own hands in the forest. It isn't. Research and intervention belong to professionals who already work with wildlife, such as biologists, veterinarians and animal welfare scientists. The rest of us are asked for what we already do for other causes: pay taxes, donate if we wish, support research and bring the issue into public debate. And even if the duty were large, a demanding duty is still a duty.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** IND

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If I take the suffering of trillions of animals seriously, I'd have to give up my job, my free time and my family and spend my life on it. No way."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** A moral proposal that asks too much of each person should be rejected.
2. **P2.** Suffering in nature is so vast that the proposal to reduce it would require each of us to devote almost our whole life to it.
3. **C.** So the proposal to reduce wild animal suffering should be rejected.

P1 has serious defenders: the so-called demandingness objection is one of the best-known criticisms of utilitarianism. The philosopher Alison Hills used wild animals precisely to argue, in 2010, that utilitarianism is more demanding than usually thought, and therefore less plausible. P2 is the weak premise.

The argument is **valid**, but P2 is false as a description of the proposal, and P1, in its strong form, would also condemn duties we accept toward humans.

## 3. Answer for children

When someone gets sick, you don't have to become a doctor to help. The doctor does the caring, and the hospital is paid for with everyone's money. It can be the same with animals in nature: people who know about animals study them and care for them, and we help by saying it matters.

## 4. Short answer

No one is asking you to drop everything. The proposal is for biologists, veterinarians and scientists to research and help, as they already do for other goals, and for the rest of us to support that with taxes, donations and our voice. That's what we do with public health: we don't all become doctors, but we think diseases should be treated. And if a duty were large, that would tell us the problem is large, not that the duty doesn't exist.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you think fighting malaria requires every person to drop everything and become a doctor? Or is it enough that someone does the work and the rest of us support it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The objection mixes up three different questions:

| Question | What the objection assumes | What the proposal says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who does the work? | Every person, with their own hands | Professionals in wildlife-related fields |
| How much is asked of lay people? | Their whole life | Taxes, voluntary donations and public support |
| Does demandingness cancel the duty? | Yes, if it's large | At most it limits what is asked of each person |

Once these questions are separated, the objection loses its footing: it criticizes a proposal no one has made.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Professionals do the work.** The proposal to help wild animals is, first of all, a proposal for research and for interventions carried out by people who know the field. These professionals already intervene in nature for other purposes, such as conserving species or protecting human health. What changes is the question that guides the work: besides "does the species survive?", also "how are the individuals doing?". An Animal Ethics survey of 111 life science academics from 19 countries and 226 students in the field from 24 countries found majority support for this kind of work, and identified lack of funding, more than objections in principle, as the main obstacle.

**2. Little is asked of lay people, and it already makes a difference.** For those outside the field, the duties would be similar to those we have toward any public cause: supporting the use of part of public resources to research and help these animals, donating if one wishes, spreading the word that the problem exists and, in an emergency, helping an injured animal. Cunha notes that even a modest version, such as half an hour a day from those who are well off, would already have a large effect (Cunha, 2022, p. 226).

**3. The human test.** We accept that diseases like malaria should be fought, without concluding that each person must give up their career and become a doctor. Nor do we think that, because world hunger is enormous, we have no duty at all regarding it. The size of a problem changes how the response is organized, not whether there should be one.

**4. Part of helping is to stop harming.** A lot of public money is already spent on interventions in nature that kill animals or create conditions for more suffering, such as programmes to eradicate species considered invasive. Redirecting these resources, or simply suspending them, requires no personal sacrifice from anyone (Cunha, 2022, p. 226; N3).

**5. Rest and careers can help.** Even the most demanding moral theories recognize that exhausted people do less. Brian Tomasik, quoted by Cunha, compares this to a car: to go farther, it's not wise to always drive at the limit. Leisure, health and a career that brings income or reach can serve the very goal of helping (Cunha, 2022, pp. 225–226).

**6. Demandingness isn't unique to one theory.** A duty-based theory that forbade killing any sentient being would, if insects can feel, be more demanding than consequentialism, since one could hardly walk without killing some (Cunha, 2022, p. 215, note 122). Demandingness shows up in several theories; it isn't proof that one of them is wrong.

**7. The demand is moderate, and today the accounting is unequal.** Jeff Sebo (2022) argues that morality is moderately demanding: including animals doesn't always require great sacrifices, and many measures benefit humans and animals at once. When there is conflict, he notes that today we increase benefits for less than 1% of the animal population, humans, at the cost of more than 99%, and that such an unequal distribution of burdens is unacceptable. At the same time, he rejects, for now, overly demanding proposals, such as banning every activity that kills animals as a side effect.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: even when shared, the duty has no end

The most serious version says: if the scale of suffering in nature is as great as claimed, no "reasonable minimum" is stable. If half an hour helps, an hour helps more, and the logic of the proposal keeps pushing each person toward more. Bernard Williams (1973) and Susan Wolf (1982) argued that such a morality crushes the integrity and personal projects that give life meaning, and that this is a flaw in the theory, not in the agent.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **If the criticism works, it also works for humans.** The same reasoning applies to poverty and disease around the world. Those who accept the counter-reply don't conclude that we have no duty to distant people; they conclude that the duty has limits. The same holds here.
2. **Some theories limit the duty fairly.** The philosopher Liam Murphy (2000) proposed that each person should do their fair share, the share that would fall to them if everyone cooperated. With public resources and dedicated professionals, that share would be small for each person.
3. **Saying a theory asks too much assumes what it needs to prove.** That a demanding morality is flawed is exactly what is under discussion. Cunha points out that the cost to those who help counts, but must be weighed impartially against the harm that would be prevented (Cunha, 2022, p. 225).
4. **The first step is cheap.** Supporting research, not opposing those who help and no longer funding what harms are well within any reasonable limit.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Straw man | Attacks the idea that each person must save animals with their own hands, which no one proposed |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either you give your whole life, or you owe nothing |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts limited duties regarding human diseases and disasters, but denies any duty when the victims are animals |
| Epistemological | Size as an excuse | Uses the scale of the problem, a reason to take it seriously, as a reason to ignore it |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports taxes funding public health and research.** Without thinking everyone must become a doctor or scientist.
2. **Gives to or supports disaster relief campaigns.** Without thinking this obliges them to drop everything.
3. **Thinks it's good for injured animals to be taken to a rehabilitation centre.** Which is already help for wild animals given by professionals.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains N13 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collapse of compassion | The larger the number of victims, the less weight each one gets, and the problem seems too big to be worth it | [Fetherstonhaugh et al., 1997](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007744326393) |
| Omission bias | Doing nothing seems morally neutral | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if solving a problem would ask a lot of everyone, then no one has any duty regarding it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Malaria and other diseases | There'd be no duty to support fighting them | Almost no one accepts this |
| World hunger | Aid would only ever be charity, never a duty | Many consider it at least a limited duty |
| Climate change | No one would have to do anything, because the problem is huge | This is the reasoning climate policy tries to overcome |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that morality must be livable and that asking each person to sacrifice everything would be a mistake. It's also right that, if no one organizes, the size of the problem crushes whoever tries alone.

But the answer to that is to share the work, not to deny the duty. The proposal asks for research done by professionals and support from the public, and that fits into any life.
