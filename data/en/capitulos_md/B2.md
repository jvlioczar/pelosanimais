# B2. "Killing without pain isn't harm"

**Thesis:** death harms the one who dies because it takes away all the good experiences they would still have had: if an individual had good experiences ahead, they lose all of them. That's why killing a person in their sleep, without pain or fear, is still one of the worst wrongs there are. Farmed animals are killed very young, far from the natural end of their lives, and lose almost everything they had ahead of them. A painless death removes the suffering of the moment, not the harm. And you don't need to know you'll die to lose your future: babies and people with advanced dementia don't know either, and many animals, though they may not understand death, anticipate the future and feel fear in the face of danger.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If the animal didn't suffer, what's the problem? It doesn't even know it's going to die."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Something only harms someone if it's experienced as bad.
2. **P2.** An instant, painless death isn't experienced: after it, there's no one left to miss anything. And whoever doesn't know they'll die doesn't suffer in anticipation either.
3. **P3.** Animals have no plans or awareness of their own future, so they lose nothing they already desired.
4. **C.** So killing an animal painlessly doesn't harm it.

The strong version has a long history. Epicurus already said that death is nothing to us, because when it is here, we are not. And P3 appears in serious authors, who distinguish beings with and without awareness of their own future. The second part of P2 has some truth to it: most animals probably don't have a concept of their own death as we do.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, which no one accepts in their own case, and at P3, which confuses desiring the future with having a future to lose and underestimates what many animals know of the future.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone takes a puppy from your house while he's asleep, and he never wakes up. He didn't feel any pain. But he lost all the walks, the games and the cuddles he was still going to have. That's bad for him, not just for you. It's the same with farm animals: even without pain, they lose the whole life they had ahead of them.

## 4. Short answer

Death harms the one who dies because it takes away everything they would still live: think of everything you would lose if you died today, even without feeling a thing. That's why killing a person in their sleep, without pain, is a very serious crime and not a neutral act. A baby or someone with advanced dementia doesn't know they're going to die either, and that wouldn't make killing them harmless. Animals don't make plans for next year, but they have a future of good experiences, and they lose that future when they're killed. And farmed animals are killed very young: they lose almost all the life they would have had. Besides, even without understanding death, they perceive the threat during transport and slaughter, and that fear can be measured.

**Question to ask back:** "Doesn't killing someone in their sleep, without pain, harm that person?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument depends on an idea about what it is to be harmed. There are two answers in philosophy:

| View | What makes death bad | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Experiential (Epicurus) | Only what is felt can be bad | A painless death would harm no one, not even humans |
| Deprivation (Nagel) | Death is bad because it takes away the goods life would still have brought | Death harms anyone who had a good future ahead |

The form "they don't even know they're going to die" adds a third idea: death would be bad because of the fear it causes. It confuses the suffering of anticipating death with the harm of death itself. Fear of dying is an additional suffering, worth avoiding, but its absence doesn't remove the main harm, the loss of the future.

The argument uses the first view for animals and the second for humans. Whoever thinks killing a person in their sleep harms them has already accepted the deprivation view, and it applies to any being with a future of experiences.

The deprivation view has a condition, and it matters: death harms when it takes away a future with good experiences. That's why it does not condemn euthanasia carried out in the interest of the one who dies, when what lies ahead is mostly pain without relief, as with an animal with a terminal illness. In that case death does not deprive the individual of goods, and may even spare them harms. Slaughter is the opposite: farmed animals are killed young and, for the most part, healthy, not for their own good, and they lose a future that, away from farming, could hold many good experiences.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. No one accepts P1 in their own case.** If only what is felt harms, killing a healthy person while they sleep wouldn't be bad for them. Almost everyone rejects that conclusion, and that's exactly why the philosopher Thomas Nagel argued, in 1970, that death is bad because of what it deprives us of.

**2. You don't need to plan the future, or know you'll die, to lose it.** A baby makes no plans and has no idea what death is, and yet their death takes a whole life from them. The same goes for a person with advanced dementia or a profound intellectual disability, for an adult asleep with no inkling of what's coming and for someone killed by surprise, without noticing the danger: none of them suffers in anticipation, and all of them lose what they would still have lived. What matters isn't desiring the future, but having a future of good experiences that death eliminates.

**3. Farmed animals lose almost everything.** Broiler chickens and pigs are slaughtered with a fraction of the life they would have had. On the deprivation view, the loss is proportional to the future taken, and in their case it's almost the whole life.

**4. Many animals anticipate the future.** Western scrub-jays cache food with the next morning's hunger in mind. Chimpanzees and orangutans choose and keep tools to use later. The idea that animals live in a pure present doesn't match what the studies show, even though we don't know whether any of them understands its own death.

**5. Fear doesn't require understanding death.** Even if they don't know they're going to die, animals at the very least perceive when they're threatened, and that tends to cause fear and stress. The stages before slaughter bring together several known sources of it: a change of environment, separation and mixing of groups, handling by strangers, food withdrawal, transport and restraint. Scientific reviews measure this stress through behavior, such as escape attempts, refusal to move forward and vocalizations, and through physiological indicators, such as stress hormones and heart rate (Terlouw et al., 2008; EFSA, 2020). An animal may not know what dying is and still suffer at the moment it's taken to die.

**6. The argument doesn't address suffering.** Even if accepted, it would concern only the moment of death. Confinement, mutilations and separation from offspring would still be harm (B1, B4).

**7. It's used selectively.** No one accepts the painless death of a healthy dog as neutral. The argument only shows up when the victim is a farmed animal.

**8. Intellectual pleasures aren't the only ones that count.** To say a pig loses little by dying because it doesn't read or listen to music, you'd have to show that non-intellectual pleasures are worth almost nothing. But being with those you're fond of, eating, playing and resting in the sun are worth a lot, to us as well. And seeing a loved one again after years isn't an intellectual pleasure, and may be worth more than many intellectual activities.

**9. Little time ahead isn't little to lose.** We don't accept that killing an old person is a small harm just because they have fewer years ahead. Besides, broiler chickens are slaughtered at around six weeks, when they could live for years: they lose almost all their possible life. And there are indications that smaller animals with faster metabolisms perceive changes at shorter intervals than we do, which would make their lives longer on the inside than they look from the outside.

**10. The hypothesis of a better life after death doesn't authorize killing.** We don't know whether it exists or whether it would be better. We do know, on the other hand, what the animal's life would be like if it went on living. And no one accepts this reasoning for killing happy humans.

**11. Singer also rejects P1, but by another route and with a caveat this guide doesn't follow.** For Peter Singer, in *Practical Ethics*, even the painless death of an animal without self-awareness is a wrong when it takes away a life that would have been pleasant, because it removes the pleasure the animal would still have had (ch. 5). Up to there, he agrees that killing painlessly can be a harm. But he adds that, in this case, the loss could be made up for if the death allowed another animal with an equally good life to be born; only for self-aware beings, with desires about their own future, would death be a loss that no birth makes up for. The deprivation account, used in points 1 to 3, is different: the harm belongs to the one who dies, and someone else's birth doesn't undo it. Replacement, and the narrow limits Singer himself puts on it, are dealt with in B7. Even granting that killing a self-aware being is more serious, Singer refuses, in practice, to kill animals for food: when in doubt about self-awareness, the hunters' rule applies, and they don't shoot at something moving in the bushes without knowing whether it's game or another hunter; there are indirect reasons, such as dairy cows that keep calling for their calves for days after they are separated; and, as a practical rule, he thinks it better not to kill animals for food unless it is needed to survive.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: an animal's death is a lesser harm

The most serious version accepts the deprivation view but says the harm of death also depends on the psychological connection between a being and its future. Jeff McMahan called these time-relative interests: those with plans, memory and self-awareness lose more by dying. Animals would have a weaker connection, and so their death would be a lesser harm than an adult human's.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **A lesser harm is still a harm.** The counter-reply abandons the conclusion "killing without pain isn't harm" and starts discussing how much harm it is. It no longer authorizes killing.
2. **The harm has to be weighed against the benefit.** On the other side is a pleasure of taste (J6). Even a reduced harm weighs more.
3. **The same account would downgrade humans.** Babies and people with advanced dementia also have a weak psychological connection to the future. Almost no one accepts that killing them is a lesser harm (A18, A19).
4. **Animals have a connection to the future.** Many remember, anticipate and have stable preferences (point 4 of (b)). The difference is one of degree, not of kind.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Proves too much | If it held, killing a person in their sleep wouldn't harm them |
| Logical | Double standard | Uses the experiential view for animals and the deprivation view for humans |
| Logical | Confusing concepts | Treats the fear of dying as if it were the harm of death |
| Epistemological | Confusing desiring with having | Assumes only those who plan the future can lose it |
| Epistemological | Underestimating animal cognition | Assumes animals live in a present without a future |
| Moral | Reducing harm to pain | Ignores the deprivation caused by death |
| Moral | Silence about suffering | Talks about the moment of death and ignores the life before it |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Considers killing someone in their sleep a crime.** The absence of pain has never been a mitigating factor.
2. **Mourns the early death of a pet.** Even when the pet didn't suffer, the loss is felt as the pet's own.
3. **Protects babies and people with advanced dementia, who make no plans and don't know they'll die.** The lack of projects doesn't reduce their protection.
4. **Values their own life for what they'll still live.** No one would accept being killed painlessly for having no plans for tomorrow.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a painless death doesn't harm the one who dies, especially if they don't know they'll die".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People asleep | Killing them without their noticing wouldn't be bad for them | Contradicts one of our firmest moral judgments |
| Babies | Killing them painlessly wouldn't harm them, because they make no plans and don't know what death is | No one accepts this |
| People with advanced dementia | They could be killed painlessly, because they have no notion of death and make no plans | Contradicts human rights |
| Victims of sudden death | Someone killed by surprise, without noticing the danger, would have suffered no harm | Contradicts the common judgment that they lost their life |
| Pets | Putting down a healthy dog painlessly would be neutral | Animal advocates and guardians reject this |
| People without family | If no one missed them and there were no pain, the death would have no victim | The harm belongs to the one who loses their life |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There's something right here. Pain and fear at the moment of death are real harms, and reducing them matters. Anticipating death is also a real source of suffering, which many humans have and most animals perhaps don't. The philosophy of death is also genuinely difficult, and there's serious debate about how much death harms different beings. It's reasonable to think that the death of a being with long life projects may be, in some sense, a greater loss.

But none of this makes death a non-harm. Whoever dies loses everything they would still live, and farmed animals lose almost their whole life. A painless death is better than a painful one; it isn't better than staying alive.
