# B2. "Free-range animals are happy"

**Thesis:** a better life does not make an early death acceptable. Free-range animals are still killed young, and labels like "free-range", "cage-free" or "from a small farm" speak of space and scale, not of how the story ends. And this meat is a tiny fraction of what is eaten: worldwide, about three quarters of farmed animals live in intensive systems.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this chapter also answers "I buy from small farmers", which was merged into it.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I only buy free-range chicken and eggs from free-range hens. Those animals live well."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The problem with animal farming is the suffering of intensive farming.
2. **P2.** Animals raised free-range or by small farmers have good lives, with space and natural behaviors.
3. **C.** So consuming products from these animals is not wrong.

P2 may be true in some cases: there are farms where animals live much better than in intensive systems. The problem lies in P1, which forgets death, and in the gap between the label and the practice.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, because death is also a harm (A16), and in generalizing P2 from rare cases.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine a dog that lives in a big yard, runs, plays and is well cared for. Would it be right, after a few months, to kill him for food because he had a good life? Most people would say no. A good life is great, but it's no reason to take that life before its time.

## 4. Short answer

A better life is better, no doubt. But the free-range animal is still killed young, and for eggs the male chicks are still discarded, whether the hens are free-range or not. Labels speak of space, not of the end. And this meat is the exception: about three quarters of the world's farmed animals live in intensive systems. If a good life matters to the animal, its life matters too.

**Question to ask back:** "If an animal had a good life, does that give anyone the right to take it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument reduces the problem to the way animals are raised:

| What the label improves | What the label doesn't change |
| --- | --- |
| Space, outdoor access, stocking density | The animal is killed long before the natural end of its life |
| Behaviors like scratching and grazing | For eggs, males are still discarded at birth |
| Sometimes, feed and antibiotic use | The animal is still treated as a product |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Death remains.** Free-range animals are slaughtered young, like the others. If death deprives the animal of a good life (A16), the better the life, the greater the loss.

**2. Males are still discarded.** In egg production, hens and roosters hatch in roughly equal numbers, and the males are useless both for laying and for meat. An estimated 7.83 billion male chicks were killed worldwide in 2024. This also happens in free-range egg supply chains. Some countries, such as Germany and France, now require sexing in the egg, but this is the exception.

**3. Labels have little to do with the imagined scene.** "Free-range" or "cage-free" set minimum conditions of space and handling, not an idyllic life. The image of the little farm rarely matches what is in the package.

**4. It is a tiny fraction.** Our World in Data estimates that three quarters of the world's farmed land animals live in intensive systems; in the United States, 99%. And people who defend free-range often don't eat only free-range, at home, in restaurants and in processed foods.

**5. Scale doesn't change the nature of the act.** Buying from a small farmer changes who profits and, sometimes, the living conditions. It doesn't change the fact that an animal who wanted to live is killed.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Our World in Data, *How many animals are factory-farmed?*](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-are-factory-farmed) | About 74% of the world's farmed land animals live in intensive systems; in the US, 99% in 2022 | The author notes uncertainty in the global estimate |
| [Faunalytics, global chick culling, 2019-2024](https://faunalytics.org/global-chick-culling-2019-2024/) | 7.83 billion male chicks culled in 2024, up from 7.08 billion in 2019 | Estimate based on egg production data |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: buying better is a real step

The most serious version says the world won't go vegan all at once, and that supporting higher-welfare farms reduces the suffering of real animals now. Demanding all or nothing may block concrete improvements.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Reducing suffering is good, but it isn't the point.** A better farm is better than a worse one. The question is whether it makes killing acceptable, and it doesn't.
2. **The label can work as a license.** Feeling that one buys "ethical meat" can reassure people and keep consumption going rather than reduce it (moral licensing).
3. **The animal-free alternative exists.** Between free-range and intensive there is a third option, which involves neither.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the question | Answers about life when the question includes death |
| Epistemological | Generalizing from the exception | Takes the small-farm case as a description of real consumption |
| Epistemological | Trust in the label | Assumes "free-range" guarantees a good life |
| Moral | Reducing harm to the farming method | Ignores death and the culling of males |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wouldn't kill their own dog after giving him a good life.** A good life doesn't license its end.
2. **Eats meat outside the home without checking where it came from.** The free-range criterion rarely applies at restaurants.
3. **Doesn't know about the male chicks.** The concern for welfare doesn't reach what happens at the hatchery.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B2 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Optimism bias | "The meat I buy is different" | Linked to B1 and H8 |
| Moral licensing | "Better" consumption licenses carrying on consuming | Linked to E6 and J12 |
| Willful ignorance | The label makes it unnecessary to know what actually happens | Linked to E7 |
| Framing effect | "Free-range" and "cage-free" highlight the best of the farming and leave the slaughter out of the picture | [Levin and Gaeth, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1086/209174) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the animal had a good life, killing it for food is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Well-treated dogs | They could be bred and slaughtered for food | Almost no one accepts this |
| Humans with happy lives | A good life would justify an early death | Absurd |
| Shelter animals | They could be killed after a period of good treatment | Animal advocates fight against this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Living conditions matter a great deal, and the difference between an intensive shed and a farm with space is real for the animal. Wanting animals to live well is the right starting point, and reforms that improve welfare genuinely reduce suffering.

But the same care that asks for a good life for the animal asks that this life not be cut short without necessity. A better life is not a license to take it.
