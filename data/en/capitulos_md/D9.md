# D9. "My grandparents ate meat and lived to 90"

**Thesis:** one case doesn't refute millions. Grandparents who lived to 90 are remembered precisely because they lived; those who died young don't come up in the conversation. That's survivorship bias. Population data show that poor diets are among the biggest risk factors for death worldwide, mainly through too much salt and too few whole grains and fruit, and that processed meat is carcinogenic. And even if meat harmed no one, the moral question would still stand.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My grandmother ate lard and lived to 100. Nobody had these diseases back then."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many people of earlier generations ate meat and lived long lives.
2. **P2.** If meat were harmful, those people wouldn't have lived so long.
3. **C.** So eating meat isn't harmful, and there's no reason to change.

P1 is true in many cases. P2 is false: risk factors increase the probability of disease but don't make it certain, and individual cases say nothing about the average effect.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, and the conclusion ignores that the moral debate doesn't depend on the eater's health.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people who don't wear seat belts never have an accident. Does that mean seat belts are useless? No! It means those people were lucky. To know whether something is good or bad for us, scientists look at lots and lots of people, not just one.

## 4. Short answer

One case doesn't refute millions. Some grandparents smoked and lived to 90, and cigarettes still kill. We remember those who lived long and forget those who died young: that's survivorship bias. Population studies show that poor diets were linked to about 11 million deaths in 2017, mainly from too much salt and too few whole grains and fruit, and processed meat is classified as carcinogenic. And even if meat were healthy, the moral question about the animals would stay the same.

**Question to ask back:** "If a grandfather who smoked lived to 90, does that prove cigarettes are harmless?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument draws a general conclusion from selected cases:

| What the argument uses | What it leaves out |
| --- | --- |
| Grandparents who lived long | Relatives who died young of heart disease, stroke or cancer |
| The diet of the past | Lifestyle, physical activity and the amount of meat, which were different |
| One generation | Data from entire populations |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's anecdotal evidence.** Individual cases don't show average effects. Risk factors increase the probability of disease, but they never guarantee it.

**2. It's survivorship bias.** Grandparents who lived to 90 are remembered precisely for that. Those who died at 50 of a heart attack don't enter the conversation.

**3. Population data show something else.** The Global Burden of Disease study estimated that dietary risks were associated with about 11 million deaths in 2017. Most of them came from too much salt and too few whole grains and fruit, not from meat; the figure shows that diet matters, not that meat is the main risk. In 2015, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified processed meat as carcinogenic to humans and red meat as probably carcinogenic.

**4. "Back then" was different.** Earlier generations tended to eat less meat and fewer ultra-processed foods and to be more physically active. And life expectancy was lower, not higher.

**5. The argument changes the subject.** Even if meat harmed no one, the question would remain the same: is it just to kill animals when we don't need to?

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators, *The Lancet* (2019)](https://healthdata.org/research-analysis/library/health-effects-dietary-risks-195-countries-1990-2017-systematic-analysis) | Dietary risks were associated with about 11 million deaths in 2017 | The main risks were high sodium and low intake of whole grains and fruit |
| [IARC, press release No. 240 (2015)](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf) | Processed meat classified as carcinogenic (Group 1); red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A) | Each 50 g of processed meat a day associated with an 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the individual risk is small

The most serious version accepts the data, but says the increase in individual risk is small, and that each person may accept that risk, as they accept others.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It abandons the original argument.** It no longer says meat isn't harmful, but that the harm is acceptable.
2. **Risk to oneself isn't the heart of the debate.** What's at stake for the animal isn't a risk; it's its whole life.
3. **Personal choice has limits.** Accepting a risk for oneself is different from imposing a certain harm on another (E1).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Anecdotal evidence | One case against the data |
| Epistemological | Survivorship bias | Only the cases that turned out well are remembered |
| Epistemological | Confusing risk with certainty | Assumes a risk factor would have to kill everyone |
| Moral | Changing the subject | Answers about health to a question about justice |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept the same reasoning about cigarettes.** No one concludes smoking is healthy because a grandfather who smoked lived long.
2. **Wears a seat belt.** Even knowing many people without one never had an accident.
3. **Knows life expectancy has risen.** Earlier generations lived shorter lives on average.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains D9 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survivorship bias | Only the successful cases are seen | Linked to G9 |
| Availability bias | Family cases come to mind more easily than statistics | General literature on heuristics |
| Confirmation bias | Accepts the case that confirms the habit | General literature on confirmation bias |
| Rosy retrospection | The grandparents' lives are remembered without the illnesses and early deaths | [Questão de Ética, vol. X, ch. 2](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v10/02-biases-that-incline-us-to-neglect-the-situation-of-wild.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone did something and lived long, it isn't harmful".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cigarettes | They wouldn't be harmful, because there are long-lived smokers | No one accepts this |
| Drunk driving | It would be safe, because many never had an accident | No one accepts this |
| Vegan diets | They'd also be healthy because there are long-lived vegans | The argument favors neither side |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The experience of earlier generations deserves respect, and it's true that many people lived long lives eating meat. It's also true that meat's effect on health depends on the amount, the type and the rest of the diet, and that nutritional alarmism helps no one.

But family stories don't replace data from millions of people. And even if meat were perfectly healthy, the question about the animals would remain unanswered.
