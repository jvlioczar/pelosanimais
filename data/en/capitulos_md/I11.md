# I11. "Silk and down are natural and don't harm animals"

**Thesis:** in conventional silk production, the cocoons are boiled with the live pupa inside so the thread doesn't break; thousands of silkworms are needed for each kilogram of raw silk. Down and feathers come from ducks and geese killed for meat, and sometimes from live birds: the European Food Safety Authority concluded in 2010 that plucking feathers from live birds causes pain and suffering, and that under commercial conditions this is hard to avoid; investigations still catch the practice, which is banned in the European Union. Besides, the birds are generally confined in sheds, often without water to bathe in, which harms the health of their eyes, nostrils and feathers. "Natural" describes the material, not what happens to the animal.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Silk is a natural thread, and feathers fall off the duck by themselves. What's wrong with a down duvet?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Silk and down are natural, renewable and biodegradable materials.
2. **P2.** Down can be collected during moulting, without pain.
3. **P3.** Silkworms are insects with simple nervous systems.
4. **C.** So using silk and down doesn't harm animals.

P1 is true. P2 holds for a tiny part of production. P3 is true, but doubt about insect sentience doesn't license ignoring it (A3).

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and in the conclusion.

## 3. Answer for children

To make silk, cocoons are put in boiling water with the little caterpillar still inside, so the thread doesn't get ruined. And some pillow feathers are pulled from live ducks and geese, which hurts. Many ducks and geese live shut inside sheds, with no pond to swim and clean themselves in. There are cozy fabrics and fillings made without needing any of that.

## 4. Short answer

"Natural" describes the material, not what happens to the animal. Do you know how conventional silk is made? The cocoons are boiled with the live pupa inside so the thread doesn't break; thousands of silkworms are needed per kilogram of raw silk. Down comes from ducks and geese killed for meat, and sometimes from live birds: Europe's food safety authority concluded in 2010 that plucking feathers from live birds causes pain and suffering, and investigations still find the practice. The birds usually live confined, often without water to bathe in, which harms their health. Does a pillow or a blouse justify that, when there are fabrics and fillings that need no animal at all?

**Question to ask back:** "If feathers fell off by themselves, why did European rules need to ban plucking them from live birds?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Material | How it's obtained | What happens to the animal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional silk | Cocoons boiled or heated | The pupa dies inside the cocoon |
| "Peace" (ahimsa) silk | The moth leaves before processing | The moth survives, but breeding continues |
| Down from slaughter | Taken from birds killed for meat | Tied to breeding and slaughter |
| Down from live birds | Plucked or collected during moulting | Plucking causes pain and bleeding |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Conventional silk kills the silkworm.** Whole cocoons are boiled with the pupa inside to keep the thread continuous. About 5,500 silkworms are needed to produce 1 kg of raw silk. Domestication has also limited them: the domestic silk moth cannot fly and depends entirely on humans to survive, and the species does not exist in the wild.

**2. Plucking feathers from live birds causes pain.** The EFSA panel concluded in 2010 that collecting feathers during moulting, by brushing, can be done without pain, but that plucking feathers causes pain and bleeding, and that under commercial conditions some plucking is unavoidable because not all feathers and not all birds moult at the same time. The practice is banned in the European Union, but it is still documented: in 2022, the organization Four Paws released footage of geese being plucked alive on farms in Poland and reported that almost half of the 35 farms visited were doing it. According to the organization, on breeding farms, where birds live longer, a goose can be plucked many times before slaughter. These figures come from an animal protection organization, and the extent of the problem varies by country and supplier.

**3. Confinement limits them and makes them ill.** Ducks and geese are waterfowl, but in intensive farming they generally live in sheds, often with water only from nipple drinkers. A University of Oxford study of Pekin ducks showed that when they have water they can dip their heads in and wet their feathers with, such as baths, troughs or showers, ducks have eyes, nostrils and feathers in better condition than with drinkers alone. In other words, depriving the birds of bathing water, a common practice for reasons of cost and hygiene, harms their health.

**4. Down from slaughter isn't neutral.** Even when it comes from birds killed for meat, down makes the breeding more profitable (I8).

**5. There are alternatives.** Synthetic and plant fillings, such as corn fiber and kapok, and fabrics such as viscose, lyocell and recycled fibers serve the same purposes.

**6. Hundreds of insects per person.** Brian Tomasik estimated that, in rich countries, the silk consumed by an average person kills a few hundred silkworms a year. To release the thread from the cocoon, the pupae are boiled, baked, dried, pierced with a needle, steamed or fumigated. It isn't known whether the pupa inside the cocoon feels pain, but the doubt calls for caution, and there are fabrics that don't require killing anyone.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: there's peace silk and certified down

The most serious version points out that there's silk in which the moth leaves before processing and certifications that guarantee down without live plucking.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **They're small shares of the market.** Conventional silk and untraced down make up most of what's sold.
2. **The breeding continues.** Even in peace silk, silkworms are bred for production, and certified down comes from birds bred for slaughter.
3. **The underlying question remains.** If there are alternatives, why breed and kill animals for fillings and fabric?

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to breed and kill animals, or pluck their feathers, for comfort and elegance, when there are materials that do the same job.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says feathers fall off by themselves.** And doesn't know where the ones in their duvet came from.
2. **Values silk as a noble fiber.** Without knowing the silkworm dies in the process.
3. **Would reject pulling fur from a live dog.** And accepts the same with geese.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the material is natural, obtaining it does no harm".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plucking feathers from live birds | It would be acceptable | Contradicted by EFSA and by a Council of Europe recommendation |
| Fur from wild animals | It would be acceptable | See I9 |
| Plant fillings | They serve the same purpose | Without animals |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that silk and down are natural, biodegradable materials, and that there are production methods that reduce suffering, such as peace silk and collection during moulting.

But those methods are the minority, and even they depend on breeding animals for production. There are materials that keep people warm and well dressed without that.
