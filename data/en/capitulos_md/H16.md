# H16. "Leather and wool are more sustainable than synthetics, which are plastic"

**Thesis:** it's true that many synthetics are petroleum-based plastic and shed microplastics. But leather and wool also have a high impact, because they depend on raising animals, and estimates vary a lot depending on who does the math: an analysis by an ethical fashion organization estimated cowhide leather's carbon footprint at about seven times that of synthetic leather per square meter, while data backed by the leather industry cut the impact attributed to leather by more than 60% in an index used by the sector. Neither side settles the question alone, and the choice isn't only between leather and plastic: there are recycled, plant-based and second-hand materials.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegan leather is plastic. Real leather is natural, lasts a lifetime and pollutes much less."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Synthetic leathers and fibers such as polyester come from oil and release microplastics.
2. **P2.** Leather and wool are biodegradable and durable.
3. **P3.** Leather uses hides that the meat industry would otherwise discard.
4. **C.** So leather and wool are more sustainable than synthetics.

P1 is true. P2 is partly true: tanning, often with chromium, reduces biodegradability. P3 depends on how the impact of raising the animal is divided between meat and leather, and that's exactly what's in dispute.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** depends on contested calculations.

| Context | Typical form |

## 3. Answer for children

Some clothes and bags are made of plastic, which takes a long time to disappear from nature. Others are made from animals' skin and hair, and for that the animals have to be raised, eat a lot and be killed. There are also fabrics made from plants, recycled materials or used clothes, which can be the best choice.

## 4. Short answer

It's true that many synthetics are plastic. But leather and wool also have a high impact, because they depend on raising animals. Estimates vary with who does the math: an ethical fashion organization put cowhide leather at about seven times the carbon footprint of synthetic per square meter, while leather industry data cut the impact attributed to it by more than 60%. And you don't have to choose only between leather and plastic: there are recycled, plant-based and second-hand materials. Besides, the carbon count leaves out whoever pays the highest price: the animal whose skin became a shoe or a bag.

**Question to ask back:** "If the alternative to leather doesn't have to be new plastic, does the argument still favor leather?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Material | Main impacts |
| --- | --- |
| Cowhide leather | Raising cattle (land, emissions), tanning with chemicals |
| Wool | Raising sheep (land, methane), practices such as mulesing in some countries |
| Synthetic leather and polyester | Oil, microplastics, little biodegradability |
| Recycled, plant-based and second-hand | Generally lower impact; varies by material |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The numbers depend on who calculates.** The materials index used by the fashion industry (Higg MSI) cut, in 2024, based on data backed by the leather sector, the climate impact of cowhide leather from 36.8 to 14.6 points. Collective Fashion Justice, meanwhile, estimated cowhide leather's carbon footprint at about seven times that of synthetic leather per square meter. Both sources have interests, and the disagreement shows the question is far from settled.

**2. The impact of raising the animal has to count.** How much of cattle farming's impact is attributed to leather is a methodological choice. Attributing almost nothing to leather, by calling it a byproduct, favors leather (I8).

**3. The comparison is usually with the worst synthetic.** Recycled and plant-based materials and second-hand clothes can have a lower impact than both.

**4. The environment isn't the only question.** Even if an animal material had a lower impact, it would still require raising and killing animals.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Leatherbiz, Higg MSI revision (2024)](https://leatherbiz.com/News/170089) | Cowhide leather's climate impact in the index fell from 36.8 to 14.6 points with new sector data | Leather industry source |
| [Collective Fashion Justice](https://www.collectivefashionjustice.org/articles/carbon-cost-leather-goods) | Cowhide leather has a carbon footprint about seven times that of synthetic per square meter | Ethical fashion organization |
| [CE Delft, 2011](https://www.cedelft.eu/en/publications/1409/natural-mink-fur-and-faux-fur-products-an) | Mink fur has a higher impact than textiles in 17 of 18 categories | See I9 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: leather lasts decades and cheap synthetics are soon trash

The most serious version says durability changes the math: a leather shoe that lasts ten years may have a lower impact than several cheap synthetic shoes.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Durability depends on quality, not material.** There are durable non-animal products and disposable leather ones.
2. **Buying less and using longer applies to any material.** Durability is an argument for buying well, not for buying leather.
3. **The animal is still left out of the math.** Even the most durable leather comes from an animal raised and killed.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The environmental question matters, but it doesn't decide the moral one: a lower-impact material would still require animals to be killed. The best choice is usually the one that avoids both problems.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in H16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either leather or plastic |
| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Uses the calculation that favors leather without mentioning others |
| Moral | The environment as the only criterion | Environmental impact decides, and the animal is left out |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Criticizes plastic.** And doesn't consider materials that are neither leather nor plastic.
2. **Calls leather a byproduct.** And argues that durability justifies raising animals.
3. **Cares about the environment.** And leaves cattle farming's impact out of leather's account.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains H16 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Naturalness bias | Natural material seems greener | Linked to C1 |
| Confirmation bias | People look for the study that confirms their preference | [Nickerson, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the material is natural, it's more sustainable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mink fur | It'd be more sustainable | The CE Delft study suggests the opposite |
| Recycled synthetic leather | It'd be worse | It depends on the material and durability |
| Second-hand clothes | They serve the same function | Usually with lower impact |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that petroleum synthetics are a real environmental problem, that microplastics are a concern, and that durable products are better than disposable ones.

But leather and wool also have a high impact, the numbers are disputed, and the choice doesn't have to be between animal and plastic. And even once the environmental question is settled, the moral question remains.
