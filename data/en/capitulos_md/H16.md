# H16. "Hunting controls populations"

**Thesis:** hunting often helps cause the imbalance it claims to correct: predators were eliminated, species were introduced, and populations are managed so there are animals to hunt. There are real cases of invasive species, such as wild boar in Brazil, where control is authorized by law. But that's managing a human-made problem, not a justification for hunting as recreation. And there are non-lethal methods, such as fertility control and barriers.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Wild boars are a pest. Without hunting, deer starve."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In some ecosystems, certain animal populations grow beyond what the environment can support.
2. **P2.** This causes starvation, disease and damage to vegetation and other species.
3. **P3.** Hunting reduces these populations.
4. **C.** So hunting is necessary for ecological balance.

P1 and P2 are true in some cases. So is P3. The problem lies in the conclusion: the imbalance was often caused by humans, and there are alternatives.

The argument is **valid** for specific management cases. It fails as a general justification for hunting.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes people take animals to places where they didn't live before, or kill the animals that used to eat them. Then they multiply too much. That's a problem people created. To fix it, we can use ways that don't kill, like fences and medicines that prevent babies, and any control should be done carefully, not for fun.

## 4. Short answer

The imbalance was often caused by humans: predators eliminated, species introduced, populations kept up for hunting. Wild boar in Brazil, for example, is an invasive alien species, and its control has been authorized by the federal environment agency since 2013. But that's managing a problem we created, not hunting for recreation. There are also non-lethal methods, such as contraception, barriers and reintroducing predators, which should be studied and preferred where possible.

**Question to ask back:** "If the problem was created by humans, does the solution have to be killing, or can it be fixing the cause?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes up different situations:

| Situation | Origin of the problem | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An invasive species introduced by humans, such as wild boar | Human action | Management with effective, less cruel methods; preventing new introductions |
| Overpopulation after predators were eliminated | Human action | Reintroducing predators, contraception, barriers |
| Sport or recreational hunting | Doesn't solve a problem | Not justified by population control |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The imbalance is often human-made.** Eliminating predators, introducing species and artificial feeding create overpopulation. Hunting treats the symptom, and sometimes sustains it.

**2. Hunting can make the problem worse.** In some species, killing individuals stimulates reproduction among those left or scatters groups, spreading the problem.

**3. The wild boar case is a management case.** In Brazil, a 2013 regulation by the federal environment agency (Ibama) authorized population control of wild boar, considered an invasive alien species. That's different from hunting for sport, and the control itself should seek the least cruel methods.

**4. There are non-lethal methods.** Contraception, barriers, habitat management and reintroducing predators are studied and used in various places.

**5. The argument doesn't justify eating meat.** Almost all meat comes from farming, not hunting. Controlling an invasive species says nothing about cattle, pigs and chickens.

**6. Protecting what, exactly?** Programs to exterminate invasive species usually aim to preserve a species composition considered "original", not to reduce animal suffering. If the aim were the animals' wellbeing, many of these programs would look different. Valuing being "native" more than the life of someone who feels is a criterion we would never accept among humans.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: non-lethal methods are expensive and slow

The most serious version grants that non-lethal methods exist, but says they're expensive, slow and hard to apply over large areas, and that for invasive species, culling is often the only effective option.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That justifies technical management, not recreational hunting.** Controlling invasive species should be done by professionals, with the least cruel methods possible.
2. **The cost should be borne by those who caused the problem.** And preventing new introductions is essential.
3. **It says nothing about farming animals.** The argument doesn't apply to the meat people buy.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Managing invasive species is a real and difficult problem, caused by humans. But it doesn't justify hunting as recreation, or raising and slaughtering domestic animals. The moral question remains: when there's no need, what justifies killing?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in H16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Unwarranted generalization | From management cases concludes that hunting in general is necessary |
| Epistemological | Ignoring the cause | Treats a human-made imbalance as natural |
| Moral | The individual treated as a number | The animal becomes part of a population to be controlled |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the criterion to humans.** No one defends reducing human populations by culling.
2. **Ignores the alternatives.** Even when they exist and are workable.
3. **Hunts species that aren't invasive.** The population-control argument doesn't apply to them.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains H16 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | Control serves as a justification for an activity one enjoys | General literature on motivated cognition |
| System justification | Culling seems the natural form of management | Linked to B9 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there are too many animals, killing them is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stray dogs and cats | They should be killed, not neutered | Many countries use neutering |
| City pigeons | They should be culled | There are non-lethal methods |
| Farmed animals | The argument doesn't apply: their population is bred on purpose | This is the case of C12 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Invasive species and overpopulation can cause real damage to ecosystems, to other species and to agriculture. The wild boar case in Brazil is serious, and ignoring it helps no one. Sometimes control is necessary.

But management isn't recreation, and human-made problems call for careful solutions, with less cruel methods and prevention. None of this justifies raising and slaughtering animals for food.
