# H16. "Plant monoculture destroys as much as industrial animal farming"

**Thesis:** monocultures cause real damage, but the argument gets the address wrong: most of the world's soy and a large share of its grain feed farmed animals, not vegans. Animal farming takes up about 80% of agricultural land and supplies 17% of calories. A plant-based diet would cut agricultural land by about 75%, cropland included. Comparing the worst plant agriculture with the best animal farming is an unfair comparison, and food sovereignty doesn't require raising animals.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Soy and corn monocultures destroy as much as industrial animal farming. A vegan world would be a soy desert. It's regenerative, local systems, with animals integrated into the crops, that look after the land."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Large-scale monocultures degrade soil, depend on pesticides and reduce biodiversity.
2. **P2.** A diet without animal products would depend even more on those monocultures.
3. **P3.** Regenerative, local systems that integrate animals and plants restore the soil and strengthen food sovereignty.
4. **C.** So swapping animal farming for plants only swaps one harm for another, and the way out lies in systems with animals.

This is a typical argument from advocates of regenerative agriculture. P1 is true. The problem lies in P2, which the data contradict (animal farming is the biggest customer of monocultures), and in P3, which describes real local benefits but leaves out the land these systems need and how little food they produce.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Growing just one thing in a huge field can be bad for the land. That's true. But do you know who eats most of the soy and corn from those fields? The cows, pigs and chickens on farms. If people ate the plants directly, we would need to grow less, and there would be more room left for nature.

## 4. Short answer

Monoculture does damage, I agree. But most of it exists to feed animals: about 77% of the world's soy becomes feed, and only about 7% becomes tofu, soy milk and similar foods. Animal farming uses about 80% of agricultural land and delivers 17% of calories. With a plant-based diet, the world would need about 75% less agricultural land, and less cropland too. A vegan world would have less soy, not more.

**Question to ask back:** "If eating plants directly would mean the world needs about 75% less agricultural land, would there be more or less monoculture than today?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument compares the worst of one side with the best of the other:

| Comparison | What the argument does | Fair comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Worst plant system vs. best animal farming | Industrial monoculture against a model regenerative farm | Not done this way |
| Typical vs. typical | Left out | Crops eaten directly against animal farming that depends on feed crops |
| Best vs. best | Left out | Plant-based agroecology against regenerative animal farming |

In both fair comparisons, the plant-based diet uses less land.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Monoculture largely belongs to animal farming.** According to Our World in Data, with 2018 data, 77% of the world's soy goes to animal feed (37% to poultry alone, about a fifth to pigs). Tofu, soy milk, tempeh and other direct foods add up to about 7%. With cereals the picture is similar: 41% become feed and 48% are eaten by people (H1).

**2. Fewer animals means less cropland.** Animal farming takes up about 80% of the planet's agricultural land, counting pasture and feed crops, and supplies 17% of calories and 38% of protein. Based on the study by Poore and Nemecek (2018), Our World in Data calculates that, in a plant-based world, agricultural land would fall from 4.1 to 1 billion hectares, about 75%. Cropland would shrink too, because what is no longer grown for feed is more than what would newly be grown for people.

**3. The comparison is unfair.** Putting the worst plant agriculture next to the best animal farming shows little. Chicken, pork and eggs almost always come from systems that depend on grain, and the regenerative farm in the photo accounts for a small fraction of what is eaten (B2, H8).

**4. Regenerative systems with animals use more land and don't scale.** The report *Grazed and Confused* (2017), coordinated at the University of Oxford, calculated that solely grass-fed animals supply about 1 gram of protein per person per day worldwide, against 49 grams from plants. It also concluded that carbon sequestration from grazing is small, time-limited and reversible: even under very generous assumptions, it would offset 20% to 60% of the emissions of the grass-fed animals themselves (H3, H4).

**5. "Soy desert" describes today's world better.** Today most soy and a large share of cereals are already grown to become feed. A vegan world would free up about three quarters of agricultural land, which could go back to being forest, savanna or native grassland.

**6. Food sovereignty doesn't require raising animals.** The Declaration of Nyéléni (2007), a reference text for the movement, defines food sovereignty as the right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food, produced by ecologically sound methods, and to define their own food systems. The declaration counts pastoralists and fisherfolk among producers, and the movement isn't vegan. But the definition is about who decides, not about using animals: beans, cassava, corn, vegetable gardens, orchards and agroforestry fit in it entirely, with green manures and compost in place of manure (H9).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: where crops can't grow, animals are the autonomy of those who live there

The most serious version speaks of dry, mountainous or very cold regions, and of traditional communities that live from their herds. There, animals turn grass into food, and asking those people to depend on grain brought in from outside would take their autonomy away.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It holds for those in that situation.** The definition of veganism says "as far as is possible and practicable". Those who repeat the argument generally buy their food at a store and have alternatives (D3).
2. **These systems produce a small fraction.** One gram of protein per person per day shows they can't sustain current meat consumption or serve as a model for cities (H4).
3. **The special case doesn't justify the general one.** That a herder in a dryland depends on goats says nothing about supermarket factory-farmed chicken.
4. **The moral question remains.** Even where there are few alternatives, it's worth seeking those that exist. Where there are many, the justification is missing.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Suppose it were proven that a farm with integrated animals looks after the soil better than any cropland. We would still have to answer for what happens to each animal there, raised to be killed. The discussion about monoculture and soil is important, and in it the numbers favor plants. But it treats animals as parts of a farming system. For those who feel, being part of a well-managed system doesn't change how it ends.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in H16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Selective comparison | Puts the worst plant agriculture next to the best animal farming |
| Logical | False equivalence | Treats harms of very different sizes as equal |
| Logical | Tu quoque | "You destroy too" in place of comparing how much |
| Epistemological | Misattribution | Charges to plant eaters a soy crop that becomes feed |
| Epistemological | Ignoring scale | Takes as a general model a system that supplies a tiny fraction of food |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Criticizes monoculture and eats those who consume it.** Store-bought chicken, pork, eggs and milk depend on soy and corn.
2. **Rejects monoculture, but not feed.** If the problem is monoculture, the first step would be to stop feeding animals with it.
3. **Asks for food sovereignty with the system that uses the most land.** The more land per calorie, the less food each territory can produce for itself.
4. **Doesn't apply the soil criterion to plants.** If what matters is the health of the land, plant-based agroecology should be the first option.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains H16 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Halo effect | "Regenerative" and "local" make the system seem good in every respect | [Thorndike, 1920](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0071663) |
| Salience bias | The soy field stretching to the horizon draws more attention than the feed in the trough | Linked to H1 |
| Hypocrisy hunting | Pointing to the harm of plants replaces comparing the sizes | Linked to L4 and L5 |
| Optimism bias and exceptionalism | The model farm is taken as the rule | Linked to B2 |
| Confirmation bias | The comparison that confirms the habit is accepted | General literature on confirmation bias |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the alternative also causes some harm, the two options are equivalent".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bicycle and car | Since making bicycles also pollutes, it makes no difference | The difference in size is what matters |
| Solar power and coal | Since panels require mining, they would be the same as coal | No sensible person concludes this |
| Recycling | Since recycling uses energy, it would be the same as not recycling | We compare how much, not just whether |
| Crops for people and crops for feed | They would be equivalent | The second requires several times more land per calorie |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that large-scale monoculture does real damage: impoverished soil, pesticides, loss of biodiversity, concentration of land. It's right that a vegan plate can come from a bad farming system, and that vegans would do well to care more about that. It's also right that diversified, local systems have value, and that food sovereignty is a serious cause.

But the limit of the concession lies in the numbers: animal farming is the main reason so many monocultures exist, and the plant-based diet is the one that needs the least cropland. The path the argument itself asks for, with less monoculture and more well-tended land, runs through eating fewer animals, not more. And no farming system, however good, answers for what happens to them.
