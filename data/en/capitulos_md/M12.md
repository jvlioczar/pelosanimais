# M12. "That's science fiction; we have real problems now"

**Thesis:** factory farming, with tens of billions of birds bred and killed every year, would also have seemed like science fiction a hundred years ago. An unlikely risk that could cause suffering on an immense scale can deserve attention even when it competes with certain, present problems. And much of what reduces these risks, such as teaching that all sentient beings matter, also helps animals now.

**Family:** M. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Robot suffering, space colonization, future risks? That's science fiction. Animals are suffering now."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Resources and attention are limited.
2. **P2.** Present problems are certain; future risks are speculative.
3. **P3.** We should prioritize what is certain and urgent.
4. **C.** So discussing risks of future suffering wastes resources that should go to today's real problems.

P1 is true, and P3 carries weight. The problem lies in P2, which treats "uncertain" as "irrelevant", and in the conclusion, which assumes the two concerns always compete.

The argument is **valid** in form, but P2 ignores that importance depends on probability and also on the size of what's at stake.

## 3. Answer for children

Before going out on your bike, you put on your helmet, even though you probably won't fall. Why? Because if you do fall, you could get badly hurt. Thinking about the future is similar: some bad things might never happen, but if they do, they could be enormous. So it's worth preparing, without stopping caring for those who need help now.

## 4. Short answer

Factory farming would also have seemed like science fiction a hundred years ago. An unlikely but enormous risk can deserve attention, as we do with pandemics and climate. And the two don't compete much: teaching that all sentient beings matter helps today's animals and protects against tomorrow's suffering.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you think preparing the world for a pandemic before it happened was science fiction?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

A risk's importance depends on three things, and the argument looks at only one:

| Factor | Question | What the argument considers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability | What's the chance it happens? | Considers it, and finds it low |
| Scale | How many beings would be affected, and how much? | Ignores it |
| Tractability | Can we do anything to reduce the risk? | Ignores it |

So-called **s-risks**, risks of suffering on an astronomical scale, combine uncertain probability with immense scale. That combination is what makes them worth attention.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Yesterday's unlikely is today's reality.** A hundred years ago, the idea of raising tens of billions of birds a year in closed sheds, with genetics selected to grow so fast that many can barely walk, would have seemed like fantasy. Technologies can multiply suffering faster than ethics keeps up.

**2. We already take future risks seriously.** Plans against pandemics, climate change, nuclear safety and asteroids deal with uncertain, future risks. No one calls them science fiction.

**3. The human test.** If there were a risk, even a small one, that billions of people would suffer extremely in the future because of decisions made now, no one would say thinking about it is a waste of time. S-risks include animals, artificial minds and humans; dismissing them because they mostly involve non-humans is speciesism.

**4. It isn't just AI.** Risk factors for future suffering include the expansion of animal farming, wild animal suffering, the possible creation of artificial minds, and political institutions that ignore those who don't vote. Each person can work on one part.

**5. Many actions serve both.** Widening the moral circle, strengthening consideration for all sentient beings and creating institutions that represent them help animals today and reduce risks tomorrow.

**6. Technology speeds up the scenarios, and morality is slow.** Large-scale animal exploitation was once a distant idea too: new technologies and population growth made it what it is today. Technology expands what can be done exponentially, so scenarios arrive faster than we imagine, while morality and societies change slowly. That is why it is worth preventing new catastrophes and fighting current ones at the same time.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: Pascal's mugging

The most serious version says: if any tiny risk multiplied by astronomical numbers can dominate our priorities, we'll be manipulated by imaginary scenarios. This is the problem known as "Pascal's mugging": accepting arguments like this leads to absurd decisions.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Not every future risk is tiny.** The s-risks seriously discussed involve real trends, such as the growth of factory farming and the rapid advance of AI, not infinitesimal probabilities.
2. **The answer to the problem is prudence, not indifference.** One can avoid betting everything on extreme scenarios and still devote part of one's efforts to plausible risks.
3. **The recommended actions are robust.** Advocating consideration for all sentient beings is good in almost any scenario, not only the unlikely ones.

| Level | Error | How it appears in M12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either we take care of the present, or the future |
| Epistemological | Ignoring scale | Assesses the risk only by its probability |
| Epistemological | Normalcy bias | Assumes the future will resemble the present |
| Moral | Speciesism | Dismisses risks that mostly involve non-humans |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports pandemic prevention and action on climate change.** Future and uncertain risks.
2. **Insures their home or car.** Against unlikely but serious risks.
3. **Recognizes that factory farming grew beyond what anyone imagined.** Which shows the "impossible" happens.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normalcy bias | The future seems a calm continuation of the present | Literature on risk perception |
| Temporal discounting | What's far away seems less important | Literature on hyperbolic discounting |
| Status quo bias | Today's world seems the stable default | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Neglect of probability | The risk is dismissed for being uncertain, without weighing the chance together with the size of the harm | [Senciência, 2024 (in Portuguese)](https://senciencia.org/2024/04/22/vieses-que-contribuem-para-a-negligencia-do-longo-prazo-e-dos-riscos-s/) |
| Evaluability bias | Today's results are easy to measure, so they seem more important than future risks that are hard to estimate | [Caviola et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1930297500006185) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "future, uncertain risks don't deserve attention while there are present problems".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pandemics | Preparing in advance would be wasteful | Covid-19 showed otherwise |
| Climate | Cutting emissions could wait | Contradicts climate science |
| Nuclear safety | Preventing unlikely accidents wouldn't be worthwhile | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that there is real, urgent suffering now, that resources are limited, and that discussions about the distant future can turn into empty speculation or an excuse to ignore the present. It's also right that arguments with tiny probabilities and huge numbers call for caution.

But ignoring the future is also a bet, and a risky one. Preventing is cheaper than fixing, and many of the best ways to protect the future are the same ones that help animals today.
