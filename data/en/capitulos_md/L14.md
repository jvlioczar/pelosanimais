# L14. "Vegans shouldn't watch violent films or play violent games"

**Thesis:** depicting violence isn't committing it. In a film or game where an animal is hunted, nobody was hunted: there are actors, effects, animation, lines of code. Veganism is about real victims, and that is where the accusation gets one thing right: there are productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killed during filming, and avoiding funding those works is consistent with veganism. Beyond that, the rule would also forbid the documentaries that show what happens in slaughterhouses, which are among the most violent images there are and are exactly what animal advocates ask people to watch.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND · **Note:** this chapter is about depicting violence. Refusing to look at what happens on farms is E12

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "A vegan who plays hunting games or watches a film with an animal dying has no right to talk about cruelty."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Veganism opposes violence against animals.
2. **P2.** Violent films and games present violence against animals as entertainment.
3. **P3.** Consuming violent entertainment normalises violence and funds those who produce it.
4. **C.** So vegans who watch violent films or play violent games are inconsistent.

P1 is right. P2 is true of some works and false of many others, which show violence precisely to criticise it. P3 mixes two things: funding real harm, which is a legitimate concern, and "normalising" depicted violence, whose link to real violence the evidence doesn't support.

The argument is **valid** only if depicting violence is the same as committing it. Its **soundness** fails there: veganism opposes real victims, and fiction has none.

## 3. Answer for children

In a cartoon or a game, when an animal gets hit, nobody really gets hurt: it's all pretend, made of drawings or computer code. Being vegan is about not hurting real animals. So a film where an animal was really hurt to shoot a scene is different, and many people prefer not to watch that kind of film.

## 4. Short answer

In a game or a fiction film, no animal was hunted: there are actors, effects and animation. Veganism is about real victims. When an animal was genuinely injured or killed to make a scene, the concern is legitimate, and avoiding those productions is consistent. Beyond that, the rule would forbid even the documentaries about slaughterhouses, which are among the most violent images there are and are exactly what vegans ask people to watch. And the leading US psychological association concluded there isn't enough evidence that violent games cause violent behaviour.

**Question back:** "Does someone who watches crime films support murder? If not, why would a vegan who watches a film with a hunting scene support hunting?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument lumps together three situations that call for different answers.

| Situation | Is there a real victim? | What veganism says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violence against animals that is staged, animated or digital | No | Nothing to refuse: nobody was harmed |
| Productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killed | Yes | A legitimate concern: avoiding funding them makes sense |
| Works that show real violence in order to expose it, such as documentaries on animal farming | Yes, but the work didn't cause it | Watching can be part of confronting the problem |

The accusation treats all three as if they were the first and the second at once.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Depicting isn't doing.** A war film isn't a war, and a crime novel isn't a murder. In a game where the character hunts an animal, no animal feels anything: there are pixels and code. Veganism opposes harming beings who feel, and in fiction there are none.

**2. Where there is a real victim, the criticism is right, and veganism agrees.** There are films in which animals were killed for real in front of the camera, such as the water buffalo sacrificed in a ritual filmed for *Apocalypse Now* (1979). And a *Hollywood Reporter* investigation in 2013 pointed to injuries and deaths of animals on productions that carried the "no animals were harmed" label. Avoiding funding works like these is exactly what veganism asks, as far as is possible and practicable.

**3. The rule would forbid exposés too.** Documentaries showing what happens in slaughterhouses and on farms are among the most violent images there are. If watching violence against animals were inconsistent, vegans couldn't watch or share those works, which are exactly what the defence of animals asks people to see. Ironically, it's the people who won't watch those videos who tend to avoid the real violence (E12).

**4. The evidence doesn't support violent games causing violence.** The American Psychological Association concluded in 2020 that there is insufficient scientific evidence of a causal link between violent video games and violent behaviour, while noting a small association with mild aggression, such as yelling and pushing. Concern about subtle effects is legitimate, but it doesn't turn playing into committing violence.

**5. It's the same structure as other charges of inconsistency.** As with the phone and artificial intelligence, the argument looks for a failing in the advocate so as not to discuss what happens to animals (L11, L13). Even if a vegan were inconsistent, that wouldn't make the exploitation acceptable.

**6. Many works show violence in order to criticise it.** A scene of cruelty to an animal may exist precisely to provoke revulsion and empathy. Judging a work by the presence of violence, rather than by what it does with it, treats denunciation and glorification alike.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [APA, resolution on violent video games (2020)](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2020/03/violent-video-games-behavior) | There is insufficient scientific evidence of a causal link between violent video games and violent behaviour; there is a small association with mild aggression | Updates the 2015 resolution |
| [NPR, on the *Hollywood Reporter* investigation (2013)](https://npr.org/2013/11/26/247392855/report-humane-association-covered-up-animal-abuse-on-hollywood-sets) | The investigation accused the body that grants the "no animals were harmed" label of covering up injuries and deaths of animals on productions | The body disputed the allegations |
| [The Vegan Society, definition of veganism](https://www.vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism) | Excluding the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable" | Applies also to entertainment that uses real animals |

### (d) The best reply: desensitisation

The serious version doesn't say playing is killing. It says repeated exposure to violence, even fictional, may reduce sensitivity to suffering, and that those who defend animals should cultivate exactly the opposite.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The evidence is weak for what is claimed.** Small associations with mild aggression don't amount to moral inconsistency.
2. **It's advice, not proof of hypocrisy.** It may apply to anyone concerned about their own wellbeing, and says nothing against veganism.
3. **It would forbid the exposé documentaries too.** And those, on the contrary, tend to make viewers more sensitive to real suffering.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in L14 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Depiction confused with act | Treats staged violence as violence committed |
| Logical | Tu quoque | The vegan's supposed failing becomes an excuse not to discuss the animals |
| Logical | Proves too much | It would also forbid the documentaries that expose exploitation |
| Epistemic | Causation without evidence | Assumes that playing violence produces violence |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **They watch crime and war films.** Without thinking themselves in favour of murder or war.
2. **They don't want to see slaughterhouse videos.** Which show the real violence behind their own plate.
3. **They don't demand the same of meat eaters.** Who commit, through others, the violence the film only stages.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up L14 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tu quoque | The inconsistency attributed to the other eases one's own choice | Literature on the tu quoque fallacy |
| The meat paradox | Pointing out the other's failing reduces discomfort with one's own | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| Information avoidance | Avoiding real violence while debating fictional violence | Literature on deliberate information avoidance |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "someone who opposes a form of violence can't consume works that depict it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pacifists | They couldn't watch war films | It would silence much of anti-war art |
| Opponents of violence against women | They couldn't watch works that expose it | Confuses denunciation with glorification |
| Animal advocates | They couldn't watch or share slaughterhouse documentaries | The criterion would destroy the exposé itself |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets two things right that deserve acknowledgement. The first is that there are productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killed, and that the "no animals were harmed" label hasn't always been reliable. Avoiding funding those works is consistent with veganism, and many animal advocates already do so. The second is that it's worth paying attention to what entertainment teaches about animals, for example when it treats hunting as fun.

But depicting violence isn't committing it. Veganism is about real victims, and fiction has none. If the rule held, it would also forbid the images that have most helped people see what happens to animals.
