# C13. "I honor the animals I eat"

**Thesis:** gratitude is a sincere feeling in the person who eats, but it changes nothing for the animal: they lose their life all the same. Honoring someone usually means respecting their interests, and an animal's greatest interest is to go on living without suffering. When eating animals isn't necessary to survive, the clearest way to honor them is not to have them killed. Many Indigenous traditions thank the hunted animal in subsistence settings, and that deserves respect; but meat bought at the supermarket comes from anonymous animals, bred and slaughtered on an industrial scale.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I don't eat on autopilot. I thank the animal before every meal and waste nothing. I honor the animals I eat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We are part of nature, and every living thing feeds on others.
2. **P2.** The moral problem lies in waste, indifference and cruelty, not in the act of eating.
3. **P3.** Someone who eats with gratitude, without waste, recognizes the animal's value.
4. **C.** So eating animals with that attitude is morally acceptable.

P1 is true, but doesn't say what we should do (see C7). P2 is the decisive premise and is false: for the animal, the main problem is being killed. P3 describes the attitude of the one who eats, not what happens to the one who is eaten.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2.

## 3. Answer for children

Saying thank you is lovely. But if someone took your favorite toy and then said "thank you very much", the toy would still be gone. For the animal, a thank-you doesn't give their life back. The best way to show you care about them is to let them live.

## 4. Short answer

The gratitude is sincere, but it changes nothing for the animal: they lose their life all the same. Honoring someone usually means respecting what matters to them, and what matters most to an animal is staying alive and not suffering. When we don't need to eat animals to live, the clearest way to honor them is not to have them killed.

**Question to ask back:** "If the animal could choose, would they prefer to be thanked or to stay alive?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument changes | For whom | What stays the same |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The attitude of the one who eats | For the one who eats | The animal's death |
| Waste | For the environment, in part | Breeding, transport and slaughter |
| Awareness of where food comes from | For the one who eats | What the animal loses |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Gratitude lives in the one who eats, not in the one who is eaten.** The feeling can be real and deep, but it doesn't reach the animal. They were bred, transported and slaughtered the same way.

**2. Honoring means respecting interests.** When we say we honor a person, we mean we take seriously what matters to them. Killing them and then thanking them wouldn't be honoring them. The same holds for an animal who has an interest in living.

**3. Not wasting is good, but doesn't solve the main thing.** Using the whole animal reduces waste, but doesn't reduce the number of animals killed.

**4. Context matters.** In many Indigenous traditions, thanking the hunted animal is part of a subsistence relationship without easy alternatives, and those traditions deserve respect (see D7). Someone who buys meat at the supermarket, with alternatives available, is in a very different situation: the animal came from an industrial, anonymous supply chain.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: indifference is the real evil

The most serious version says the problem with modern animal farming is turning animals into things, and that eating mindfully and with gratitude restores a lost relationship of respect.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Indifference is a problem, but not the only one.** An animal killed with respect is still dead.
2. **Awareness leads to a question.** Someone who truly thinks about the animal ends up asking whether they need to kill them.
3. **Respect shows in what we do.** If the attitude changes nothing for the animal, the respect stays in words.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing attitude with consequence | The eater's gratitude is treated as if it changed what happens to the animal |
| Moral | Ritualization | A symbolic gesture replaces the question of whether killing is necessary |
| Moral | Borrowing a context | Subsistence practices are used to justify urban consumption |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they honor the animal.** And takes from them what they have most to lose.
2. **Talks about awareness.** And avoids the question of necessity.
3. **Cites subsistence traditions.** And buys meat from an industrial supply chain.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the one who kills feels gratitude and respect, the death is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trophy hunting with a thanksgiving ritual | It would be acceptable | Many trophy hunters say they respect their prey |
| Dogs and cats killed with gratitude | It would be acceptable | Almost no one accepts that |
| Letting the animal live | Honors their most basic interest | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that indifference toward animals is a problem, that wasting is worse than using, that thinking about where food comes from is better than eating on autopilot, and that subsistence traditions that thank the animal deserve respect.

But the gratitude of the one who eats changes nothing for the one who is eaten. When killing isn't necessary, the clearest way to honor an animal is to let them live.
