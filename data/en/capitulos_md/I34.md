# I34. "The bird was born in a cage; it wouldn't survive if released"

**Thesis:** it's true that a bird raised in a cage may not survive if suddenly released, so anyone who already has one should keep caring for it, with as much space and enrichment as possible. But that doesn't justify buying or breeding others: each new bird is born to live in a cage. And the bird and exotic pet market feeds trafficking: a 2001 estimate by Brazil's anti-trafficking network RENCTAS put the number of wild animals taken from nature in Brazil at about 38 million a year, with only 1 in 10 reaching the buyer alive.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My bird was born in a cage, it's legal. If I let it go, it dies. And it sings because it's happy."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Birds raised in captivity don't know how to survive in the wild.
2. **P2.** Legal birds don't come from trafficking.
3. **P3.** The bird sings and eats, signs that it's doing well.
4. **C.** So keeping birds in cages is acceptable.

P1 is true for the bird that already exists, and it's a reason to keep caring for it. P2 is partly true, but the legal and illegal markets overlap. P3 confuses surviving with living well. The conclusion about breeding new birds doesn't follow.

The argument is **valid** for the bird that already exists. It fails when used to justify breeding and buying.

## 3. Answer for children

Birds were born to fly far, in flocks, looking for food and choosing where to stay. In a cage, they just hop from one perch to another. If a bird was already born caged, it's best to look after it well, with plenty of space. But it's better not to buy others, so no new bird is born to live caged.

## 4. Short answer

It's true: a bird raised in a cage may not survive if suddenly released. If you already have one, keep caring for it, with as much space as possible. But that doesn't justify buying or breeding others: each new bird is born to live caged. And the bird market feeds trafficking: a 2001 estimate by Brazil's network RENCTAS put the figure at about 38 million wild animals taken each year in Brazil, with only 1 in 10 reaching the buyer alive. So I ask: is it fair to bring into the world more birds who will spend their whole lives caged?

**Question to ask back:** "If the problem is that it wouldn't survive outside, is the solution to look after it well, or to buy more birds that also wouldn't survive outside?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Question | Bird already in captivity | New bird bought or bred |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is releasing it a good idea? | No, without proper rehabilitation | — |
| What to do? | Care for it, with space and enrichment | Don't buy or breed |
| What does the market encourage? | — | More breeding and, indirectly, trafficking |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Surviving isn't living well.** Birds fly, live in groups and explore large areas. In a cage, even well fed, they are deprived of almost everything they naturally do. Song may be a territorial or mating call, not a sign of contentment.

**2. The argument is about the bird, not the market.** That a captive-bred bird wouldn't survive outside is a reason to care for it, not to bring others into the same situation.

**3. The legal and illegal markets overlap.** Brazil's National Network to Fight Wild Animal Trafficking (RENCTAS) estimated in 2001 that about 38 million wild animals were taken from nature each year in Brazil, and that only 1 in 10 reached the final buyer alive. The estimate is old, but it remains the reference cited.

**4. Exotic pets and aquariums have the same problem.** Reptiles, primates, parrots and aquarium fish have needs that are hard to meet in a home, and demand for them encourages capture and breeding.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Jornal da USP](https://jornal.usp.br/?p=790423) | Brazil accounts for the trafficking of about 38 million wild animals a year | Cites the 2001 RENCTAS report (in Portuguese) |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: buying from a legal breeder fights trafficking

The most serious version says a legal breeder offers an alternative to the trafficked animal, and that banning breeding would push buyers to the illegal market.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The legal market keeps demand going.** Keeping caged birds still seems normal, and some buyers turn to trafficking for price or species.
2. **A legal bird also lives caged.** Legal origin doesn't change life in a cage.
3. **There are ways to love birds without caging them.** Watching free birds, planting native trees and supporting rehabilitation centers.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question isn't whether the bird that already exists should be suddenly released, but whether it's fair to bring new birds into the world to live in a cage. Caring for those that exist and not buying others solves both.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I34 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Undue generalization | What holds for the bird that exists is used to justify breeding |
| Epistemological | Song as proof of happiness | A behavior is read as contentment |
| Moral | The owner's pleasure above the bird's life | Hearing the song justifies a whole life caged |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the bird wouldn't survive outside.** And buys others that also wouldn't survive outside.
2. **Likes birds.** And keeps them unable to fly.
3. **Condemns trafficking.** And keeps up the demand that feeds it.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I34 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selective anthropomorphism | Song is read as human happiness | General literature on animal cognition |
| Status quo bias | The cage seems like the bird's normal place | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the animal wouldn't survive outside, keeping it caged, and breeding others, is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primates raised as pets | They'd be acceptable | They're targets of trafficking and have complex needs |
| Caring for the bird that already exists | It's the right answer | Here the argument gets it right |
| Breeders who breed birds for sale | They'd be acceptable | Each new bird is born to live caged |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that suddenly releasing a captive-bred bird can kill it, that many people love their birds and care for them well, and that legal breeding is better than trafficking.

But that calls for caring well for the birds that already exist, not for buying or breeding others for the same life.
