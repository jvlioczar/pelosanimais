# B11. "Live animal transport is regulated and follows standards to ensure the animals' welfare"

**Thesis:** there are standards, but they allow very long journeys and are poorly enforced. In the European Union, a bovine can travel 14 hours, rest 1 and travel another 14, and a European Parliament committee of inquiry concluded in 2021 that the rules were repeatedly breached in member states. Australia passed a law in 2024 ending live sheep exports by sea from 2028, recognizing that rules weren't solving the problem. And even when fully followed, the standards regulate the journey of an animal who will be killed at the destination.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** some activists think NGOs spend too much time and money on reforms, such as transport rules, that don't end exploitation; others see them as a real reduction in suffering and a step toward bigger changes. The discussion remains open

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Animal transport is regulated, there are rules for everything: journey time, water, rest. They exist to ensure the animals' welfare."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There are laws regulating live animal transport: journey time, space, water, food and rest.
2. **P2.** These laws are based on technical knowledge about animal welfare.
3. **P3.** If the journey follows technical standards, the animal doesn't suffer significantly.
4. **C.** So live animal transport is acceptable.

P1 is true in many countries. P2 is partly true. P3 fails twice: the standards allow long, stressful journeys, and they are often not followed.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine spending a whole day in a crowded truck, not understanding where you're going, hot or cold, and sometimes without water. There are rules for these journeys, but they allow the journey to be very long, and they aren't always followed. And at the end of the journey, the animal goes to the slaughterhouse.

## 4. Short answer

There are standards, but they allow very long journeys: in the European Union, a bovine can travel 14 hours, rest 1 and travel another 14. And they are poorly enforced: a European Parliament committee of inquiry concluded in 2021 that they were repeatedly breached. Australia decided to end live sheep exports by sea from 2028, because rules weren't solving the problem. And even when followed, the standards regulate the journey of an animal who will be killed at the destination.

**Question to ask back:** "If the standards solved the problem, why did a country decide to ban live sheep exports by sea altogether?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats the existence of a standard as proof that the problem is solved:

| Question | What the standard says | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How long can the animal travel? | In the EU, cattle can travel 14 hours, rest 1 and travel another 14; pigs, up to 24 hours with access to water | Long journeys are legal |
| Is the standard followed? | Member states must enforce it | The European Parliament found repeated breaches and no Commission proceedings against the countries |
| And at sea? | Specific rules for ships | Australia decided to ban sheep exports by sea |
| And at the destination? | — | Slaughter |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The standards allow a lot.** EU Regulation 1/2005, one of the most detailed in the world, lets cattle travel 14 hours, rest 1 hour and travel another 14, and pigs travel up to 24 hours with access to water. In 2022, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommended shorter journeys, more space and temperature limits inside vehicles.

**2. The standards are poorly enforced.** The European Parliament's committee of inquiry on the protection of animals during transport concluded in 2021 that the regulation was not consistently complied with in member states, and that the European Commission had opened no infringement proceedings against any country despite repeated violations.

**3. Some problems can't be fixed with rules.** After mass deaths on ships, Australia passed a law in 2024 banning live sheep exports by sea from 1 May 2028. Cattle and goats are still exported.

**4. Reform is slow.** In December 2023, the European Commission proposed a new regulation, with a 9-hour limit for journeys to slaughter. The proposal received thousands of amendments, and negotiations stalled.

**5. The destination doesn't change.** Even a perfect journey ends at the slaughterhouse. The standard regulates how the animal arrives at its death, not whether it should die (B1).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: standards improve over time

The most serious version acknowledges the failures and says the standards are being revised, that welfare science guides those revisions, and that the way forward is better enforcement, not condemning transport itself.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Improving isn't solving.** Even the most ambitious proposals keep journeys of many hours and slaughter at the destination.
2. **Experience shows the limits.** When a country concluded that rules weren't enough, it chose to ban, not to regulate better.
3. **The underlying question remains.** Transport exists because there is demand for animal products; reducing that demand also reduces the journeys.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing the rule with the practice | The existence of the rule is taken as proof it's followed |
| Epistemological | Not knowing what the standards say | Assumes the rule prevents long journeys, when it allows them |
| Moral | Regulating the means without asking about the end | Discusses the conditions of the journey and not the destination |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Trusts the standard.** And doesn't know how many hours it allows.
2. **Says the videos are exceptions.** Without knowing that the European Parliament itself found repeated breaches.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same journey for a dog.** A whole day in a crowded truck would be seen as cruelty.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B11 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority bias | The existence of a law conveys safety, regardless of its content | General literature on obedience and authority |
| Motivated reasoning | The standard is a way of not thinking about what happens on the road | General literature on motivated cognition |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a practice is regulated, it's acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live sheep exports by ship | They would be acceptable while regulated | Australia decided to ban them |
| Battery cages, when they were legal in the EU | They would be acceptable | They were banned in 2012 |
| Any legal practice | It would be right | Legal isn't the same as just (D9) |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that there are standards, that some were based on welfare science, and that they prevent some of the worst abuses. It's also true that improving transport reduces real suffering, and many organizations devote effort to it.

Some activists think NGOs spend too much time and money on reforms of this kind, which don't end exploitation; others see them as a real reduction in suffering and a step toward bigger changes. The discussion remains open. What isn't open is that the standard, however good, regulates the journey of an animal who will be killed at the end.
