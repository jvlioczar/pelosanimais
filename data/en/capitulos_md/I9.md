# I9. "A fur coat is natural and comes from regulated farms"

**Thesis:** the fur sold today comes almost entirely from farms where mink, foxes and chinchillas spend their lives in wire cages, far from any natural life, until they are killed for their fur. Being "natural" doesn't lower the impact: a 2011 study by the Dutch consultancy CE Delft concluded that 1 kg of mink fur has a higher environmental impact than textiles in 17 of 18 categories. The United Kingdom banned fur farms in 2000, and in 2020 Denmark culled about 15 million mink because of a coronavirus mutation.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Fur is natural and lasts a lifetime. And it comes from regulated farms, not from hunting."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Animal fur is a natural, durable material.
2. **P2.** Fake fur is made from petroleum-based plastic.
3. **P3.** Fur farms are regulated.
4. **C.** So wearing fur is an acceptable choice, even better for the environment.

P1 is true. So is P2. P3 says nothing about what the rules allow. And the conclusion ignores the impact of raising the animals.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3 and in the environmental comparison.

## 3. Answer for children

To make fur coats, foxes and mink live in wire cages their whole lives, unable to run or dig, and then they're killed. Today there are warm, beautiful coats made of other materials. That's why many countries have banned these farms.

## 4. Short answer

Fur is natural, but think about where it comes from: almost all the fur sold today comes from farms where mink and foxes live in wire cages until they're killed. And being natural doesn't lower the impact: a 2011 CE Delft study found a higher environmental impact than textiles in 17 of 18 categories, because of feed and manure. The United Kingdom banned fur farms in 2000, and in 2020 Denmark culled about 15 million mink because of a coronavirus mutation. But the question remains: is a coat worth a whole life in a cage?

**Question to ask back:** "If fur comes from such good farms, why have so many countries decided to ban them?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| "It's natural" | Wild animals raised in wire cages |
| "It's regulated" | The rules allow small cages and killing by gas or electrocution |
| "It pollutes less than synthetic" | Raising the animals takes a lot of feed and produces a lot of manure |
| "Farmed crocodile isn't hunting" | Animals raised in tanks and killed for their skin |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Life on the farms isn't natural.** Mink, foxes and chinchillas are wild animals, with behaviors such as swimming, digging and roaming large areas, raised in wire cages.

**2. The environmental impact is high.** CE Delft calculated that a mink eats almost 50 kg of feed over its life and that 1 kg of fur has a higher impact than cotton, acrylic, polyester and wool in 17 of 18 environmental categories, including climate change.

**3. Countries have banned the practice.** The United Kingdom banned fur farms in 2000, and several European countries followed. The farms have also become a health risk: in 2020, Denmark ordered the culling of all its farmed mink, about 15 million, after finding a coronavirus variant circulating among them.

**4. There are alternatives beyond plastic.** Recycled, plant-based and second-hand fabrics provide warmth and durability without raising animals (H16).

**5. Whoever condemns fur needs to explain food.** Many people who don't consider themselves animal advocates oppose fur. Asked why, they tend to say it's wrong to make animals suffer and kill them for something we don't need. The philosopher Nathan Nobis notes that these reasons seem to apply to meat, milk and eggs as well, and that whoever uses them has three consistent ways out: revise their view on fur, revise their view on food, or point to a relevant difference between the two cases (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 51). One difference often pointed out is that fur is a luxury and food a necessity. But for those with access to alternatives, animal-based food is also dispensable (G1), and the coat and the steak come from animals who spent their lives confined and were killed young. The same challenge applies to leather (I8).

**6. Mink farms and the pandemic.** In November 2020, after coronavirus variants were found in mink farmed for fur, Denmark announced the culling of every mink in the country, more than 17 million, in a decision that later faced legal challenges. Jeff Sebo (2022) notes that the debate dealt almost only with human costs, and that, when the animals were discussed, the only question was whether they should be killed: in an epidemic among humans, no one would think only of that. The case shows the risks these farms create and how little those who live in them are considered.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: fur lasts much longer than synthetics

The most serious version says that a well-kept fur coat lasts decades, while cheap synthetic clothes are soon thrown away, and that durability reduces the impact per year of use.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Durability isn't exclusive to fur.** Good-quality clothes in other materials also last decades.
2. **The initial impact is much higher.** The calculated difference is too large to be made up by time in use alone.
3. **The animal remains at the center.** Even if fur lasted forever, the animal lived caged and was killed for it.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to raise wild animals in cages and kill them for a coat, when there are other ways to keep warm and dress well.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | "Natural" is taken to mean good |
| Epistemological | Incomplete environmental comparison | Considers the material's end of life and ignores raising the animals |
| Moral | Fashion above suffering | A coat justifies an animal's whole life in a cage |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says it's natural.** And accepts wild animals in wire cages.
2. **Criticizes plastic.** And ignores the impact of feed and manure.
3. **Wouldn't wear dog or cat fur.** And wears fox and mink.
4. **Condemns fur and eats meat.** The reasons given against fur, killing and causing suffering for something dispensable, also apply to meat, milk and eggs. Either one of the positions is revised, or a relevant difference between them is pointed out.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the material is natural and regulated, using it is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dog and cat fur | It would be acceptable | Banned in the European Union |
| Fur farm bans | They'd be unjustified | They were based on welfare and public health |
| Second-hand clothes and recycled fabrics | They meet the same need | Without raising animals |
| The opposite argument, against fur | Whoever rejects fur for killing and causing suffering without need would also have to reject meat, milk and eggs | The fur-and-food consistency challenge (Nobis, 2016, p. 51); see point 5 of (b) |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that many synthetic fabrics are made from oil and shed microplastics, and that durable clothes are better than disposable ones. It's also true that there are rules for fur farms.

But the plastic problem isn't solved by raising animals in cages, and the rules allow a life that several countries have judged unacceptable.
