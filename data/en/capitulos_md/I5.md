# I5. "Bees make extra honey"

**Thesis:** honey is the bees' food reserve for periods without flowers, not a surplus. In beekeeping it's taken and often replaced with sugar syrup. Honeybees are kept like domestic animals and can compete with native bees, which are the main pollinators in many ecosystems; there are some 20,000 bee species worldwide. And there is growing evidence that bees have experiences, which the New York Declaration recognizes as a realistic possibility.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Honey is natural. Without beekeepers, bees disappear. Bees don't even feel anything."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Bees produce more honey than they consume in good seasons.
2. **P2.** Beekeeping helps maintain bee populations, which are important for pollination.
3. **P3.** Bees are insects, with little or no capacity to suffer.
4. **C.** So eating honey causes no harm and even helps bees.

P1 is partly true. P2 confuses honeybees, which are kept animals, with native bees, which are in decline. P3 is increasingly disputed by science.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Bees make honey so they have food in winter, when there are no flowers. When people take the honey, they often give the bees sugar water instead. Besides, there are lots of wild bees that live freely and help plants. They're the ones that need protecting.

## 4. Short answer

Honey is the bees' food reserve, not a surplus; in beekeeping it's often replaced with sugar syrup. The bees in decline are wild native species, not honeybees, which are kept like domestic animals and can compete with them for flowers and spread disease. Buying honey doesn't help native bees. And there's growing evidence that bees have experiences.

**Question to ask back:** "If the problem is the decline of native bees, does keeping more honeybees help?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two groups of bees:

| Group | Situation | Relationship to honey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honeybees (*Apis mellifera*) | Kept by beekeepers, like domestic animals | Produce commercial honey |
| Native bees (around 20,000 species worldwide) | Many in decline from habitat loss and pesticides | Not helped by buying honey |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Honey is a reserve, not a surplus.** Bees store honey for periods without flowers. Beekeeping removes that store and often replaces it with sugar syrup, which is nutritionally inferior.

**2. Honeybees are kept animals.** They aren't threatened with extinction for lack of beekeeping. The decline that worries scientists is that of native bees.

**3. Honeybees can harm native bees.** A 2017 review found that most competition studies indicated negative effects of managed bees on wild bees, and that a large share of the disease studies pointed to transmission risks. The evidence is still debated, but the claim that honey "saves the bees" doesn't hold up.

**4. Bees may have experiences.** The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) recognizes a realistic possibility of consciousness in insects. Research shows bees learn, play and respond flexibly to stimuli.

**5. Beekeeping also kills bees.** When honey is harvested, when hives are transported and when queens are replaced.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Mallinger, Gaines-Day and Gratton, 2017, *PLoS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0189268) | 53% of competition studies found negative effects of managed bees on wild bees; 70% of pathogen studies pointed to potential negative effects | A systematic review; later studies find mixed results |
| [The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024)](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/declaration) | A realistic possibility of consciousness in insects | Signed by hundreds of researchers |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: beekeeping helps crop pollination

The most serious version points out that many crops depend on pollination by honeybees, and that beekeeping is important for food production.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Pollination doesn't depend on eating honey.** It's a service that can be paid for separately, as already happens.
2. **Native bees pollinate too.** And often more efficiently for certain crops.
3. **It doesn't answer the question about honey.** Buying honey isn't the same as supporting pollination.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Honey is a borderline case, and there are honest debates about insect experience. But the argument used to defend it, that it saves the bees, is false. If there's uncertainty about what bees feel, and easy alternatives to honey, caution recommends leaving it to them.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they want to save the bees.** But doesn't support native bees, which are in decline.
2. **Accepts that honey is the bees' food.** And replaces it with sugar.
3. **Doesn't eat other insect products.** The "they don't feel" criterion is used only here.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a species is kept in large numbers, it's protected".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chickens | They'd be protected, because there are billions | This is the argument of B6 |
| Honeybees | They'd be helping bees in general | They can harm native bees |
| Farmed animals in general | They'd be fine, because they're numerous | Ignores the individual |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The decline of bees is a real and serious problem, and pollination is essential for food production. Well-run beekeeping can play a role in agriculture, and insect experience is indeed an open question.

But buying honey doesn't protect the bees that are in decline, and may even harm them. Protecting bees means protecting their habitats, reducing pesticides and planting flowers, not eating their food reserve.
