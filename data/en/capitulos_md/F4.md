# F4. "What about the insects you kill while driving?"

**Thesis:** accidental and unavoidable harm is different from intentional and avoidable harm. Killing insects unintentionally while driving or walking is a side effect of ordinary activities that are hard to avoid; paying for an animal to be raised and killed is a direct choice. Morality distinguishes the two in every area, and the question doesn't reveal an inconsistency, only the absence of that distinction.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You step on ants. Your car kills mosquitoes. So you're a killer too."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Every human activity kills animals, including driving, walking and farming.
2. **P2.** Vegans also engage in these activities.
3. **P3.** If vegans accept killing animals in these activities, they can't condemn those who kill them for food.
4. **C.** So the vegan condemnation is inconsistent.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: accepting accidental harms that are hard to avoid doesn't commit anyone to accepting intentional harms that are easy to avoid.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which ignores the difference between causing something intentionally and causing it as a side effect.

## 3. Answer for children

If you're walking and step on an ant without seeing it, it was an accident. If you look for an ant to step on on purpose, that's different. We can't avoid everything, but we can avoid hurting on purpose when we don't need to.

## 4. Short answer

Killing insects unintentionally while driving is different from paying for an animal to be raised and killed. One is accidental and hard to avoid; the other is intentional and easy to avoid. Morality makes this distinction everywhere: a traffic accident isn't the same as running someone over on purpose. No one is asking you to avoid every possible harm, only not to cause the harm you can easily avoid.

**Question to ask back:** "For you, is running someone over by accident the same as running them over on purpose?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument ignores two differences:

| Feature | Insects on the windshield | Animals raised for food |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intention | None: a side effect | Direct: the animal is raised to be killed |
| Avoidability | Hard: it would mean not getting around | Easy: just choose another food |
| Scale of avoidable harm | Small | Billions of animals a year |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Intention matters.** Throughout morality and law, causing harm on purpose is different from causing it as a side effect of a legitimate activity.

**2. Avoidability matters.** Veganism asks us to avoid exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" (D7). Not getting around isn't practicable; changing what's on the plate is.

**3. The argument is the nirvana fallacy.** Since it's impossible to avoid all harm, it concludes that no harm needs to be avoided. But reducing avoidable harm is still better.

**4. The question about insects leads to the boundary.** If the problem is knowing which animals feel, the discussion moves to A3, and it doesn't justify consuming animals who clearly do feel.

**5. Vegans also try to reduce these harms.** Many avoid killing insects when they can. But the inconsistency of some people doesn't change what's right.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [The Vegan Society, definition of veganism](https://www.vegansociety.com/go-vegan/definition-veganism) | Veganism seeks to exclude exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" | The demand is to avoid what's avoidable |
| [Our World in Data, animals slaughtered](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-get-slaughtered-every-day) | Billions of animals are slaughtered every year for food | Shows the scale of intentional, avoidable harm |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the difference is only one of degree

The most serious version says that whoever drives knows they'll kill insects, so the harm is foreseeable and accepted. The difference between that and buying meat would only be one of degree, not of kind.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Even if it were degree, degree matters.** Billions of animals raised and killed don't compare to insects hit by chance.
2. **Foreseeing isn't aiming.** Knowing a side effect may occur is different from having the harm as one's goal.
3. **The cost of avoiding differs.** Avoiding the first would mean giving up essential activities; avoiding the second means swapping foods.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If not all harm can be avoided, none would need to be |
| Logical | Tu quoque | Accuses the other of doing something similar instead of answering |
| Moral | Confusing intention with side effect | Treats harms of different kinds as equal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Distinguishes accidents from intentional acts in everything.** In traffic, at work, in law.
2. **Doesn't think drivers are killers.** Which shows they recognize the difference.
3. **Accepts avoiding avoidable harm on other subjects.** Such as waste or pollution.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I cause harm unintentionally, I may cause harm on purpose".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traffic | Whoever once ran someone over by accident could do it on purpose | No one accepts this |
| The environment | Since every activity pollutes a little, polluting on purpose would be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Work | Since accidents happen, causing them would be acceptable | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The question points to something real: living causes harm, including to animals, and no one can avoid it completely. It's also healthy to remember that veganism isn't purity and that insects may deserve moral consideration.

But the impossibility of avoiding all harm doesn't excuse us from avoiding the harm we can. The difference between the insect on the windshield and the chicken on the plate isn't one of purity; it's one of choice.
