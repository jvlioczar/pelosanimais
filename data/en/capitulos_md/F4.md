# F4. "Should we stop lions from hunting them, then?"

**Thesis:** what lions do doesn't justify what we do. A lion isn't a moral agent, has no alternative and doesn't choose; we have alternatives and we choose. And that there is suffering in nature doesn't authorize causing more. The question also touches, as an additional point, a serious issue: the suffering of wild animals. Taking it seriously doesn't lead to "locking up predators", but to studying carefully how to reduce suffering without causing greater harm.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Will you lock up every predator? So nature is immoral?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** If animal suffering matters, it also matters when caused by predators.
2. **P2.** It would be absurd to intervene in nature to prevent predation.
3. **P3.** So whoever takes animal suffering seriously reaches an absurd conclusion.
4. **C.** So the premise that animal suffering matters must be rejected or weakened.

P1 is true. The problem lies in P2 and P3: recognizing the problem doesn't commit anyone to absurd solutions, and the conclusion doesn't apply to what we do to animals.

The argument is **valid** as a reductio ad absurdum. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which confuses "taking seriously" with "intervening in any way".

## 3. Answer for children

A lion hunts because it needs to and doesn't know how to do anything else. We do, and we can choose to eat other things. So what the lion does is no excuse for what we do. And there's more: in nature, many animals suffer from hunger, cold, disease and predators. That's sad, and some people study how to help, for example by vaccinating sick wild animals. But suffering in nature doesn't mean we can cause even more.

## 4. Short answer

What lions do doesn't justify what we do. A lion has no alternative, can't reflect on what it does and isn't morally answerable for it; we can, and we are. And that there's suffering in nature is no reason to cause more: if there's a lot of suffering we don't control, that's all the more reason not to add the suffering we do control. As an additional point, the suffering of wild animals is a serious issue, and researchers are studying ways to reduce it, such as vaccination and rescue during disasters. No one needs to "lock up predators": poorly thought-out interventions can cause more harm.

**Question to ask back:** "A lion has no choice, but we do. Why should what it does define what we may do?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes up two questions:

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Should we stop causing animals suffering? | Yes, whenever we can avoid it |
| Should we intervene to reduce suffering caused by others, including in nature? | Perhaps, carefully, when there are safe and effective ways |

The duty not to cause harm is stronger and simpler than the duty to help. The difficulty of the second question doesn't affect the first.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. What lions do doesn't justify what we do.** A lion isn't a moral agent: it has no alternative, can't reflect on what it does and can't be held responsible (C5). We are agents, we have alternatives and we answer for what we choose. We don't take rules for ourselves from other animals' behavior on any other subject.

**2. The question doesn't favor meat.** Suffering in nature, whatever the answer about it, doesn't make suffering on farms acceptable.

**3. Not causing harm comes before helping.** Even those who don't think we should intervene in nature accept that we shouldn't cause unnecessary suffering. The question isn't punishing predators, but whether we, as agents, can help the victims.

**4. As an additional point: the issue is serious, and already studied.** The field that studies wild animal suffering analyzes interventions such as vaccination against disease, rescue during disasters and fertility control, always weighing the ecological risks.

**5. Poorly thought-out interventions can make everything worse.** Removing predators from an ecosystem can cause overpopulation, starvation and more suffering. That's why taking the problem seriously demands caution, not absurd solutions.

**6. Rights theories admit the question is legitimate.** The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane, who defends rights based on animals' interests, acknowledges that such a theory needs to say whether a gazelle chased by a lion has the same right not to suffer as a pig on a factory farm (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 47–48). He leaves the question open and notes that not every interest becomes a right: the strength of each interest has to be weighed case by case (p. 144). Taking the question seriously is part of an honest debate. But the difference in point 1 still stands: on the farm, the suffering is imposed by agents who can choose otherwise.

**7. "The prey can escape" confuses the species with the individual.** Tom Regan (2004), the leading animal rights theorist, accepts defending a human child under attack, but not a wild prey animal, because the prey would be able to escape: the proof would be that its species hasn't gone extinct. Cunha (2022, pp. 161–162) replies that the argument slides from the species to the individual, and most prey are young animals who escape nothing. And showing that helping isn't necessary doesn't show that helping would be wrong.

**8. The question is addressed to us, not to the lion.** The Brazilian philosopher Sônia T. Felipe (2015), an animal rights advocate, criticized the idea of intervening by saying it would demand of animals the human duty not to kill. But no one asks anything of the lion: the question is what we, who can reflect and choose, should do about the prey's suffering (Cunha, 2022, pp. 158–159).

**9. Judging the predator is one thing; preventing the harm is another.** The biocentrist Paul Taylor (1986) said that judging predation would mean attributing wickedness to the predator. But a small child who hurts another is not to blame either, and we still prevent the harm. Asking whether someone is guilty and asking whether we should stop the harm are separate questions, as Steve Sapontzis (1987), the economist Tyler Cowen (2003) and Martha Nussbaum (2006) note; in *Frontiers of Justice*, Nussbaum accepts in principle limiting predation (Cunha, 2022, pp. 158–159).

**10. What if we were the prey?** The philosopher Charles Fink (2005) imagines aliens far more intelligent than us who needed to hunt us to live. No one would say it would be wrong to rescue the humans being hunted. If the answer changes only because the victims changed species, the criterion is species (Cunha, 2022, p. 160, n. 84).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: if we don't intervene, animal suffering weighs little

The most serious version says that if we aren't willing to intervene in nature, it's because deep down we don't give much weight to animal suffering, and then eating meat wouldn't be serious either.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Not intervening doesn't mean giving no weight.** We often don't intervene because we don't know how to help without causing more harm.
2. **The asymmetry between causing and allowing also exists among humans.** Not saving everyone who suffers in the world doesn't make it acceptable to cause someone suffering.
3. **Many animal advocates do support intervening.** The issue is far from ignored.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Straw man | Attributes to vegans a proposal to lock up predators |
| Logical | False reductio ad absurdum | Assumes taking the problem seriously leads to absurd solutions |
| Moral | Confusing causing with allowing | Treats the duty not to cause harm as a duty to prevent all harm |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Helps wild animals in other situations.** Many people support disaster rescues and care for injured animals.
2. **Doesn't think human suffering in nature is irrelevant.** Natural disasters move people.
3. **Uses nature as an argument only when convenient.** No one draws rules for human life from it on other subjects.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if we can't prevent all suffering, we needn't avoid causing our own".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diseases | Since there are natural diseases, we could spread disease | No one accepts this |
| Natural disasters | Since there are earthquakes, we could destroy houses | No one accepts this |
| Human suffering | Since we haven't ended all hunger, we could cause hunger | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The provocation gets one thing right: if animal suffering matters, it matters in nature too. That's a conclusion many philosophers take seriously, and it goes beyond veganism. It's also true that intervening in nature is hard and risky.

But recognizing this problem doesn't weaken the argument against causing suffering. On the contrary: in a world with so much suffering we don't yet know how to reduce, the least we can do is not add what we can avoid.
