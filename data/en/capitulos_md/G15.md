# G15. "My doctor told me to eat meat"

**Thesis:** medical advice deserves to be taken seriously and understood. If it's general rather than tied to the patient's case, it carries less weight than institutional positions. The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and the British Dietetic Association recognize that well-planned plant-based diets are adequate. Many doctors receive little training in nutrition, and some dietitians report insufficient training in plant-based diets; since science is a process, every professional needs constant updating. There may be specific clinical reasons for the advice, and they should be understood, but "eat meat" is rarely the only solution.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My dietitian said I need it. No doctor recommends veganism."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** My doctor or dietitian knows my health and has examined me.
2. **P2.** They recommended that I eat meat.
3. **P3.** It's prudent to follow the advice of health professionals.
4. **C.** So I should eat meat.

P1, P2 and P3 may be true. The problem is that the recommendation may have reasons that allow alternatives, or may reflect a personal opinion contrary to the positions of the professional bodies in the field.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** depends on the case: it fails when the advice runs against the evidence without a specific clinical reason.

## 3. Answer for children

Doctors and dietitians know a lot and help us stay well. But sometimes they each think differently. That's why it's good to ask: "Why do I need this? Is there another way?" Science also keeps learning new things, so even people who know a lot need to keep studying. Many dietitians know how to plan meals with only plants that keep us strong and healthy.

## 4. Short answer

It's worth understanding why your doctor said that. If there's a clinical reason, such as an iron or B12 deficiency, it almost always has a solution without meat, with supplements and adjustments. If the recommendation is general, it runs against the main nutrition bodies, which consider well-planned vegan diets adequate. Many doctors receive little training in nutrition, and not every dietitian is up to date on plant-based diets: science moves on, and everyone needs to keep up. A second opinion, from a dietitian who knows plant-based diets, can help. "Eat meat" is rarely the only solution, and if your health can be looked after without it, an animal doesn't have to pay with its life for a generic recommendation.

**Question to ask back:** "Did your doctor explain which nutrient in meat you need, and whether it can come from another source?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The recommendation may have different bases:

| Basis of the recommendation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| A specific deficiency (B12, iron) | Correct it with supplements and adjustments, with no need for meat |
| A rare clinical condition that greatly limits possible foods | Follow the advice, with possible adaptations |
| The professional's general opinion about meat | Compare with institutional positions; seek a second opinion |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Following your doctor is reasonable, but it's worth asking why.** Trusting the person who follows your case isn't a fallacy. The error lies in turning individual advice, or a professional's general opinion, into a rule for everyone: in that case, it weighs less than the positions of the bodies that gather the evidence in the field.

**2. Nutrition bodies consider the diet adequate.** The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and the British Dietetic Association recognize that well-planned plant diets can support healthy living at all ages.

**3. Medical training in nutrition is limited.** In the United States, a 2010 survey found an average of about 20 hours of nutrition across the whole of medical school, and only a quarter of schools met the recommended minimum of 25 hours. A 2019 systematic review of 24 studies from several countries concluded that nutrition is insufficiently incorporated into medical education, regardless of country, setting or year of study. This isn't a criticism of doctors but of the curriculum: many of them weren't prepared to give detailed dietary advice. Dietitians are the professionals best placed to plan a diet.

**4. Not every dietitian is up to date on plant-based diets.** In a 2023 survey of 335 dietitians in the United Kingdom and Ireland, 79% said they hadn't received sufficient education on whole food plant-based diets, and 75% believed, incorrectly, that plant protein is incomplete and needs to be combined with others (G3). At the same time, 76% agreed that such a diet, well planned, is suitable for all life stages. It's a survey from one region, with voluntary responses, but it shows that knowledge of the subject varies a lot among professionals.

**5. Science is a process.** Recommendations change as new evidence emerges, and no professional can keep up with everything. Asking what evidence a piece of advice is based on isn't distrust, it's part of good care, and good professionals usually welcome the question.

**6. Almost every clinical reason has an alternative.** There may be specific clinical reasons for the advice, and they should be understood. But deficiencies of iron, B12 or protein can be corrected without meat, and "eat meat" is rarely the only solution.

**7. "No doctor recommends it" is false.** Many doctors and dietitians recommend plant-based diets, and medical and nutrition bodies recognize them.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the professional knows my case

The most serious version says the professional who follows the person knows details that general recommendations don't consider, and that it would be unwise to ignore them.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Knowing the case matters, which is why it's worth asking the reason.** If there's a specific reason, it may have another solution.
2. **A second opinion is common practice in medicine.** It isn't disrespect; it's care.
3. **If there really is a need, it's an exception.** Veganism already allows for what is "possible and practicable" (D7).

### (f) Back to the moral question

If the recommendation has a real clinical reason with no alternative, it's an exception, not a rule. For most people, the positions of nutrition bodies show that meat isn't necessary, and the moral question returns to the center.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in G15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to authority | Individual opinion replaces the evidence |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | "No doctor recommends it" from a single case |
| Epistemological | Ignoring institutional positions | Disregards the consensus of professional bodies |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Seeks second opinions on other matters.** Especially for important diagnoses.
2. **Doesn't follow all of their doctor's advice.** Many people ignore recommendations about exercise or alcohol.
3. **Trusts health bodies on other subjects.** But not on this one.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains G15 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority bias | A professional's word settles the question | General literature on heuristics |
| Confirmation bias | Advice that confirms the habit is accepted without questions | General literature on confirmation bias |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a professional recommended it, it's right".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doctors who recommended cigarettes | They'd be authority enough | This happened in the past |
| Professionals with differing opinions | All advice would be equally valid | Consensus is what makes the difference |
| Doctors who recommend a plant diet | They'd be authorities too | The argument cancels itself out |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Following the advice of health professionals is generally prudent, and there are clinical cases where dietary restrictions make sense. No one should ignore a doctor or dietitian who looks after their health, and the lack of nutrition training is a problem with curricula, not with professionals' dedication.

But it's possible to respect the professional and understand the reason for the advice. Almost always, what the body needs is a nutrient, not an animal, and there are professionals ready to help get it another way.
