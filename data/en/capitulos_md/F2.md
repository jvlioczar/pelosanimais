# F2. "If a dog and a child were drowning, which would you save?"

**Thesis:** most of us would save the child, and that authorizes nothing about the dog outside the drowning. Choosing whom to save in a tragic conflict doesn't give permission to exploit those who aren't in conflict. Someone who would save their own child rather than a stranger doesn't thereby have the right to use strangers. The question measures preferences in emergencies, not rights in everyday life.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Your mother or a cow? Would you save your dog or a stranger?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In a conflict where only one can be saved, almost everyone would save the human.
2. **P2.** This shows we value human life more.
3. **P3.** If human life is worth more, human interests can prevail over animals' interests.
4. **C.** So we can use animals for our interests, including eating them.

P1 and P2 may be true. The problem lies in P3: valuing one life more in a tragic conflict doesn't mean any human interest, such as taste, prevails over an animal's life outside any conflict.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which extends the conclusion of a tragic case to ordinary life.

## 3. Answer for children

If two friends fell into a pool and you could only help one, maybe you'd choose your best friend. Does that mean you can push the other friend into the pool on a normal day? Of course not! Choosing whom to help in an emergency doesn't give you the right to hurt anyone afterwards.

## 4. Short answer

I'd probably save the child, just as many people would save their own child rather than a stranger. But no one concludes from that that they can use strangers. Choosing whom to save when you can't save everyone doesn't give permission to hurt those who aren't in danger. Eating meat isn't a tragic dilemma: it's a choice that can be made without sacrificing anyone.

**Question to ask back:** "If you'd save your child rather than a stranger, does that give you the right to exploit strangers?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument moves from a dilemma to a general permission:

| Situation | What's at stake | Legitimate conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drowning, only one can be saved | Life against life | Perhaps priority to the human |
| Eating meat day to day | The animal's life against taste and habit | No priority justifies it |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Dilemmas don't authorize exploitation.** In a fire, a mother would save her own child before a stranger. That doesn't give her the right to exploit strangers in normal situations.

**2. Everyday life isn't a dilemma.** Eating meat isn't choosing between a human life and an animal's. It's choosing between the animal's life and a different dish.

**3. The preference also holds between humans.** Many people would save their own dog rather than a stranger. If preference in a dilemma gave permission, those people could use strangers. No one accepts that.

**4. The question presupposes that the animal matters.** It only makes sense to ask whom to save if both have value. The dilemma confirms that saving the dog would also be good.

**5. The case is used to change the subject.** The debate is about causing harm to those who needn't be harmed, not about whom to save when not everyone can be saved.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: if human life is worth more, so are human interests

The most serious version says that if human life is worth more than animal life, then important human interests, such as food and health, should also count more than animals' interests.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The human interest at stake is small.** For those with alternatives, eating meat protects neither life nor health, only taste and habit (J5).
2. **Being worth more isn't being worth everything.** Even if human life is worth more, that doesn't make the animal's life worthless.
3. **Among humans, the same logic is rejected.** Valuing my family more doesn't authorize me to sacrifice strangers for convenience.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Fallacy of accident (converse) | An exceptional dilemma becomes the rule |
| Logical | False analogy | Compares life against life with life against taste |
| Moral | Priority treated as permission | From "would save first" to "may use" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't use the same logic between humans.** Preferring one's family doesn't authorize exploiting strangers.
2. **Would save the dog if both could be saved.** Which shows the dog's life matters.
3. **Isn't choosing between lives when eating.** The plate isn't a drowning.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I'd save A before B, I may use B for A's benefit".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Child and stranger | Parents could exploit strangers for their children's benefit | No one accepts this |
| One's own dog and a stranger | Whoever would save their own dog could use strangers | No one accepts this |
| Young and old | If we save the young first, we could use the old | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The dilemma touches something real: in tragic situations, most people prioritize humans, and that intuition deserves to be taken seriously. Animal advocates don't need to pretend they'd save the dog instead of the child.

But a tragic dilemma is, by definition, an exception. It tells us what to do when not everyone can be saved, not what to do when there's no one to save, only a dish to choose.
