# H3. "Regenerative grazing sequesters carbon"

**Thesis:** well-managed grazing can improve soil and store some carbon, but the effect is limited, temporary, reversible and not scalable. A University of Oxford report concluded in 2017 that this sequestration is far smaller than the emissions of the animals themselves. And pasture-raised meat requires even more land per calorie. There's no such thing as "carbon-neutral meat" at scale.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Well-managed cattle restore the soil. Holistic grazing reverses desertification. Carbon-neutral beef."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Degraded soils can recover organic matter with rotational grazing practices.
2. **P2.** Soil organic matter stores carbon.
3. **P3.** If the carbon stored offsets the cattle's emissions, meat can be carbon neutral or even negative.
4. **C.** So regenerative grazing is a climate solution.

P1 and P2 are true in some contexts. The problem lies in P3: the evidence shows that sequestration is far smaller than the animals' emissions and that it saturates over time.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Soil can store a bit of carbon, which is good for the climate. Some people say cattle help the soil store more. But scientists found that cattle release more of the gases that warm the planet than the soil can store. So even with care, cattle still warm the planet.

## 4. Short answer

Well-managed grazing can help the soil and store some carbon, but not much. The University of Oxford's *Grazed and Confused* report concluded in 2017 that this gain is small, temporary and reversible, and far smaller than the animals' own emissions. And pasture-raised meat requires even more land per calorie. If that land were rewilded, with forests and native vegetation, it would store much more carbon.

**Question to ask back:** "If the soil stops storing carbon after a few years but the cattle keep emitting methane, does the balance stay neutral?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument compares sequestration with zero, not with the emissions or the alternatives:

| Factor | Regenerative grazing | Rewilded land |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon stored in soil | Some, temporary, until it saturates | More, in soil and vegetation |
| Methane and nitrous oxide emissions | Continue as long as there are cattle | None from cattle |
| Land needed per calorie | Very high | Freed up |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Sequestration is limited.** The *Grazed and Confused* report concluded that carbon sequestration by grazing occurs only under specific conditions, and even then is small and far smaller than the animals' emissions.

**2. Sequestration is temporary.** The soil reaches a new equilibrium after a few decades and stops accumulating carbon. The cattle's emissions continue.

**3. Soil carbon is reversible.** A drought, a fire or a change of management can return the carbon to the atmosphere.

**4. Pasture-raised meat uses far more land.** Per kilocalorie, beef requires about 50 to 100 times more land than plant foods. At scale, there isn't enough land.

**5. The alternative is rewilding.** If land used for pasture returned to native vegetation, it would store far more carbon than any grazing management.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Garnett et al., *Grazed and Confused* (2017)](https://www.tabledebates.org/publication/grazed-and-confused) | Carbon sequestration by grazing is small, temporary, reversible and far smaller than the animals' emissions | Food Climate Research Network, University of Oxford |
| [Our World in Data, land use and diets](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets) | Beef requires about 50 to 100 times more land per kilocalorie than plant foods | Based on Poore and Nemecek, 2018 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: it's better than conventional farming

The most serious version grants that regenerative grazing isn't carbon neutral, but says it's better than conventional intensive farming and should be encouraged.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Better than the worst isn't good.** Comparing with the worst option hides the best.
2. **It requires more land.** If all meat were pasture-raised, far more land would be needed, which would mean more deforestation.
3. **The relevant comparison is with plant diets.** No form of livestock farming comes close to them in emissions and land use.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if regenerative grazing were carbon neutral, the cattle would still be killed young. The climate debate is important, but it doesn't answer the moral question: is it fair to kill animals when we don't need to?

| Level | Error | How it appears in H3 |

| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Uses specific cases of sequestration and ignores the whole |
| Epistemological | Ignoring saturation | Treats a temporary gain as permanent |
| Logical | Selective comparison | Compares with conventional farming, not with plant diets |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Buys supermarket meat.** Which doesn't come from regenerative grazing.
2. **Ignores land use.** Which would be even greater with pasture-raised meat.
3. **Doesn't support rewilding.** Which would store more carbon.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a practice offsets part of its own emissions, it's sustainable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A car that plants one tree a year | It would be carbon neutral | Emissions remain greater |
| A coal plant with a small reforestation project | It would be sustainable | No sensible person concludes this |
| Regenerative grazing | It would be neutral | The data show the opposite |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Grazing management matters: better practices can restore degraded soils and reduce some harms. In regions that already have cattle, improving management is real progress, and some producers are sincerely committed to it.

But the promise of carbon-neutral meat at scale doesn't hold up. The most efficient way to store carbon and free up land is to eat fewer animals and let nature recover.
