# H3. "Being vegan isn't enough: if you really cared, you'd only eat from gardens or pesticide-free farms"

**Thesis:** the harm is real. Industrial crop farming kills animals through harvesters, pesticides, rodenticides, fertilizer runoff and land clearing, and some of these deaths are intentional. But real harm isn't, for that reason, unnecessary harm. The philosopher Joshua Jarvis-Campbell, in a 2026 paper devoted to this argument, argues that this harm is plausibly necessary in the short term, because cheap food for many people currently depends on agriculture at scale. The additional harm of animal farming fails that test: cheap food can be had without it. Whether better-off people have a duty to buy from less harmful sources remains an open question. Cheap measures to reduce deaths in crop farming, where feasible, may be a duty. The argument shows up on both sides: as a critique some vegans make of other vegans, and as an attack on vegans, a purity test that serves to dismiss change. And the alternative it proposes doesn't always spare animals: organic and agroecological farming can also cause harm, through manure from farmed animals, fertilizers made from slaughterhouse waste, integrated animal farming, lethal pest control by other means and more soil tillage. What should decide is the effect on sentient victims, not the label.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans shop at the supermarket, and supermarket crops kill animals with machines and poison. If you really cared, you'd only eat what you grow in your garden or what comes from pesticide-free farms. Being vegan isn't enough."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Industrial plant agriculture causes extensive harm to animals: harvesters, pesticides, rodenticides, fertilizer runoff and land clearing.
2. **P2.** This harm is unnecessary, because we could eat plants from less harmful sources, such as gardens, smaller farms using little pesticide, and vertical farms.
3. **P3.** It's wrong to cause, or support practices that cause, extensive, unnecessary harm to animals.
4. **P4.** Buying industrially produced food supports that practice.
5. **C.** So buying that food is wrong, and being vegan isn't enough.

P3 is the same principle used against animal farming, and the guide accepts it. P1 is plausible, and attempts to deny it run into problems. The weak point is P2: calling unnecessary a harm on which many people's access to cheap food currently depends.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, at least for the practice as a whole. The individual version, aimed at better-off people, remains an open question. P2 has a further problem: it assumes that gardens, organic farms or agroecological farms cause less harm to animals, which depends on how each one produces.

**Two uses of the same argument.** It looks like a critique that would come from within veganism, and sometimes it does: some vegans hold that the only consistent choice is to eat from your own garden or a pesticide-free farm. But it's also used against vegans, by meat eaters, as a purity test: if not even vegans are pure, there'd be no need to change anything. In the first use, it's a call to go further. In the second, it's a reason not to start.

## 3. Answer for children

On big farms, machines and poisons hurt little animals. That's true, and it's good to try to reduce it. But almost everyone needs the cheap food from the shop, and not everyone can have a vegetable garden. And not every garden or "pesticide-free" farm is good for animals: some use fertilizer made from animal remains, or raise animals too. What we can already do is stop raising and killing animals when we don't need to, and help farms hurt fewer and fewer animals.

## 4. Short answer

Industrial crop farming does kill animals, including on purpose, with poison. But notice that not all harm is unnecessary. Today, cheap food for many people depends on agriculture at scale, and a philosopher who studied exactly this argument concluded that this harm is plausibly necessary in the short term. The extra harm of animal farming isn't: cheap food can be had without it, and animal farming depends on the same crops. Reducing deaths in crop farming is a duty where it's cheap. But demanding purity from people who have already stopped eating animals may push away the very people who were changing. And "organic" or "agroecological" doesn't guarantee less harm: these farms may also use manure and slaughterhouse waste as fertilizer, raise animals and kill pests by other means. What counts is the effect on the animals, not the label, and the most clearly avoidable harm is still raising and killing animals for food.

**Question to ask back:** "If crop farming already kills animals, does it make sense to add animal farming on top, which depends on the same crops and also kills the farmed animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industrial crop farming kills many animals | The harm is real and may be large; some of it is intentional | The numbers are very uncertain |
| This harm is unnecessary | Some sources, such as certain gardens and vertical farms, cause less harm | Today they can't feed everyone at an affordable price; and an organic or agroecological label doesn't guarantee less harm to animals |
| So being vegan isn't enough | People who can reduce harm do well to reduce it | The additional harm of animal farming is what is clearly unnecessary |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The harm is real.** According to Jarvis-Campbell, industrial plant agriculture kills mammals, birds and fish: harvesters and trucks run over small animals, fertilizer runoff kills fish, and pesticides kill animals directly or indirectly. An estimate by Fischer and Lamey (2018), cited in the paper, reached 7.3 billion animals killed per year in the United States. The authors themselves consider it "clearly too high", because it generalizes from little data and contains calculation errors. But the lower bound they propose is still about 63.75 million deaths per year. And pesticides and rodenticides don't kill by accident: killing is what they're for.

**2. The easy defences don't hold.** "The animals would have died soon anyway" rests on a few studies, almost all about harvesting, and doesn't show that most of the animals killed had little life left. "They wouldn't exist without the crops" would only work if those lives were good, which hasn't been shown, and if existing were itself a benefit, which is doubtful. The paper concludes that denying extensive harm requires more evidence than exists.

**3. Not all harm is unnecessary.** Kicking a cat for fun is unnecessary harm. Locking up a violent person, to protect others, causes harm but is seen as necessary. The paper uses a test: harm is morally necessary when it serves an end worth the cost and that end can't be achieved without some activity that causes the harm.

**4. Agriculture at scale serves an end that matters.** Jarvis-Campbell argues that enough food might come from medium-sized farms using less pesticide, if the cropland now used for feed went to feeding people. But cheap food for many people currently depends on industrial agriculture. In the United States in 2022, 11.5% of the population (37.9 million people) lived in relative poverty, according to the census figures cited in the paper. For them, more expensive food means more financial strain and a worse quality of life. Advances in vertical farming or large subsidies could change this, but not in the technological and political near term. The author calls this conclusion plausible and doesn't run a cost-benefit calculation. He also sets aside harms to people and the environment, and grants that, if included, they could change the outcome.

**5. The proposed alternative can also cause harm.** The argument assumes that a garden, an organic farm or an agroecological farm means less harm to animals. Not always. Without synthetic fertilizer, these farms often rely on manure from farmed animals and on animal-based fertilizers allowed in organic production, such as blood meal, made from dried slaughterhouse waste, and feather meal, which comes from poultry slaughter (Colorado State University Extension). The FAO, among the elements of agroecology, values integrating crops with animal farming, with manure fertilizing the crops; those animals are generally slaughtered too. Without herbicides, organic production relies more on tilling the soil to control weeds (Schonbeck, eOrganic), and conventional tillage substantially reduces earthworm abundance, according to a global meta-analysis (Briones and Schmidt, 2017). Pest control remains lethal by other means: US organic rules allow, for example, a vitamin D3 rodenticide and slug baits. And organic yields are on average about 19% lower (Ponisio and colleagues, 2015), which may require more land for the same food. There's evidence on the other side too: on average, organic farming hosts about 30% more species than conventional farming (Tuck and colleagues, 2014), although more species doesn't mean fewer animals injured or killed. The net balance for animals hasn't been measured and varies from farm to farm. There is also vegan ("veganic") farming, which does without animal inputs. So the right test isn't the label, but the effect on sentient victims.

**6. The additional harm of animal farming fails the test.** If cheap food is the end, it can be had without animal farming. Cheap animal farming depends on the same crops, because the animals eat cheap feed, and it adds breeding and slaughter on top. In the United States, 67% of the calories produced by agriculture go to animal feed (Cassidy et al., 2013, cited in the paper). So the same reasoning that may make crop farming necessary leaves the extra harm of raising animals without justification (H2, H4).

**7. The duty of better-off people remains open.** A rich person doesn't need cheap food. Perhaps they have a duty to buy from less harmful sources. The paper gives reasons to doubt this: there's no evidence that a single purchase carries a high risk of causing extensive harm, and mass consumption helps keep prices low for people who need it. But the author says these reasons aren't conclusive, and that further research could change the answer.

**8. Reducing harm is still a duty where it's cheap.** The paper argues that nets and barriers to keep animals out of fields may be obligatory where the cost is small. In the same spirit, it makes sense to support less use of rodenticides and pesticides where this doesn't make food more expensive, less cropland for feed, and alternatives such as vertical farms as they become viable.

**9. Demanding purity may push people away.** In a footnote, Jarvis-Campbell notes that saying veganism "isn't good enough" may lead people who eat animals to give up on any change, finding it too demanding. Presenting veganism as an acceptable choice may lead more people to leave animal farming behind, which is the change that most reduces harm. This holds for both uses of the argument: when one vegan demands purity of another, and when a meat eater uses the demand to avoid changing.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: people who can afford it don't need cheap food

The most serious version accepts that industrial crop farming, as a whole, may be necessary, but says this applies to people who depend on cheap food. For people with money, buying from industrial farms serves no important end, and the harm that purchase causes would be unnecessary.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The question is open, not settled.** The very paper that discusses it doesn't reach an answer: it isn't known whether a single purchase carries a high risk of causing extensive harm.
2. **Better-off people's purchases also help keep prices low.** Mass consumption sustains the scale that makes food cheap for people who need it.
3. **Even if the duty exists, it doesn't favor meat.** It would call for buying plants from even less harmful sources, not for going back to eating animals, whose production depends on the same crops and much more.
4. **It makes sense to support people who can go further, without making it a condition.** Gardens, low-pesticide farms and vertical farms can be good choices for people who have access to them, provided they actually cause less harm to animals, which the label alone doesn't guarantee. Demanding them of everyone, as if being vegan were worthless without them, may push away people who are changing.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument starts from a principle the guide accepts: it's wrong to cause extensive, unnecessary harm to animals. Taken seriously, that principle first condemns the harm that is clearly unnecessary, which is raising and killing animals for food. Then it calls for reducing what remains, wherever this is possible without taking cheap food away from people who need it. And it calls for judging each choice by its effects on sentient victims, not by its label: an organic farm that fertilizes with slaughterhouse waste and raises animals for slaughter isn't, just by being organic, better for animals. The question isn't whether anyone is pure, but why keep the harm that can be avoided today.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in H3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | Either total purity, or veganism isn't enough |
| Moral | Confusing real harm with unnecessary harm | Treats all harm from crop farming as avoidable |
| Epistemological | Assuming the alternative is available | Gardens and vertical farms can't feed everyone affordably today |
| Moral | Leaving out the additional harm | Ignores that animal farming adds slaughter to the same crops |
| Epistemological | Taking the label as a guarantee | Assumes that "organic" or "agroecological" means less harm to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **When said by meat eaters, it demands of vegans what they don't demand of themselves.** Cheap meat depends on the same crops, and on more of them.
2. **It uses the principle of unnecessary harm and ignores its first consequence.** The harm that principle most clearly condemns is that of animal farming.
3. **It demands purity and offers no feasible step.** For most people, their own garden or pesticide-free food isn't within reach.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a less harmful source exists somewhere, all harm from the usual source is unnecessary".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clothing | Only handmade clothing, from fiber you produce, would be acceptable | No one concludes this |
| Housing | Only a house built without killing any animal would be acceptable | No one concludes this |
| Animal farming | It would be condemned before crop farming | Its harm is larger and avoidable today |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that industrial crop farming kills animals, including on purpose, and that the easy defences ("they'd die soon anyway", "they wouldn't exist otherwise") don't hold. It's right that reducing this harm is a duty where it's cheap, with nets, barriers, less poison and less cropland for feed. And it's right that people who can buy from less harmful sources do well to consider it, provided "less harmful" is measured by the effect on animals, not by the label.

But the step to "being vegan isn't enough" doesn't follow. Today, this harm is plausibly necessary for cheap food to reach many people, and the additional harm of animal farming isn't. Demanding purity may push people away from the change that most reduces harm.
