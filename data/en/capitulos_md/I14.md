# I14. "I only use cruelty-free products"

**Thesis:** "cruelty-free" usually means only that the product and its ingredients weren't tested on animals. A product with that label can contain ingredients that come from the exploitation and killing of animals, such as carmine, lanolin, beeswax, honey, collagen, keratin and shark-liver squalene. So, in practice, cruelty-free isn't free of cruelty. Since 2025, Brazilian law bans testing cosmetics on vertebrate animals, as the European Union has done since 2009 (and since 2013 it has also banned selling cosmetics tested on animals), so "not tested" is becoming the minimum. Anyone who wants to avoid exploitation needs to look for products that are both vegan and not tested.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I already do my part: I only buy cruelty-free makeup and shampoo, with the bunny on the package."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Testing cosmetics on animals causes suffering.
2. **P2.** Products with a cruelty-free label aren't tested on animals.
3. **P3.** By buying only those products, I stop funding that suffering.
4. **C.** So my cosmetics don't harm animals.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 is true only for testing. The conclusion doesn't follow, because the label says nothing about the ingredients.

The argument is **valid** as far as testing goes. Its **soundness** fails in the step from "not tested" to "doesn't harm animals".

## 3. Answer for children

The bunny on the package means nobody tested that product on animals, and that's good. But the lipstick or cream can still have things taken from animals, like beeswax or a dye made from insects. To be sure, it's good to look for the vegan label too.

## 4. Short answer

It's a good start, but check what the label says. Cruelty-free usually means only that the product wasn't tested on animals. It can still contain carmine, made from killed insects, lanolin, from sheep's wool, beeswax, collagen and keratin, from slaughtered animals, or shark-liver squalene. Not testing is an important step, and in Brazil it became law in 2025. But if you really want to avoid funding exploitation, the product also needs to be vegan. Otherwise, the animal stops suffering in the lab but goes on dying to become an ingredient.

**Question to ask back:** "The label says the product wasn't tested on animals. Does it say it wasn't made from animals?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Label | What it guarantees | What it doesn't guarantee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cruelty-free or "not tested on animals" | No animal testing, under each certifier's rules | No animal-derived ingredients |
| Vegan | No animal-derived ingredients | Sometimes, no testing; it depends on the certifier |
| Vegan and cruelty-free | Both | — |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The label is about testing, not ingredients.** Certifications such as Cruelty Free International's Leaping Bunny deal with animal testing; they don't restrict animal-derived ingredients. A certified shampoo can contain keratin, lanolin or honey.

**2. Many ingredients come from the exploitation and killing of animals.**

| Ingredient | Origin | Where it appears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carmine (CI 75470) | Killed cochineals | Lipsticks, blushes, eyeshadows |
| Lanolin | Fat from sheep's wool | Moisturizers, lip balms |
| Beeswax and honey | Beekeeping | Balms, mascaras, creams |
| Collagen and keratin | Skin, bones, hooves and feathers of slaughtered animals | Creams, shampoos, hair treatments |
| Squalene | Sometimes, shark liver | Moisturizers and serums |
| Guanine | Fish scales | Nail polishes and shimmery eyeshadows |

Several of these ingredients also have plant or synthetic versions, and only the label or the brand can say which was used.

**3. Not testing has become the legal minimum in several places.** Brazil's Law 15,183 of 2025 bans testing cosmetics, hygiene products and perfumes on live vertebrate animals, and the European Union banned the sale of cosmetics tested on animals in 2013. A label that only repeats the law says little.

**4. The term isn't always regulated.** In many countries, "cruelty-free" has no legal definition, and each brand can use it in its own way. Labels from independent certifiers are more reliable than brands' own claims.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: finding untested products is already hard; demanding vegan is too much

The most serious version says choosing untested products already takes effort, and adding the requirement that they be vegan makes shopping impractical.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Today there are many vegan, untested brands, including popular ones.** Apps and certifiers' lists make the search easier.
2. **You don't need to switch everything at once.** As each product runs out, you can choose a vegan replacement.
3. **The effort doesn't change the fact.** Even if it takes work, a product with animal ingredients still funds exploitation.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's enough not to test on animals, when the product itself is made from animals who were exploited and killed. Anyone who already cares about testing has the same reasons to care about ingredients.

| Moral | Moral licensing | One good step excuses the others |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Rejects testing on a rabbit.** And accepts lanolin from sheep and collagen from slaughtered animals.
2. **Reads the label.** And doesn't read the ingredient list.
3. **Calls the product cruelty-free.** And it contains killed insects.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I14 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Halo effect | An ethical label makes the whole product seem ethical | General literature on labeling and consumption |
| Moral licensing | Having done something good reduces attention to the rest | General literature on moral behavior |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the product wasn't tested on animals, it doesn't harm animals".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fur coat not tested on animals | It wouldn't harm animals | Absurd: the product is the animal |
| A lipstick with carmine and a cruelty-free label | It wouldn't harm animals | Thousands of insects were killed |
| A vegan, untested product | Uses no animals in testing or in ingredients | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that animal testing causes suffering, that avoiding it is an important step, and that the cruelty-free movement helped pass laws like Brazil's in 2025.

But the label is about testing, not ingredients. For a product to be truly free of cruelty, it also needs to be vegan.
