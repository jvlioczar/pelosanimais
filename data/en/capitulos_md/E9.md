# E9. "Eating meat isn't an ideology; veganism is"

**Thesis:** every practice that involves choices about who may be used rests on beliefs, eating meat included. The difference is that the dominant belief is invisible to those who follow it. The psychologist Melanie Joy called this belief system carnism: it teaches which animals are food and which are companions, and makes this seem natural. Veganism doesn't politicize food; it only makes visible a choice that already existed.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Eating meat is normal, it's not a philosophy. You're the ones politicizing food. Carnism is a made-up word."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Eating meat is a traditional and almost universal practice that doesn't depend on any theory.
2. **P2.** Veganism depends on a specific moral theory about animals.
3. **P3.** Whoever proposes changing a practice on the basis of a theory carries the ideological burden.
4. **C.** So eating meat is neutral, and veganism is an ideology.

The strong version has some truth in it: whoever proposes changing a custom has to offer reasons. The problem lies in P1: eating meat does depend on beliefs, such as the belief that certain animals may be used and others may not.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, which confuses "dominant" with "neutral".

## 3. Answer for children

Who decided the dog is a friend and the pig is food? Nobody is born knowing that: we learn it from our family and our culture. In other countries, people learn it differently. So eating certain animals also comes from ideas someone taught us, even if they seem normal.

## 4. Short answer

Eating meat also depends on beliefs: that certain animals may be used and others may not, that the pig is food and the dog is family. These beliefs seem neutral because they're the majority's, and the dominant norm is invisible to those who follow it. The psychologist Melanie Joy called this system carnism. Veganism doesn't invent an ideology; it only makes visible a choice that was already being made.

**Question to ask back:** "Who taught you that the pig is food and the dog is family?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats the dominant norm as an absence of belief:

| Belief behind eating meat | Is it neutral? |
| --- | --- |
| Certain animals may be raised and killed for food | No: it's a moral position |
| Which animals count as food varies by culture | Shows the choice is learned |
| Eating meat is normal, natural and necessary | These are justifications, studied by psychology |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Dominant isn't neutral.** Every practice that decides who may be used involves moral judgments. Most people simply don't notice the judgments they share with almost everyone.

**2. The choice of which animals to eat is cultural.** In Europe and the Americas people eat pigs but not dogs; in some places it has been different, and in others cows are not eaten. This shows the division is taught, not natural.

**3. The concept of carnism describes this system.** Melanie Joy proposed the term in 2009 to name the belief system that conditions people to eat certain animals. One can disagree with the term, but the idea that eating animals is sustained by beliefs is supported by research on the 4Ns: natural, normal, necessary and nice.

**4. "Ideology" isn't an insult.** Every moral position is a set of beliefs about how to act. The relevant question isn't whether something is an ideology, but whether its reasons are good.

**5. The burden applies to both sides.** Whoever proposes change has to give reasons, and so does whoever causes harm to others. A practice being old doesn't exempt it from justification (D1).

| Source | What it says | Note |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: not every habit is an ideology

The most serious version says not every habit expresses an ideology: brushing your teeth, for example, is just a custom. Calling meat eating an ideology would inflate the word.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Brushing your teeth has no victim.** Habits that involve other beings require moral justification.
2. **The argument doesn't depend on the word.** One can swap "ideology" for "set of beliefs", and the question remains.
3. **The justifications exist and are studied.** The fact that people reach for them shows there are beliefs sustaining the habit.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Persuasive definition | Calls only the minority position an "ideology" |
| Logical | Shifting the burden | Demands justification only from whoever proposes change |
| Epistemological | Invisible norm | Doesn't notice the beliefs shared with the majority |
| Moral | Apparent neutrality | Treats as neutral a practice that has victims |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wouldn't eat dogs.** Which shows they follow a rule about which animals to eat.
2. **Defends eating meat with arguments.** Natural, normal, necessary: these are beliefs.
3. **Would call practices of other cultures ideological.** Such as eating dogs in other countries.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "the majority's position isn't an ideology; only the minority's is".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abolitionism in the 19th century | It would be an ideology; slavery would be neutral | No one accepts this |
| Women's suffrage | It would be an ideology; exclusion would be neutral | No one accepts this |
| Any majority belief | It would be immune to questioning | The principle protects any majority |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that whoever proposes changing a custom has to give good reasons, and that accusing everyone of "ideology" can impoverish the debate. It's also true that veganism is a moral position, with assumptions that can be discussed.

But the same holds for eating meat. The difference isn't that one side has beliefs and the other doesn't; it's that the majority's beliefs are invisible. Making those beliefs visible is the first step to examining them.
