# E9. "Everything in moderation"

**Thesis:** moderation is a good rule for things that affect only the person choosing, like sweets or hours of sleep. It doesn't work for harm to others: no one defends stealing "in moderation". The middle ground between causing a harm and not causing it is still causing a harm. The middle position isn't automatically the most reasonable.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Nothing to extremes. Balance is the secret. Extremes are bad."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Extreme positions tend to be unrealistic and to ignore complexity.
2. **P2.** Veganism is an extreme position; eating meat without limits is too.
3. **P3.** Virtue usually lies in the middle.
4. **C.** So eating meat in moderation is the most reasonable position.

The strong version rests on an old and respectable idea: Aristotle saw virtue as a mean between excesses. The problem lies in P3 applied to this case: Aristotle himself said some actions have no mean.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which calls simply not causing a harm "extreme", and at P3, which doesn't hold for harm to others.

## 3. Answer for children

Eating candy "in moderation" makes sense: a little won't hurt you. But hitting classmates "in moderation"? That makes no sense, because hurting others doesn't become right just because it's a little. When someone gets hurt, the best thing isn't to hurt them less: it's not to hurt them.

## 4. Short answer

Moderation makes sense for things that affect only you, like sweets or coffee. For harm to others, it doesn't: no one defends stealing or assaulting "in moderation". The middle ground between causing a harm and not causing it is still causing a harm. And not hurting someone isn't an extreme; it's the starting point. Eating less meat is better than eating more, but that doesn't make what's left justified.

**Question to ask back:** "Is there anything you think is wrong to do, even in moderation?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument applies moderation to a kind of choice where it doesn't work:

| Kind of choice | Does moderation make sense? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affects only the person choosing | Yes | Sweets, alcohol, screen time |
| Causes harm to others | No: the goal is to avoid the harm | Theft, assault, cruelty |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the false middle ground.** Assuming the position between two options is the right one is a fallacy. The middle between truth and a lie isn't an acceptable half-truth.

**2. Not causing a harm isn't extreme.** The argument presents "not eating animals" and "eating animals without limits" as two symmetrical extremes. But not doing something that harms another is the starting point of ethics, not an excess.

**3. Moderation is about the eater, not the eaten.** For the animal who dies, it makes no difference whether the person eating it is moderate.

**4. Aristotle didn't apply the mean to everything.** In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle says some actions, like theft and murder, admit no mean: they are bad in themselves.

**5. Reducing is better, but it isn't justifying.** Eating less meat reduces the harm, and that's good (J7). But what remains still needs a justification.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, book II](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8438) | Virtue is a mean, but some actions admit no mean | Full text on Project Gutenberg; the example of actions with no mean, like adultery, is in book II |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: moderation is more realistic

The most serious version doesn't say moderation is the moral ideal, but that it's what most people can manage, and that asking for total abstention drives people away, while asking for moderation produces more harm reduction.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's about strategy, not justification.** It can be useful to promote reduction as a step; that doesn't make the remaining consumption right.
2. **It admits the ideal is something else.** If moderation is merely the realistic option, the ideal is still not causing the harm.
3. **For those who can do more, moderation isn't an excuse.** Realism applies to those with real difficulties (E10).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False middle ground | The middle between two positions is treated as the truth |
| Logical | False symmetry | Not causing a harm is treated as an extreme |
| Moral | Moderation applied to harm | Uses a rule of self-care for harm to others |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply moderation to other harms.** No one defends moderate violence.
2. **Wouldn't find it moderate to eat dogs in moderation.** Which shows the real criterion is species.
3. **Admires "extreme" positions of the past.** Abolishing slavery was "extreme" too.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "the middle ground between doing and not doing is the most reasonable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assault | Assaulting in moderation would be reasonable | No one accepts this |
| Racism | Being racist in moderation would be balanced | No one accepts this |
| Cruelty to dogs | Mistreating a little would be the middle ground | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Moderation is a real virtue in many areas, and rigid positions sometimes drive people away. It's also true that eating less meat is better than eating more, and that gradual change is, for many people, the most realistic path.

But moderation is a rule for looking after oneself, not for deciding how much harm to cause others. For whoever suffers the harm, there's no middle ground.
