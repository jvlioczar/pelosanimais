# N12. "It's impossible to solve suffering in nature; it's too big and complex"

**Thesis:** not being able to solve everything is no reason to do nothing. There are already ways of helping wild animals that work, such as vaccinating foxes against rabies, which helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe, and rescuing animals in disasters. And the most important part today is cheap: researching, taking the problem seriously, and not treating it as absurd.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "There are trillions of animals. We'll never end predation, hunger and disease. It's pointless."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We only have a duty to do what is possible.
2. **P2.** Eliminating suffering in nature is impossible, because of the scale and complexity of ecosystems.
3. **C.** So we have no duty regarding the suffering of wild animals.

P1 is true ("ought implies can"). P2 is also true, if we mean eliminating all suffering. The mistake is moving from "we can't solve everything" to "we have no duty at all".

The argument is **invalid**: the conclusion would only follow if the only possible action were solving everything at once.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "There are trillions of animals, it's pointless." |
| Everyday conversation | "We'll never end predation." |
| Activism | "It's an impossible cause; let's focus on what we can do." |
| Social media | "Are you going to give medicine to every ant?" |
| Academic version | "The problem is intractable at the relevant scale." |
| Disguised version | "One person doesn't make a difference." (which is K1) |

## 3. Answer for children

No one can clean the whole world alone. But if everyone picks up the litter where they are, things get better. Doctors can't cure every disease either, but they keep studying and cure many. It's similar with animals in nature: we can't help all of them at once, but we can already help some, and scientists can find ways to help many more.

## 4. Short answer

It's also impossible to end all the diseases that affect humans, and you wouldn't think it right to stop researching prevention, treatments and cures, or to stop treating those we can. There are already ways to help wild animals: vaccinating foxes against rabies helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe. And the first step is cheap: researching and taking the problem seriously.

**Question to ask back:** "Since we can't end all the poverty in the world, do you think we shouldn't help anyone?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses "solve" in an all-or-nothing sense:

| What is asked | Possible today? |
| --- | --- |
| Eliminating all suffering in nature | No |
| Helping some animals in specific situations | Yes, it's already done |
| Researching the wellbeing of wild animals | Yes, it has begun |
| Considering wild animals in decisions we already make | Yes |
| Spreading the idea that this suffering matters | Yes |

The duty under discussion is the one in the lower rows, not the first.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. We already help, and it works.** Oral rabies vaccination of foxes, begun in Switzerland in the late 1970s, helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe, protecting other animals and people as well. There are also rescues in floods and fires, treatment of injured animals, and vaccination of endangered animals.

**2. Today's impossible can be tomorrow's possible.** Problems that seemed too big, such as smallpox, eradicated in 1980, became tractable through research and coordinated effort. Starting with study is the way, and that's why a whole field, welfare biology, is proposed to study how wild animals live and what would help them.

**3. The human test.** We won't end all poverty, hunger and disease among humans. No one concludes from this that helping people is useless. The scale of a problem is a reason to prioritize well, not to give up.

**4. Medicine will never end all diseases either.** It's impossible to solve all the diseases that affect human beings: new ones emerge, others change, and many still have no cure. That doesn't make us stop researching ways to prevent, treat and cure them. No one says that, since we can't cure everything, we'd better close the laboratories. With the suffering of wild animals, the reasoning is the same: not being able to solve everything is a reason to research, not to give up.

**5. Scale increases importance.** If trillions of animals suffer, even small improvements at scale can prevent a great deal of suffering. The size of the problem is an argument for taking it seriously.

**6. The first task is changing attitudes.** Today, the biggest barrier isn't technical: it's the idea that this suffering doesn't matter. Changing that costs little and is a precondition for everything else.

**7. "Ought implies can" has been misapplied.** It's true that no one has a duty to do the impossible. But the correct conclusion would only be "there is no duty to eliminate all suffering in nature", and no one proposes that (Sapontzis, 1987). The duty is fulfilled case by case: giving water to a thirsty animal, treating a sick one, vaccinating a population. Not being able to save everyone doesn't excuse us from saving someone (Cunha, 2022, pp. 216–218).

**8. Even when we can't act yet, we can research and spread the word.** Faced with a serious disease that has no cure yet, no one concludes there's nothing to do: research is funded in proportion to the severity. The same goes for wild animals, and there's also a duty to spread the arguments, so that future generations have the knowledge and willingness that are lacking today (Cunha, 2022, p. 224; Horta, 2010).

**9. The efficiency objections confuse proportion with quantity.** It's said that helping wild animals reaches few cases, only pays off in the long run, or is "mopping the floor with the tap running", because the problem comes back. Cunha (2022, pp. 229–236) answers each one. Solving 0.1% of a gigantic problem can prevent more suffering than solving 10% of a smaller one; what counts is how much suffering each resource prevents, not the fraction solved. One intervention can be worth many: sterilizing a single female ocean sunfish, which can lay up to 300 million eggs, prevents more doomed births than sterilizing hundreds of thousands of female dogs. Sterilizing half a population prevents half the deaths of the young, even if the problem doesn't end. And large-scale techniques already exist: the sterile insect technique, which releases sterile males to reduce a population, has been used for decades against pests such as the screwworm fly (Dyck, Hendrichs and Robinson, 2005).

**10. There's already a long list of ways animals are helped.** Besides the rabies vaccine, there are examples for almost every kind of harm (Cunha, 2022, pp. 280–283; Animal Ethics):
- **Vaccines.** Against sylvatic plague in prairie dogs, with baits spread even by drones on refuges in Montana, in the United States, from 2017; against anthrax in cheetahs and black rhinos (Turnbull et al., 2004); against tuberculosis in wild boar, with an oral vaccine in baits (Garrido et al., 2011); against brucellosis in bison, with the RB51 vaccine. In 2023, the US Department of Agriculture gave a conditional license to the first vaccine for honeybees, against American foulbrood.
- **Treatments.** Wombats with sarcoptic mange are treated with an antiparasitic applied through flaps at burrow entrances, without capture, although the method requires repeated doses and covering every burrow; in a recent study, this eliminated visible mange from a population for about 20 months (Leary et al., 2025). In a field trial with bats exposed to white-nose syndrome, a probiotic raised overwinter survival from about 8% to 46%, in a small group of animals (Hoyt et al., 2019).
- **Disasters.** In Kaziranga National Park, in India, artificial raised platforms serve as refuges during floods; animal deaths in the park fell from about 400 in 2017 to about 200 in 2019, according to the Indian press, and the authorities credit the platforms with part of the drop. Critics argue that freeing the corridors to the natural high ground would be a better solution.
- **Others.** Orphanages for young animals, artificial shelters and nests, rescuing animals trapped in ice or mud, and feeding in times of scarcity combined with fertility control, so as not to create more hunger later.

**11. What we still don't know.** Cunha (2022, p. 236) admits that there are no studies comparing the cost-effectiveness of helping wild animals with that of fighting exploitation. That's a good reason to do research, not to give up: welfare biology exists precisely to produce that data.

**12. It isn't always futile, and even when it seems so it can be a duty.** Jeff Sebo (2022) answers the futility objection: including animals in decisions isn't always futile, and even when a step seems not to change the outcome, it can be a duty, because it respects rights, cultivates virtues and builds relationships of care. He also takes the objection seriously: a strategy that would be futile now, or that could do more harm than good, such as moving animals without care for welfare and the environment, should be dropped. The point is to choose well, not to give up.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: opportunity cost

The most serious version says: resources are limited. Every dollar spent on wild animals doesn't go to exploited animals, where we know what to do. It would be better to focus on the tractable problem.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It concedes the problem matters.** Discussing priorities already admits there's a duty, and the question becomes how to distribute efforts.
2. **Research is cheap and opens paths.** What's proposed today is mainly study and outreach, which costs little compared with what is spent on other causes.
3. **The causes reinforce each other.** Teaching that every sentient animal matters, whoever caused the harm, also strengthens the fight against exploitation (N14).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If everything can't be solved, nothing is worth doing |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either we eliminate all suffering, or we do nothing |
| Epistemological | Ignorance of what already exists | Ignores interventions that already work |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts partial help for humans and denies it to animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports partial help for humans.** Vaccines, donations and relief don't solve everything and are still valued.
2. **Approves of rescuing animals in disasters.** Which is partial help for wild animals.
3. **Doesn't say medicine is useless.** Though it will never end all disease.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains N12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | The unreachable ideal becomes an excuse not to do what's possible | [Kunda, 1990](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480) |
| Scope insensitivity | Huge numbers stop moving us and start paralyzing us | Linked to K1 |
| Omission bias | Not acting seems safer and less serious | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |
| Proportion bias | Helping millions seems pointless when they are a tiny fraction of trillions | [Bartels, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2005.10.004) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if we can't solve the whole problem, we have no duty to help".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human poverty | Giving or helping would be pointless | No one accepts this |
| Disease | Medicine would be pointless, because there will always be disease | Absurd |
| Animal exploitation | Veganism would be pointless, because farming won't end tomorrow | The same reasoning is rejected in K1 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that the problem is immense and complex, that we can't solve it today, and that large, poorly planned interventions would be irresponsible. It's also right that priorities have to be chosen.

But the answer to complexity is research and caution, not indifference. What we already do shows that helping is possible, and what we don't yet know how to do is exactly what's worth studying.
