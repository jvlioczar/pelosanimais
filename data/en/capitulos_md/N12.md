# N12. "Sentientists want to get rid of predators"

**Thesis:** nobody with a voice in this debate proposes going out and killing predators. Killing them is exactly what the position rejects, because prey populations would explode and hunger and disease would rise — more suffering, not less. What exists is a discussion about the distant future, about whether it will one day be possible to reduce predation without causing harm, through things like contraception, alternative feeding and gene editing. The main site devoted to the subject calls the idea "a proposal to investigate, not a technique ready to deploy". What is actually advocated today is something else: vaccinating, treating the injured, rescuing animals from fires and floods, managing fertility where that is already done.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** this chapter is about predation. The idea that intervening is "playing God" is N8, and the fear of making things worse is N6

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "These people want to get rid of lions. They want a world without predators, as if you could press a button and rewrite nature."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Anyone who takes sentience seriously has to consider the suffering caused by predation.
2. **P2.** Reducing that suffering would require eliminating or altering predators.
3. **P3.** Eliminating or altering predators would cause ecological collapse and even greater suffering.
4. **C.** So the position leads to an absurd conclusion and should be rejected.

P1 is right, and it is accepted. P3 is right about elimination, which is exactly why nobody defends it. The mistake is P2, which treats "reducing the suffering of predation" and "eliminating predators" as the same thing, and treats a long-term research programme as a plan of action.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2: the conclusion attributed to the position is precisely the one it rejects, for the reason P3 itself gives.

## 3. Answer for children

Nobody wants to kill the lion. The lion isn't evil: it's just the only way he knows how to eat, and he feels pain and fear too. The question people are asking is a different and very hard one: might there one day be a way for the lion to live well without having to hurt anyone? Nobody knows the answer yet. For now, what can be done is caring for injured animals, vaccinating them and helping in fires.

## 4. Short answer

Killing predators is what this position rejects, and for the reason you just gave: without them, prey populations would explode and far more animals would die of hunger and disease. What is being discussed is whether, one day, predation could be reduced without causing harm, through contraception, alternative feeding or gene editing. That is long-term research, not a plan. What is advocated for today is vaccinating, treating the injured, and rescuing animals from fires and floods.

**Question back:** "Are you against vaccinating wild animals for rabies and rescuing them from fires? Because that is what this is about today."

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

Three different things are treated as one. Separating them takes the objection apart.

| What it is | Who defends it | Where it stands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Help already practised: vaccination, treating the injured, rescue from fires and floods, fertility management | Practically the whole field, and environmental agencies already do it | Broad consensus, barely controversial |
| Research on predation: investigating whether there are safe ways to reduce it in the future | Part of the field, as a research programme | Open, and explicitly conditional |
| Eliminating predators now | Nobody | Rejected, for the ecological reason the objection gives |

The objection takes the third row, which has no defenders, and attributes it to people in the first two.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Elimination is rejected by the criterion itself.** If what matters is suffering, killing predators is counterproductive: prey would multiply to the limit of the food supply, and hunger and disease would kill more of them, and worse. Anyone who takes sentience seriously reaches the opposite conclusion to the one attributed to them.

**2. The predator is someone too.** He or she feels pain, hunger and fear, and goes hungry often. Not a villain to be punished, but another animal caught in a situation they did not choose. Any serious proposal has to include their welfare, which already rules out violent solutions.

**3. It is a research proposal, not a plan of action.** The main site devoted to the subject, Herbivorize Predators, presents itself as "a proposal to investigate, not a technique ready to deploy", speaks of change happening gradually across generations, and states that no general solution has been demonstrated. David Pearce, author of the best-known proposal, writes about technologies that would be decades away and treats the matter as a long-term question.

**4. Pearce doesn't advocate exterminating predators.** *Reprogramming Predators* discusses depot contraception, cultivated meat for carnivores and gene editing, not killing. He does say the case for selective extinction "isn't absurd", and that centuries from now lions may exist only in digital archives, but he adds that "prediction is not advocacy". Attributing an extermination plan to him swaps what was written for a caricature.

**5. We already intervene constantly, and nobody calls it madness.** Oral-bait rabies vaccination of foxes, rescuing animals from fires and oil spills, treating the injured, controlling invasive species, fertility management in elephants and wild horses: all of it is intervention in nature, already done and widely accepted. What changes, in the position being criticised, is only the reason: the good of the animals themselves rather than of the landscape.

**6. The scale of the problem is what motivates the question.** Suffering in nature is immense, above all because of the very high mortality of young in most species, who die shortly after birth of hunger, cold, disease and predation. Concluding that predation must be altered isn't obligatory. Not finding the question ridiculous is, for anyone who thinks animals matter.

**7. Caution is the field's own position, not an objection to it.** Those who argue for helping wild animals usually insist on prior research, tested interventions, reversibility and small scale before anything large, precisely because of the risk of making things worse (N6). The objection presents as criticism what is already the internal rule.

### (d) The best reply: the ecological risk

The serious version isn't the caricature. It is the argument that ecosystems are webs of dependencies we understand poorly, that changes at one level produce cascading effects, and that the history of human intervention in nature is full of well-meant disasters. Given that, touching predation would be far too risky, today and perhaps always.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It is an argument about method, not about value.** It grants that suffering matters and discusses how to act, which is exactly the discussion being proposed.
2. **It works against large interventions, not small ones.** Vaccinating, treating and rescuing don't bring down ecosystems, and are already done.
3. **It recommends research, not silence.** If the problem is not knowing enough, the answer is to study, which is what the proposal asks for.
4. **The risk of acting has to be compared with the risk of not acting.** Not intervening has a cost too, paid in full by the animals, and it isn't zero just because it is what already happens.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in N12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Straw man | Attributes to the position the conclusion it rejects |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either everything stays as it is, or predators are eliminated |
| Epistemic | Research confused with plan | Treats a long-term hypothesis as a programme under way |
| Moral | Appeal to nature | Assumes the present arrangement is right because it is what exists |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It accepts control of invasive species and population management.** Which is direct intervention, and often lethal.
2. **It vaccinates wild animals against rabies.** Mass intervention, accepted without controversy.
3. **It rescues animals from fires and oil spills.** Interfering with the "natural" course of things.
4. **It thinks killing predators is wrong.** Which is also the conclusion of the people being accused.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up N12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appeal to nature | What is natural seems good, and changing it seems wrong in itself | Literature on the naturalistic fallacy |
| Idyllic nature | Nature is imagined as balanced and benign, which hides the scale of the suffering | [Animal Ethics](https://www.animal-ethics.org/why-the-popular-view-of-animals-living-in-the-wild-is-wrong/) |
| Omission bias | Harm that happens without our action seems less serious than harm that would result from it | Literature on omission bias |
| Scope insensitivity | The number of animals who die in nature produces no proportional response | [Senciência, 2024](https://senciencia.org/2024/04/22/vieses-que-contribuem-para-a-negligencia-dos-problemas-com-mais-vitimas/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "proposing to investigate a future change is the same as advocating carrying it out now".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medical research | Studying gene editing in embryos would be the same as advocating its immediate use | Nobody treats research that way |
| Climate | Studying geoengineering would be the same as advocating dumping sulphur into the stratosphere | Contrary to scientific practice |
| Conservation itself | Researching species reintroduction would be the same as carrying it out unstudied | Contrary to the field's own method |
| Any hypothesis | Every research question would become a policy proposal | Makes discussing any hard subject impossible |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets several things right, and they are worth naming. Ecosystems are complex, cascading effects are real, and the history of human intervention in nature includes serious mistakes made with good intentions. It also gets right that the idea of reprogramming predators sounds strange, and that some present it with more confidence than the evidence allows. Pearce does entertain selective extinction as a discussable hypothesis, even while not advocating it, and it is understandable that this alarms people.

But the objection draws the wrong conclusion. The position being criticised rejects killing predators for the very ecological reason the objection raises, counts the predator's welfare too, and treats predation as a research question rather than a plan. Meanwhile, what is actually advocated is a good deal less exotic: vaccinate, treat, rescue, and study before doing anything larger.
