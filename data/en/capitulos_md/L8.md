# L8. "If vegans eat fake meat, they want meat"

**Thesis:** veganism's problem was never the taste of meat, but the victim. Many vegans liked the taste and texture of meat; what they refuse is what has to be done to the animal to get it. Wanting the taste without the death is exactly veganism's consistency. And names such as "burger" or "sausage" describe shapes and culinary uses, not animal origin.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Why call it a burger? A vegan sausage is a contradiction."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Products that imitate meat seek to reproduce its taste and texture.
2. **P2.** Those who eat these products want the experience of meat.
3. **P3.** If they want meat, their refusal is inconsistent or artificial.
4. **C.** So vegans are inconsistent.

P1 and P2 are true for many. The problem lies in P3: wanting a taste doesn't imply accepting what's done to obtain it.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Many vegans used to like the taste of meat, but they don't want animals to die. So they eat foods that remind them of meat, made from plants. That way they enjoy the taste without hurting anyone. That isn't a contradiction: it's a solution.

## 4. Short answer

Veganism was never against the taste of meat, but against what's done to the animal. Many vegans liked meat; what they refuse is the death. Wanting the taste without the victim is exactly veganism's consistency. And names such as "burger" or "sausage" describe shapes and uses: no one thinks a "hamburger" requires meat from Hamburg, or that "coconut milk" comes from a cow.

**Question to ask back:** "If it's possible to have the taste without killing the animal, what's the problem?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses what is being rejected:

| What the argument assumes the vegan rejects | What the vegan rejects |
| --- | --- |
| The taste of meat | Raising and killing the animal |
| The texture of meat | The suffering involved |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The problem is the victim, not the taste.** Veganism is a position about what we do to animals, not about tastes.

**2. Wanting the taste without the death is consistent.** It's precisely what plant-based products offer. The same reasoning appears in the debate about cultivated meat; see [The definition of veganism, debated](../alem/definicao-de-veganismo.html).

**3. The names describe shapes and uses.** "Burger", "sausage", "coconut milk" and "peanut butter" are culinary names, not promises of animal origin.

**4. Analogues help the transition.** People who like meat can change without giving up familiar dishes.

**5. The argument is used against any vegan choice.** If the vegan eats salad, it's "rabbit food" (J10); if they eat a plant burger, they "want meat".

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Rozin et al., 2012, *Journal of Consumer Research*, summarized by EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/505332) | The authors suggested that making soy burgers more meat-like could help hesitant men make the transition | Shows the role of analogues in changing habits |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: analogues are ultra-processed

The most serious version says that, even if wanting the taste is consistent, meat analogues are ultra-processed and aren't the best choice.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** From consistency to health (G14).
2. **Analogues are optional.** A vegan diet doesn't depend on them.
3. **Processed meat is ultra-processed too.** And classified as carcinogenic.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument unintentionally confirms the central point: taste isn't the problem. If it's possible to have the taste without the death, the moral question becomes even clearer: why choose the version that requires a death?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Uses "coconut milk" and "peanut butter".** Without finding them contradictory.
2. **Criticizes vegans who eat salad and those who eat burgers.** Any choice is criticized.
3. **Values alternatives in other areas.** Such as decaf coffee or alcohol-free beer.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone wants the taste of something, they should accept how it's produced".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol-free beer | Whoever drinks it "wanted alcohol" and should drink regular beer | Absurd |
| Decaf coffee | Whoever drinks it "wanted caffeine" | Absurd |
| Plant burgers | Whoever eats them "wanted meat" and should eat meat | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that many vegans like the taste of meat, and that analogues are made to reproduce it. It's also true that the names can confuse some consumers, and that clear labeling matters.

But wanting the taste without the victim isn't a contradiction: it's exactly the point. Veganism was never about taste, but about the animal.
