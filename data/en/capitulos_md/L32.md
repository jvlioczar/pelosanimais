# L32. "Look what you're missing"

**Thesis:** the barbecue photo in the comments of a vegan post shows the plate, but doesn't answer the question the post asked: what happened to whoever became the plate. Most vegans have eaten meat and know how it tastes; they didn't stop because they didn't know the flavor, but because they know the cost. And it's curious that those who share the photo of the steak rarely want to share the one from the slaughterhouse.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** A photo of a juicy steak in the comments of a vegan post, captioned "Look what you're missing".

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Veganism requires giving up pleasures most people value highly.
2. **P2.** No one would accept such a great loss without a very strong reason.
3. **P3.** Showing the lost pleasure makes the size of the sacrifice clear.
4. **C.** So veganism asks too much, and those who adopt it are missing something worthwhile.

P1 has a true core: changing eating habits has a cost, especially at the start. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the reason exists, and the size of the pleasure says nothing about the size of the harm on the other side.

The argument is **invalid**: from something being pleasurable it doesn't follow that it's just.

## 3. Answer for children

If someone showed off a beautiful cake made with money they secretly took from another person, the cake would still look nice, but no one would think it was right. Seeing tasty food doesn't change what happened for it to exist. Vegans know meat tastes good; they chose not to eat it because the animal wanted to live.

## 4. Short answer

I know the taste; I ate meat my whole life before I changed. I didn't stop because I didn't know the flavor; I stopped because I know the cost. The photo shows the plate, but not the animal. If the photo of the plate can be shared, why not the one of the slaughter?

**Question to ask back:** "Would you also post a photo of how this animal lived and died?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The taunt assumes vegans reject meat because they don't know or underestimate the pleasure. But the vegan decision usually has a different structure:

| What the photo assumes | What actually happens |
| --- | --- |
| The vegan doesn't know what they're missing | Most ate meat for years and know the flavor |
| Pleasure is the only factor | Pleasure is weighed against the animal's life and suffering |
| Seeing the plate will change their mind | Craving isn't an argument: wanting something doesn't make the act just |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Knowing the taste doesn't change the cost.** The vegan question was never "does meat taste good?". It was "does the flavor justify what is done to the animal?" (J5).

**2. The photo hides half the story.** It shows the result and leaves out the process. It's the same logic as "I don't want to see those videos" (E12): the plate can be seen; the animal can't.

**3. Temptation isn't a reason.** Craving something is common for people who've changed a habit, and it doesn't show the old habit was right. People who quit smoking also crave a cigarette when they see one.

**4. "Missing out" is relative.** The vegan stops eating one ingredient but still eats well. The animal, if eaten, loses everything.

**5. It's often a provocation meant to annoy, not to persuade.** It offers no reason at all; it just tries to stir up craving or a reaction.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Piazza et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2015.04.011) | "Nice" is among the four most common justifications for eating meat | The "4Ns" |
| [Bastian et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211424291) | Being reminded that meat comes from an animal that could feel causes discomfort, and people tend to reduce the mind they attribute to it | Helps explain why only the plate appears in the photo |
| Brehm, *A Theory of Psychological Reactance* (1966) | Threatened freedom leads to reasserting the behavior | The challenging tone of the taunt |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the sacrifice shows veganism is too hard

The most serious version says: if even vegans have cravings, veganism demands constant renunciation, and a morality that demands so much isn't reasonable for most people.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Cravings are common in any change.** That shows changing costs something at first, not that the change is wrong.
2. **Cravings fade.** Food preferences change with exposure, and many vegans report losing the craving over time.
3. **Difficulty isn't injustice.** That something is hard explains why many people haven't changed yet, but doesn't make what is done to the animal just (J3).

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if the photo stirred a craving in every vegan in the world, the question would remain the same: does the desire to eat a dish justify the life and death it required?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L32 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the question | Swaps "is it just?" for "is it tasty?" |
| Logical | Appeal to emotion | Uses the craving the image stirs instead of a reason |
| Epistemological | Selective omission | Shows the result and hides the process |
| Moral | Desire as justification | Takes the wish to eat as proof that eating is right |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Shows the plate, but not the animal.** Whoever shares the steak wouldn't share the slaughterhouse.
2. **Respects people who quit other habits.** No one sends a photo of a cigarette to someone who quit smoking and thinks it's a good argument.
3. **Admits pleasure is the reason.** By appealing to flavor, offers no other justification.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L32 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rationalization through the 4Ns | Flavor serves as a ready-made justification | [Piazza et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2015.04.011) |
| Meat-animal dissociation | The plate appears without the animal, which eases discomfort | [Bastian et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211424291) |
| Psychological reactance | The vegan post is felt as a threat to freedom and provokes a challenge | Brehm, 1966 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if something is tasty, whoever gives it up is wrong".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People who quit smoking or drinking | Showing the cigarette or the glass would be a good argument to go back | No one takes this seriously |
| Products of slave labor | If the product is good, whoever boycotts it is "missing out" | Pleasure doesn't erase injustice |
| Dog meat | An appetizing photo would make it acceptable to eat | Almost no one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that changing eating habits has a cost, and that this cost shouldn't be denied or ridiculed. Many vegans miss certain flavors, especially at first, and saying so honestly helps more than pretending there's no craving at all.

But the wish to eat isn't a moral reason. What's lost on one side is a dish; on the other, it's a life.
