# A27. "Equating animals with disabled humans will degrade humans"

**Thesis:** the objection gets one thing right: comparing people with animals has been used to dehumanize and mistreat disabled people, and disabled scholars and activists rightly criticize the way philosophy has used them as examples. But the argument from species overlap compares the criterion, not the worth of people: if what protects a human who doesn't reason is being able to feel and having a life that can go better or worse, that same criterion reaches many animals. The conclusion is to raise the protection of animals, not to lower anyone's. The case for animals doesn't need any hierarchy among people, and this guide doesn't endorse Peter Singer's conclusions about newborns and disability.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "When you say a pig is worth as much as a disabled person, it isn't the pig who goes up. It's the person who goes down."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The argument from species overlap, also called the argument from marginal cases, compares animals with humans who lack certain capacities, such as babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities, to show that species doesn't mark the moral difference.
2. **P2.** Comparing people with animals has a long history of being used to dehumanize, segregate and kill disabled people.
3. **P3.** An argument whose likely effect is to weaken the protection of a vulnerable group should be abandoned, even if it intends something else.
4. **C.** So the case for animals along this route should be abandoned, or it will end up degrading humans.

P1 is true. So is P2, and it should be taken seriously: the forced sterilization programmes of the eugenics movement and the Nazi regime's murder of disabled people, in the programme known as Aktion T4, were preceded by talk that described them as less than human. The problem lies in P3 and in the step to the conclusion: it assumes the argument can only work by lowering someone, and that abandoning animals would protect disabled people.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which confuses comparing criteria with comparing the worth of people, and doesn't show that the feared effect follows from the argument when it is well stated.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone says: "Don't hit your little brother, because he feels pain, just like you." That doesn't mean your brother is worth less. It means his pain matters, like yours. When we say animals feel pain too, it's the same thing: we aren't saying any person is worth less. We're saying the animals' pain matters too.

## 4. Short answer

You're right to be wary: comparing people with animals has been used to mistreat disabled people, and some philosophers have used them as examples carelessly. But the argument, done well, doesn't say anyone is worth less. It asks why we protect, rightly, a baby or a person who doesn't reason. The answer is that they feel and have a life that can go well or badly. So do animals. The conclusion is to protect animals more, not to protect anyone less. And abandoning animals wouldn't protect a single disabled person.

**Question to ask back:** "What, for you, makes a person with an intellectual disability deserve all the protection they have? Does that reason disappear when the one feeling pain is an animal?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The objection mixes two questions:

| Question | What the objection assumes | What the argument actually says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What gives someone moral consideration? | That the answer has to be a capacity that ranks people | That sentience, which all humans and many animals have, is enough |
| Which way does the comparison go? | Down: people lose protection | Up: animals gain the protection we already give people |

The argument from species overlap is a reply to those who say that reason, language or morality separate humans from animals (A9, A16, A21). It shows that these criteria would also exclude humans whom no one accepts excluding, so they can't be the criterion. The name "marginal cases" is criticized for treating people as a margin; the philosopher Oscar Horta proposes "species overlap", which describes only the fact that the capacities of humans and of other animals overlap (Horta, 2014). That's the name used in this guide.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The comparison is about the criterion, not about the worth of people.** Saying that a pig and a person feel pain doesn't say the person is worth what the pig is given today. It says the pig's pain should count as a person's pain counts. Peter Singer, who popularized the argument, writes in *Practical Ethics* that the aim is to raise the status of animals, not to lower that of any human being, and that it would be extremely pessimistic to refuse the change for fear that we might start treating disabled people the way we now treat animals, instead of extending to animals the concern we have for them (ch. 3).

**2. Equality among humans is a sufficient condition for counting, not a necessary one.** The philosopher Eva Feder Kittay, mother of a woman with a profound cognitive disability, holds that all disabled people have full worth and full moral status, because of the relations that make up each person's humanity (Kittay, 2009). The philosopher Katharine Wolfe notes that, in Kittay's formulation, the equal worth of humans is a sufficient condition for moral worth, not a necessary one: affirming it doesn't require denying worth to animals (Wolfe, 2022). This guide's position is compatible with that. No one has to leave the circle of protection for animals to come in.

**3. Disabled people's criticisms are serious.** They don't come from people who want to exploit animals, and they deserve to be heard on their own terms:

- **Eva Feder Kittay** criticized the way philosophers use people like her daughter as examples in arguments about moral status, and debated the issue with Singer and Jeff McMahan at a conference she organized with Licia Carlson at Stony Brook University in 2008, later published in the journal *Metaphilosophy* (2009).
- **Licia Carlson**, in *The Faces of Intellectual Disability* (2010), shows that people with intellectual disabilities appear in moral philosophy mostly as examples serving arguments that aren't about them, and argues for affirming their dignity by giving up comparisons with animals.
- **Harriet McBryde Johnson**, a lawyer and disability rights activist, went to a lecture by Singer on behalf of the group Not Dead Yet and recounted her encounters with him in "Unspeakable Conversations", a cover story in *The New York Times Magazine* in 2003. She challenged Singer's positions on disabled newborns.
- **Sunaura Taylor**, a disabled artist and writer, argues in *Beasts of Burden* (2017) that animal liberation and disability liberation are entangled. She defends animals and criticizes arguments that rank beings by cognitive capacities, Singer's included, and recounts a conversation with him.

**4. The criticism hits one version of the argument, not the case for animals.** What these authors contest most strongly are Singer's positions that make the value of a life depend on self-awareness, and the conclusions he draws from them about newborns and disability (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 6 and 7). This guide doesn't endorse those conclusions. A sentience criterion puts no one below the line: every human who feels is inside, whatever their capacities. The criticism is a good reason not to use the yardstick of rationality, and that is exactly what the argument from species overlap shows about that yardstick (A5, A18).

**5. Abandoning animals protects no one.** The protection of disabled people never depended on exploiting animals. The insult "you're an animal" hurts largely because we look down on animals; Taylor argues that contempt for animals and contempt for disabled people feed on the same ideas about what makes a life valuable. Undoing one helps undo the other.

**6. Research points against the fear.** In studies carried out in Canada, the psychologists Kimberly Costello and Gordon Hodson found that people who see a bigger divide between humans and animals tend to dehumanize immigrants more. In one experiment, reading a text that stressed animals' similarity to humans reduced that dehumanization (Costello and Hodson, 2010). These are studies with a specific target group, not with disabled people, but they don't support the idea that bringing animals and humans closer degrades humans.

**7. An arbitrary boundary isn't a safe protection.** Singer notes that the slippery-slope argument serves as a warning, but that a boundary drawn in the wrong place can also harm those on its human side, and that it's better to have a boundary that can be defended openly (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 3). The firmest protection for disabled people is one that rests on what they are, someone who feels and whose life matters to them, and not on a species label that can be contested.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: even well meant, the comparison wounds

The most serious version doesn't say the argument wants to lower anyone. It says it wounds anyway. Disabled people are set up as examples in debates they take no part in, next to animals, by authors who often don't know them. For someone who has been called an animal, institutionalized against their will or seen as a life not worth living, reading that "some people have capacities similar to a pig's" isn't neutral, however much the conclusion means to protect the pig. And Singer did draw conclusions that degrade these people, which shows the risk isn't imaginary.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a reason to change the way of arguing, not to abandon animals.** The criticism asks for care: talking about the criterion rather than using real people as examples; not reducing anyone to a list of capacities; listening to those affected. None of this requires accepting the exploitation of animals.
2. **The case for animals doesn't depend on the comparison.** The reason not to hurt a pig is the pig's pain. Species overlap only comes in to answer those who claim that reason or language is the criterion (A18, A21); those who don't use that criterion can do without the comparison.
3. **The risk comes from Singer's conclusions, not from the sentience criterion.** What degrades disabled people is making the value of a life depend on cognitive capacities. Sentience does the opposite: it puts every human inside and adds the animals.
4. **The two struggles can go together.** Taylor shows it's possible to defend animals and disabled people at the same time, criticizing the same cult of capacity that devalues both.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A27 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing criterion and worth | Treats a comparison of capacities as a comparison of people's worth |
| Logical | Slippery slope | Assumes that protecting animals necessarily leads to leaving people unprotected |
| Epistemological | Generalizing from one author | Attributes Singer's conclusions on disability to the whole case for animals |
| Moral | False dilemma | Presents the protection of animals and of disabled people as rivals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the comparison degrades people.** But it only degrades if being compared to an animal is a degradation, which presupposes the very contempt for animals under discussion.
2. **Defends full protection for disabled people because they feel and have a life that matters.** And denies the same reason when the one who feels is an animal.
3. **Uses disabled people's criticisms against animals.** Although authors like Taylor defend both causes together.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "an argument that compares one vulnerable group with another should be abandoned, because it degrades the first".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children's rights | One couldn't say children and adults feel pain in the same way | No one accepts this |
| Laws against cruelty to dogs | Protecting dogs because they feel pain would degrade humans | Almost no one accepts this |
| Disability rights | One couldn't say disabled people deserve the same protection as other humans, because the comparison could be misused | The principle turns against those it means to protect |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is right to remember that comparisons between people and animals have a history of violence, and that disabled people were treated as examples in debates that decided about them without them. It's also right to distrust an ethics that measures the worth of a life by cognitive capacities: that distrust is fair, and conclusions like Singer's about disabled newborns have been contested for good reasons.

But the answer to that history isn't to leave animals out. It's to use a criterion that leaves no one out. Whoever feels pain, fear and pleasure has a life that matters to them, and that holds for every disabled person and for every sentient animal.
