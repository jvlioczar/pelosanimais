# A27. "Human life can't be compared with an animal's life"

**Thesis:** the phrase usually comes from a legitimate concern: protecting human dignity and not trivializing people's suffering. But forbidding the comparison protects no one, and whoever says the phrase has almost always compared already: they concluded that human life is worth more, which can only be said by comparing. Comparing isn't equating. One can compare a person's life with an animal's, conclude that in many respects it weighs more, and still recognize that an animal's suffering and death count and can't be imposed for trivial reasons. What doesn't hold up is treating animals as if nothing that happens to them could be put on the same scale.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You can't compare them. A person's life can't be measured against an animal's."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Human life has a unique value, which can't be measured on the same scale as the life of other beings.
2. **P2.** Comparing human life with that of other beings already puts it on that scale, and this opens the way to treating some human lives as less valuable.
3. **P3.** History shows this risk: comparing people with animals has been used to dehumanize whole groups.
4. **C.** So human life can't be compared with an animal's, and animals' interests can't be put on the scale with ours.

P3 is true and should be taken seriously (A26, L22). P1 is the thesis under discussion and can't serve as a premise without argument. The bigger problem lies in P2 and the conclusion: comparing two beings doesn't require saying they are worth the same, and the ban on comparing protects no one. It only prevents us from asking whether what we do to animals is justified.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P1 assumes the conclusion, and P2 confuses comparing with equating.

## 3. Answer for children

Comparing doesn't mean saying two things are the same. You can compare a pencil and a pen and see that both of them write, even though they're different. A person and a dog are very different, but both of them feel pain and fear. That's why we shouldn't hurt either of them when we don't need to.

## 4. Short answer

I understand the concern: no one wants to see a person's life treated as if it were worth little. But comparing isn't equating. When you say human life is worth more, you're already comparing. I don't need to say a pig is worth the same as a person to say that his pain counts, and that killing him for a sandwich needs a better reason than "there's no comparison".

**Question to ask back:** "If they can't be compared, how do you know human life is worth more?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The phrase bundles three different claims:

| Claim | What it says | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Human life is worth more" | A comparison, with a result | Can be defended with reasons in many cases (A5, A20) |
| "A human life can't be sacrificed for animal interests" | A priority rule | Compatible with the case for animals, which doesn't ask for that sacrifice |
| "They can't even be compared" | A ban on asking | This is the one that doesn't hold up |

The best-known statement of the ban appears in the preface to the second edition of Peter Singer's *Practical Ethics*. In 1989, after protests against Singer's views on euthanasia for severely disabled newborns, a German organization of parents of people with intellectual disabilities withdrew its invitation for him to speak at a symposium and passed motions to distance itself from him. One of them said that the uniqueness of human life does not permit any comparison, let alone equation, of human existence with that of other living beings. Singer acknowledges that his book breaks this taboo and replies that forbidding all comparisons between species would be philosophically indefensible and would make it impossible to correct the wrongs now being done to animals (*Practical Ethics*, preface). The context matters: the motion grew out of the fear, understandable in Germany after Nazism, that comparing lives would open the way to declaring some human lives unworthy of being lived. This guide doesn't endorse Singer's conclusions about newborns and disability (A26).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Whoever says they can't be compared has already compared.** "Human life is worth more" is the result of a comparison. In practice, the ban only works one way: it prevents the comparison from giving a different result, or anyone asking whether the difference justifies everything done to animals.

**2. Comparing isn't equating.** A comparison can conclude that two beings differ in many things and are alike in others. Singer, for example, holds that pain of the same intensity and duration is equally bad in any species, but grants that the value of a life may vary with capacities such as self-awareness (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 3 and 5). One may accept that hierarchy or not (A5); what it shows is that comparing doesn't automatically lead to equality.

**3. We compare all the time, including in order to use animals.** Research that uses animals as models of human disease only makes sense because the bodies and nervous systems of other mammals resemble ours. Singer points out the dilemma: if the experiment tells us something about humans, the animal resembles us in the capacity to feel stress and pain as well (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 3). The comparison is accepted when it serves to use the animal and refused when it would serve to protect them.

**4. Since Darwin, the differences are of degree.** In *The Descent of Man* (1871), Darwin concluded that the difference in mind between humans and the other animals, great as it is, is one of degree and not of kind. Humans are unique in several things, but every species is unique in something. Being unique doesn't make something incomparable.

**5. The ban doesn't protect the people it aims to protect.** The protection of disabled people, or of any group, doesn't depend on banning questions about animals, but on a criterion that includes every human who feels (A26). A boundary that can't be examined also can't be defended when someone challenges it. And psychological research doesn't suggest that bringing animals and humans closer degrades humans: in Canadian studies, highlighting animals' similarity to humans reduced the dehumanization of immigrants (Costello and Hodson, 2010).

**6. What gets compared is interests, not lives in the abstract.** The case for animals rarely asks us to compare "a human life" with "an animal life" in general. It asks us to compare concrete interests: the suffering of a confined animal with the pleasure of a taste that has substitutes. Even someone who thinks a person's life is worth far more than a pig's has to explain why the person's lesser interest beats the pig's greater one (A5, A9).

**7. "Not of the same value" needs explaining.** Phrases like "animal life doesn't have the same value" or "animals don't have the same moral status" are vague until someone says value in what sense and status for what (Nobis, *Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, pp. 41–42). And they tend to support an invalid step: "if their life were worth as much as ours, taking it would be wrong; it isn't; so it isn't wrong". That's [denying the antecedent](../falacias.html#denying-the-antecedent): one life can be worth less than another and it can still be wrong to take it without need. Asking the person to say what they mean by "value" and "status" is often the most useful step in the conversation.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: human dignity isn't a quantity

The most serious version comes from Kant: things have a price, and persons have dignity, which has no equivalent. Comparing lives would treat dignity as a quantity that can be added up and traded. That's why a person's life wouldn't go on any scale, neither with other people's nor with animals'.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Saying something has no price doesn't stop us asking who else has it.** The Kantian idea forbids trading a person for advantages. It doesn't answer whether other beings also have something that can't be traded for advantages. The Kantian philosopher Christine Korsgaard argues that they do: animals are also ends in themselves, because things can go well or badly for them (Korsgaard, 2018).
2. **The rule the counter-reply defends is the one being asked for animals.** "A being who feels can't be sacrificed for others' advantage" is exactly what the case for animals proposes to extend. The counter-reply protects people; it doesn't show why the same doesn't hold for a pig.
3. **Not even Kant forbade thinking about animals.** Kant considered cruelty to animals wrong, though through an indirect duty: it would coarsen those who practise it. Even on his view, what is done to animals is subject to moral judgment.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A27 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Begging the question | Assumes the uniqueness of human life to conclude it can't be compared |
| Logical | Confusing comparing with equating | Treats any comparison as a claim of equality |
| Logical | [Denying the antecedent](../falacias.html#denying-the-antecedent) | "If it were worth the same, killing would be wrong; it isn't; so it isn't wrong" |
| Epistemological | Shutting down the question | Ends the discussion before the reasons are examined |
| Moral | Selective application | Accepts comparison in animal research and refuses it when it would favour animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they can't be compared.** And concludes that human life is worth more, which is a comparison.
2. **Accepts testing drugs on animals because they resemble us.** And denies the resemblance when their suffering is at issue.
3. **Compares animals with one another all the time.** Protects the dog as a member of the family and eats the pig, though both feel in comparable ways.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "the value or interests of beings from different groups can't be compared".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laws against cruelty to dogs | One couldn't say mistreating a dog is wrong because he feels pain as we do | No one accepts this |
| Medical research on animals | Animals couldn't be used as models of human disease | Those who use the argument usually accept such research |
| Veterinary medicine | Human painkillers couldn't be used on animals by analogy | A common and accepted practice |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is right to distrust comparisons made to degrade people: they have a real history of violence, and the dignity of each human being shouldn't be at the mercy of calculations. It is also right to recall that the differences between humans and animals exist and can matter.

But the answer to that risk isn't to forbid the question. It is to make the comparison carefully: compare criteria and interests, not the worth of people, and don't use the difference to justify anything at all. Recognizing that an animal feels takes nothing away from anyone.
