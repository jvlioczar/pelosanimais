# A25. "Animals don't know they're going to die, so killing them is fine"

**Thesis:** what's bad about death isn't knowing you'll die, but what death takes from the one who dies: everything they would still have lived. If not knowing made killing acceptable, it would be acceptable to kill babies, people with advanced dementia, or someone asleep with no inkling of what's about to happen. No one accepts that. And many animals, though they may not understand death, anticipate the future and feel fear in the face of danger.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** this chapter deals with anticipation and the relationship to the future. The question of pain at the moment of death is in A16

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The cow doesn't know it's going to die. It doesn't suffer over it, it doesn't make plans. So what's the harm?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Death is only bad for those who fear it or who have plans it cuts short.
2. **P2.** Animals don't know they're going to die, so they don't suffer in anticipation.
3. **P3.** Animals don't plan for the future or have self-awareness over time, so they have no projects that death frustrates.
4. **C.** So killing them does them no harm, as long as there's no pain.

P2 has some truth to it: most animals probably don't have a concept of their own death as we do. P3 is exaggerated, as studies of animal cognition show. The central problem lies in P1.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, which no one accepts when the victim is human, and at P3, which underestimates what many animals know of the future.

## 3. Answer for children

A baby doesn't know what dying is. A person who's asleep isn't thinking about it either. Even so, everyone knows that hurting them would be very wrong, because they'd lose everything they were still going to live. It's the same with animals: not knowing they'll die doesn't make their life worth less to them.

## 4. Short answer

Not knowing you'll die doesn't make death harmless. A baby, a person with advanced dementia or someone sleeping peacefully also don't know they're about to die, and killing them would be one of the worst crimes. What's bad about death is what it takes: all the life still to come. And the animal loses that, whether it knows or not.

**Question to ask back:** "Would it be acceptable to kill someone who's asleep, painlessly, just because they don't know what's about to happen?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes different ideas about why death would be bad:

| Idea | What it says | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Death is bad because of the fear it causes | Whoever doesn't know they'll die doesn't suffer | Confuses the suffering of anticipating death with the harm of death itself |
| Death is bad because it frustrates plans | Whoever doesn't plan loses nothing | Ignores that one can lose a good future without having planned it |
| Death is bad because of what it takes (deprivation) | Whoever dies loses the good experiences they would still have had | Applies to any being with a future of experiences, human or not |

The third idea is the most widely accepted in philosophy, since Thomas Nagel, and it's the only one that explains why killing someone in their sleep is wrong. It depends on neither knowing nor planning.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The human test, with those who don't know.** Babies have no idea what death is. People with advanced dementia or profound intellectual disabilities may have no notion of it and make no plans. By the argument, killing them painlessly would be acceptable. No one accepts that conclusion.

**2. The human test, with those who know but aren't thinking about it.** An adult who's asleep generally knows they're mortal, but at that moment feels no fear and isn't planning anything. If they're killed in their sleep without waking, they don't suffer in anticipation. Even so, it's a very serious crime, because they lose everything they would still have lived. The same goes for someone killed by surprise, without noticing the danger.

**3. The harm of death isn't the fear of death.** Fear of dying is an additional suffering, worth avoiding. But the absence of that fear doesn't eliminate the main harm: the loss of the future.

**4. Many animals anticipate the future.** Western scrub-jays cache food with the next morning's hunger in mind. Chimpanzees and orangutans keep tools to use later. The idea that animals live in a pure present doesn't match what the studies show, even though we don't know whether any of them understands its own death.

**5. Fear doesn't require understanding death.** Animals feel fear in the face of threats, and transport and slaughter are known sources of fear and stress. An animal may not know what dying is and still suffer at the moment it's taken to die.

**6. The perception of time may be different.** There's evidence that smaller animals with faster metabolisms perceive visual changes at shorter intervals than we do. This suggests their experience of time may be denser, not poorer, than we imagine.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: time-relative interests

The most serious version comes from Jeff McMahan: the harm of death depends on how psychologically connected a being is to its own future, through memory, plans and self-awareness. Animals would have a weaker connection, and so would lose less by dying than an adult human.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Losing less isn't losing nothing.** The counter-reply abandons the conclusion that "killing is fine" and starts discussing the size of the harm.
2. **The harm has to be weighed against the benefit.** On the other side is a pleasure of taste (J11). Even a smaller harm weighs more.
3. **The same account would downgrade humans.** Babies and people with advanced dementia also have a weak psychological connection to the future. Almost no one accepts that killing them is a small harm (A12, A16).
4. **Animals have a connection to the future.** Memory, expectation and stable preferences exist in many species. The difference is one of degree.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Proves too much | It would authorize killing babies, people with dementia and people who are asleep |
| Logical | Confusing concepts | Treats the fear of dying as if it were the harm of death |
| Epistemological | Underestimating animal cognition | Assumes animals live in a present without a future |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts deprivation as a harm for humans and denies it for animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Considers killing someone in their sleep a crime.** Even though the victim knew nothing.
2. **Protects babies and people with dementia.** Who don't know they'll die and make no plans.
3. **Mourns the unexpected death of a pet.** Even if it didn't know it was going to die.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains A25 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated denial of mind | Attributing less mind to the animals we eat makes their death less troubling | [Bastian et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211424291) |
| The meat paradox | The idea that the animal doesn't know eases the conflict between liking it and eating it | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| In-group bias | A human's death is a harm even without awareness of it; an animal's isn't | [Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29517258/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever doesn't know they'll die, or isn't thinking about it, isn't harmed by being killed painlessly".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Babies | Killing them painlessly would be acceptable, because they don't know what death is | No one accepts this |
| People with advanced dementia or profound intellectual disabilities | They could be killed painlessly, because they have no notion of death and make no plans | Contradicts human rights |
| Adults asleep | They could be killed in their sleep, because at that moment they neither fear nor plan anything | It would be one of the most serious crimes |
| Victims of sudden death | Someone killed by surprise, without noticing the danger, would have suffered no harm | Contradicts the common judgment that they lost their life |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that anticipating death is a real source of suffering, which many humans have and most animals perhaps don't. It's also right that species differ in their relationship to the future, and that this may matter in hard comparisons.

But the main harm of death isn't knowing it's coming. It's losing everything still to come. If not knowing made death harmless, we could kill babies and sleeping people. Since no one accepts that, it doesn't work for animals either.
