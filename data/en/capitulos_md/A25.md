# A25. "Treating animals well is a matter of compassion, not justice"

**Thesis:** the argument grants that cruelty to animals is wrong, but places that duty in each person's conscience, outside what the state should guarantee. The problem is that a duty no one can enforce gives way to whoever wants to break it: under a regime that protects only liberty, even a daily sacrifice of animals out of personal conviction would be allowed. The criteria used to exclude animals from justice, such as cooperating or having a sense of justice, would also exclude babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities. And liberalism itself, through the harm principle and equal concern for well-being, has the resources to include those who can be harmed.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Of course no one should be cruel to animals. But that's a matter of each person's conscience, not of law."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We have moral duties regarding animals, such as not being cruel, but they are duties of compassion and humanity.
2. **P2.** Justice, which the state may enforce, holds among moral persons: those who cooperate in social life and are capable of a sense of justice and a conception of their own good.
3. **P3.** Animals aren't moral persons in that sense.
4. **C.** So treating animals well is a matter of individual compassion, not of justice the state should guarantee.

This is, broadly, John Rawls's position in *A Theory of Justice* (1971, §77), which recognizes duties of compassion and humanity toward animals but leaves them outside the principles of justice. P1 is true, and it's an important concession. P3 is true of most animals. The problem lies in P2: it draws the boundary of justice in a place we no longer accept for humans.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, and the conclusion leaves animals unprotected exactly where protection is most needed.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine a school rule that says "don't hit your classmates", but no one enforces it: whoever wants to hit, hits, and nothing happens. That rule protects no one. It's similar with animals. Saying "be kind to animals, if you feel like it" doesn't protect a little pig from someone who doesn't feel like being kind. That's why there are laws against cruelty: to protect the animal even when someone doesn't feel sorry for him.

## 4. Short answer

I agree that cruelty to animals is wrong. But if that stays only in each person's conscience, those who don't care can do as they please, and the animal is left unprotected. Compassion matters, but it isn't enough: would you accept that hitting a child is "a matter of conscience"? And the criterion used to take animals out of justice, cooperating and having a sense of justice, would also leave out babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities, whom no one excludes. The question is whether those who can be harmed deserve protection for what they are, and not only for the pity they inspire.

**Question to ask back:** "If protecting an animal depended only on each person's compassion, what would happen to the animals of people who feel none?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument separates two questions and answers only one:

| Question | The argument's answer | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is it wrong to be cruel to animals? | Yes, out of compassion | Why that would be a purely private matter |
| Should the state protect animals for their own sake? | No, that isn't justice | That a duty without force gives way to whoever wants to break it |

The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane sums up what it means to include animals in justice in two conditions: how we treat them becomes something the political community enforces, not just a private matter; and it does so for the animals' own sake, not out of charity or because of effects on humans (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 3–4). The argument accepts the moral part and denies both conditions.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The distinction has a history we've already abandoned.** Thomas Aquinas denied animals not only justice but even charity, and saw cruelty as wrong only because it might lead to cruelty toward humans. Kant repeated the idea with "indirect duties": we shouldn't be cruel to animals because it hardens our dealings with people. On that view, hidden cruelty that affected no one's character wouldn't be wrong. Almost no one accepts that conclusion, which is why the cruelty laws of many countries define the offence by the animal's suffering, not by harm to some human.

**2. Compassion without the force of justice gives way.** The philosopher Ruth Abbey (2007) argued that Rawls's duties of compassion could offer animals strong protection. Cochrane replies that this moral duty loses to Rawls's first principle, the liberty principle: if someone's conception of the good involved the daily sacrifice of many healthy animals, and that didn't restrict other people's liberty, the Rawlsian state would have no basis to stop it, however immoral the practice (Cochrane, 2010, p. 61). A duty no one can enforce depends on the goodwill of whoever breaks it.

**3. The criteria would exclude humans too.** Rawls gives two reasons for leaving animals out: justice would govern a scheme of cooperation in which only contributors receive, and it holds only for moral persons capable of a sense of justice. Babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities neither contribute nor have a sense of justice, and no one accepts that protecting them is merely a matter of compassion (see A21). And if the criterion were contributing, many animals would count: those on farms, in laboratories and in homes sustain a large part of the economy (Cochrane, 2010, p. 56).

**4. Liberalism has the resources to include animals.** Mill's harm principle allows liberty to be limited to prevent harm to others, and harm includes well-being, not only freedom. The principle of equal concern and respect, as Ronald Dworkin reads it, has two halves: respect protects persons' autonomy, and concern protects the well-being of those with needs. Dworkin applied the principle only to humans, but Cochrane argues that the concern half reaches those who aren't persons and have a welfare, such as babies and animals (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 68–69).

**5. The law has already made the move.** Many countries punish cruelty even when committed by the owner, and Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union requires the welfare of animals "as sentient beings" to be taken into account in areas such as agriculture, fisheries, transport and research. The text comes from a protocol to the Treaty of Amsterdam (1997) and moved into the body of the treaty with the Treaty of Lisbon (2009). It has exceptions for religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage, which shows its current limit. China, by contrast, still has no general national law against animal cruelty, and protection depends on rules scattered across other laws and standards. Both cases show that the line between compassion and justice is a political choice, not a fact.

**6. Kindness and cruelty describe character, not what makes an act right.** The philosopher Tom Regan called the "cruelty-kindness" view the idea that our duties to animals come down to being kind and not being cruel. He doesn't deny that kindness is a virtue or that cruelty is a serious failing; he denies that they answer the question of what is right. A person can act out of kindness and still act wrongly: someone who is generous only to members of their own racial group has a kind motive and an unjust conduct. And the absence of cruelty doesn't guarantee that nothing wrong is done: a researcher who regrets the pain they cause isn't cruel, and the question about the experiment remains open (Regan, 1985; cited by Nobis, 2016, p. 14). That's why treating animals as "a matter of compassion" isn't enough: compassion says what the character of the person acting should be like, but not what is owed to the one affected. That is the question of justice.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the state shouldn't turn sensitivities into law

The most serious version says: in a plural society, the state shouldn't impose a conception of the good life, and how much each person is moved by animals is part of that conception. Compassion is a virtue, and virtues can't be produced by law. What the state can do is punish cruelty that shocks everyone or brutalizes people, as it already does, without saying animals are subjects of justice.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Preventing harm isn't imposing a conception of the good.** Liberal neutrality has never covered harm to others. Banning assault on a person doesn't impose a way of life; the same holds for those who feel pain, if well-being counts (E2).
2. **Protecting only for the sake of the effect on humans leaves out what no one sees.** If cruelty is punished only because it shocks or brutalizes, the cruelty hidden in sheds and laboratories, which almost no one sees, is left out. That's precisely where most animals are (B10).
3. **The law already protects the animal for its own sake.** The offence of cruelty exists even when no one saw it and no one was shocked. What defines the offence is the animal's suffering.
4. **There are hard cases, but they don't make the whole field private.** Cochrane admits it isn't easy to decide when liberty should give way to well-being: a single animal sacrificed once a year for religious reasons, hunting, horse racing (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 69–70). These are the honest frontiers of the debate. But a hard boundary doesn't show that everything inside it is a matter of individual conscience.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says cruelty is wrong.** And accepts that it go unpunished whenever the owner wishes.
2. **Supports punishing someone who mistreats a dog.** Which is already treating the case as a matter of justice, not compassion.
3. **Includes babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities in justice.** Who also don't cooperate or have a sense of justice.
4. **Accepts limiting liberty to protect human well-being.** And refuses the same when the well-being is an animal's.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "those who aren't moral persons receive only compassion, and the state shouldn't protect them for their own sake".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Babies | Protecting them would be a matter of their parents' kindness | No one accepts this |
| People with profound cognitive disabilities | Protection would depend on the pity they inspire | No one accepts this |
| Dogs and cats | Cruelty laws would be illegitimate | Most people support them |
| Daily animal sacrifice out of personal conviction | The state couldn't stop it | Almost no one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is right to recognize that we have duties to animals, and right to recall that not every moral demand should become law. It's also true that compassion matters: it's often what first alerts us to an injustice, and no law works without a culture that supports it.

But compassion is the beginning, not the limit. When what's at stake is harm to those who can suffer, protection can't depend on who feels pity and who doesn't. That is what it means to treat someone as a subject of justice.
