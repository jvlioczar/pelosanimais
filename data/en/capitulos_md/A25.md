# A25. "Animals are property"

**Thesis:** saying an animal is property describes what the law says today; it doesn't justify what the law allows. Slavery was also a regime of legal property, and that didn't make it just. The law itself already recognizes that animals aren't ordinary things: punishing cruelty to your own dog only makes sense if someone is being harmed, not an object being damaged.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The cow is mine, I bought it. I'll do what I want with it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The right of property lets the owner use, sell and dispose of what is theirs.
2. **P2.** Under the law, farmed animals are the property of those who raise or buy them.
3. **C.** So the owner may use and kill their animals as they please.

P2 is true as a description of most legal systems, which treat animals as goods. The problem is using that description as a justification.

The argument is **valid** on the legal plane and **invalid** on the moral plane: from "the law allows it" it doesn't follow that "it's just" (D9). And not even on the legal plane does the conclusion hold in full, because the owner can't do just anything with their own animal.

## 3. Answer for children

A long time ago, some people were treated as if they belonged to other people, as if they were objects. Today everyone knows that was very wrong, even though it was allowed back then. Calling someone "mine" doesn't turn them into a thing. A little pig feels fear and pain, even if someone says he's theirs.

## 4. Short answer

Being property is what the law says today, not a moral reason. Slavery was also a regime of legal property, and that didn't make it just. And not even the law lets you do anything: cruelty to your own animal is a crime, precisely because the animal can be harmed, unlike a chair. The question is whether it's right to treat as a thing someone who feels.

**Question to ask back:** "If the dog is yours, can you beat it? Why does the law say you can't?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two kinds of question:

| Question | Type | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the law treat animals as property? | Descriptive | Today, largely yes |
| Is it just to treat animals as property? | Normative | That's what's under discussion |

Using the first answer to settle the second is moving from "is" to "ought" (Hume's law) and appealing to the law as if it were the measure of morality (D9).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Property has been applied to people.** Slavery was a legal property regime, with buying, selling and inheritance. It was legal, and it wasn't just. That something is property has never settled whether it should be.

**2. Not even the law permits everything.** Cruelty to animals is an offence in the UK, the EU countries, the United States, Brazil and many other places, including when committed by the owner. No one is prosecuted for breaking their own chair. The law already recognizes that the animal is someone who can be harmed.

**3. The law is changing.** France amended its Civil Code in 2015 to define animals as living beings endowed with sentience. In Brazil, the Senate approved a bill in 2019 that removes animals from the status of things, though with an amendment excluding animals used in agriculture, research and cultural events. The bill went back to the lower house.

**4. The exception reveals the problem.** Excluding precisely farmed animals from recognition as subjects doesn't answer the moral question; it shows that property status is kept for economic convenience, not because these animals feel less.

**5. Property isn't a criterion of value.** What gives a being moral weight is the capacity to be harmed, not the title of whoever owns it.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: property with duties

The most serious version admits that property in animals isn't unlimited and defends a model of "property with responsibility": the owner may use and kill, but must avoid cruelty. That way the law would protect animals without abolishing their use.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Protection yields where there's profit.** Cruel practices such as extreme confinement and mutilations remain permitted when they're economically useful (B9). "Responsibility" adjusts itself to the owner's interest.
2. **It still doesn't justify property.** Limiting the use of something doesn't explain why it's legitimate to own someone who feels.
3. **The same model was tried with people.** Laws regulating the treatment of enslaved people didn't make slavery acceptable.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hume's law (from is to ought) | From "it's property" it concludes "it may be used" |
| Logical | Appeal to the law | Treats the current legal rule as the measure of morality |
| Epistemological | Misdescribing the law | Ignores that the law already forbids cruelty by the owner |
| Moral | Power as legitimacy | The title of ownership becomes a justification for any use |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Condemns those who mistreat their own dog.** If property decided, there would be no crime.
2. **Considers slavery unjust, though it was legal.** Recognizes that property isn't a criterion of justice.
3. **Treats their own animal as someone.** Gives it a name, takes it to the vet and grieves its death.
4. **Accepts limits on property in everything else.** No one may use their own house to pollute the neighbor's.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains A25 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System justification | The current legal arrangement seems the correct arrangement | Linked to B9 and D9 |
| Status quo bias | The current status seems the only possible one | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Functional categorization | "Production" animals are seen as things, and "pets" as someone | [Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569805/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what is property may be used however the owner wishes".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enslaved people | Slavery would be just as long as it was legal | No one accepts this |
| Dogs and cats | The owner could mistreat them | The law forbids it |
| Historic buildings | The owner could demolish at will | The law protects listed buildings even when privately owned |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument correctly describes the current law: largely, animals are still treated as goods, and changing that status requires careful institutional design, with representation and clear rules. It's also true that property is an important institution and that no one should have their goods taken arbitrarily.

But the moral question comes before the law: can someone who feels be someone else's thing? The law has already answered no to that question for humans, and has begun to answer it for animals. The argument only describes where the answer hasn't arrived yet.
