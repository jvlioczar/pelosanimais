# L13. "A vegan who buys from companies using slave labor isn't really vegan"

**Thesis:** the argument gets an important point right: veganism rests on not causing avoidable harm to sentient beings, and humans are sentient beings too. Vegans have the same reasons to avoid illegal products or products from companies convicted of slavery-like labor: once they find out, they have a duty to act. But the argument gets the conclusion wrong: failing one duty doesn't cancel the other, and the same demand applies to non-vegans, who usually consume the same products and also eat animals. In Brazil, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from such conditions.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "A vegan who buys clothes from a brand caught using slave labor, or who buys drugs from traffickers, isn't vegan. They contribute to human exploitation all the same."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Veganism rests on the idea of not causing avoidable harm to sentient beings.
2. **P2.** Humans are sentient beings.
3. **P3.** Someone who buys products from companies convicted of slavery-like labor, or illegal products linked to violence, contributes to harming humans.
4. **C.** So a vegan who does this is inconsistent, and their veganism doesn't count.

P1, P2 and P3 are true, which is why the argument deserves to be taken seriously. The conclusion, however, mixes two things: showing that someone is inconsistent doesn't show that their position is wrong, or that exploiting animals becomes acceptable.

The argument is **valid** as to the inconsistency. Its **soundness** fails in the step from "inconsistent" to "veganism doesn't count".

## 3. Answer for children

Someone who doesn't want to harm animals shouldn't want to harm people either, and that's right. Sometimes a piece of clothing or a food was made by people who were treated very unfairly. The right thing is to try to avoid both: harming animals and harming people. Getting one wrong doesn't make the other stop mattering.

## 4. Short answer

You're right on one point: veganism is about not harming those who can feel, and humans can feel. Vegans have the same reasons to avoid products made with slave labor: once they find out where something comes from, they need to act. But failing at that doesn't make exploiting animals acceptable, and the same demand applies to non-vegans: in Brazil, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from slavery-like conditions. Avoiding the exploitation of animals and of humans are duties that go together.

**Question to ask back:** "If human exploitation is the problem, will you also avoid meat from farms caught using slave labor?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What's true | What doesn't follow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Veganism asks us not to harm sentient beings | Humans are sentient too | That an inconsistent vegan should go back to exploiting animals |
| Buying from companies using slave labor causes harm | It contributes to human exploitation | That non-vegans are exempt from this demand |
| An inconsistent vegan "isn't vegan" | The inconsistency exists and should be fixed | That veganism is wrong |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The starting point is right.** If the reason not to exploit animals is that they can suffer, the same reason applies to humans. A consistent vegan should avoid products of slavery-like labor and of supply chains linked to violence. No one knows the whole supply chain, but once someone finds out, they have a duty to act: knowing creates responsibility, and preferring not to know doesn't remove it (E12).

**2. Inconsistency doesn't refute the position.** Showing that someone fails to follow a principle doesn't show that the principle is wrong. Someone who defends human rights and buys a product of slave labor is inconsistent, but that doesn't make human rights false.

**3. The two duties don't compete.** Avoiding the exploitation of animals and of humans are parts of the same idea. Failing at one doesn't license abandoning the other.

**4. The demand applies to everyone.** Non-vegans usually consume the same products, and also eat animals. According to data from Brazil's Observatory for the Eradication of Slave Labor, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from slavery-like conditions in the country.

**5. Knowing the origin is hard, but there are tools.** Brazil's Ministry of Labor publishes the "Dirty List" twice a year, the register of employers caught using slavery-like labor. No one controls the whole supply chain, but you can avoid what you know about.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: veganism is selective about human suffering

The most serious version says some vegans mobilize a lot for animals and little for workers, and that this reveals an inverted hierarchy in which animals would matter more than people.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Some people's selectivity doesn't define the position.** Consistent veganism includes all sentient beings, humans too.
2. **The criticism works as an invitation, not a refutation.** It shows where vegans can improve, not that exploiting animals is right.
3. **Those who make the criticism are held to it too.** Concern for workers is welcome, and should lead those who raise it to look at the meat they eat as well.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to exploit those who can suffer when there is an alternative, whether animal or human. The argument helps broaden that question, but doesn't answer it in favor of exploitation.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Tu quoque | The vegan's inconsistency is used to dismiss their argument |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If the vegan isn't consistent in everything, veganism doesn't count |
| Moral | Selective concern | Human exploitation comes up only to criticize vegans |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Holds the vegan to account for slave labor.** And doesn't look at the meat they eat.
2. **Says humans matter.** And uses that to justify exploiting animals.
3. **Demands total consistency.** And accepts inconsistency in their own consumption.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L13 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double standard | Demands of the vegan what they don't demand of themselves | General literature on moral judgment |
| Moral licensing | Pointing out another's failing eases one's own | General literature on moral behavior |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "someone who is inconsistent with a principle loses the right to defend it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone who defends human rights and buys from a brand caught using slave labor | They couldn't defend human rights | No one accepts that |
| A non-vegan who eats meat from a farm caught using slave labor | They'd be inconsistent too | The demand applies to everyone |
| Avoiding both kinds of exploitation | It's what the principle asks | This is the alternative |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that veganism rests on not harming sentient beings, that humans are included, and that vegans should avoid products of slave labor and of supply chains linked to violence whenever possible.

But failing at that doesn't make exploiting animals acceptable, and the same demand applies to non-vegans. The two duties go together.
