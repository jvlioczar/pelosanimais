# E18. "Why should I be ethical if it doesn't benefit me?"

**Thesis:** the question is serious and deserves an honest answer: there is no proof that every egoist is irrational, and even Peter Singer, one of the leading philosophers of the case for animals, admits it. But the question already stands outside ethics: it doesn't ask whether exploiting animals is right, but whether it's worth doing what is right. For that there are good reasons, though not demonstrations: a life turned only towards oneself tends not to satisfy even the person living it, and happiness tends to come from devoting oneself to something larger than one's own interest. And for the animals, the answer doesn't change the moral question: if the act is wrong, it stays wrong, whether or not it pays off for the person doing it.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Fine, maybe it's wrong. But what do I get out of not eating meat?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Practical reason chooses the means to get what we already want; it doesn't tell us to want one thing or another.
2. **P2.** Acting ethically towards animals brings me no benefit, and sometimes costs me.
3. **P3.** If an action serves nothing I want, I have no reason to do it.
4. **C.** So I have no reason to act ethically towards animals.

P1 is a thesis of David Hume, hard to refute, and Singer largely accepts it (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 12). P2 is debatable: what counts as a benefit depends on what the person wants, and almost everyone wants more than their own immediate pleasure, such as a meaningful life and being able to look themselves in the mirror. P3 confuses two kinds of reason: it may hold for reasons of self-interest, but it doesn't touch the ethical question, which is whether the action harms someone.

The argument is **valid**, but its **soundness** depends on P2, which is uncertain, and its conclusion answers only the question about motivation, not the moral one.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine you find a hurt little bird and nobody is watching. Helping won't win you any prize. Even so, lots of people help, and they feel good about having helped. Doing good doesn't always win a prize, but it usually makes our life feel fuller.

## 4. Short answer

It's an honest question. I won't pretend there's proof that being ethical always pays off: even philosophers who defend animals admit there isn't. But notice that you didn't say exploiting animals is right; you said you gain nothing by stopping. For the animal, that changes nothing. And for you, it's worth thinking about: are people who live only for their own interest usually happier? Many people's experience, and some of the research, suggests that satisfaction comes more from living in line with something you believe in.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you do everything only for what benefits you, or are there things you do because you think they're right, even when you gain nothing?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The question is old. In Plato's *Republic*, Glaucon asks whether anyone would stay just if they had the ring of Gyges, which made its wearer invisible and free from any punishment. Peter Singer devotes the last chapter of *Practical Ethics* to it. He notes that some readers accept the arguments about animals and about poverty but change nothing, and that some of them aren't simply weak-willed: they are asking why they should care about ethics at all, if it demands so much (ch. 12). The levels need separating:

| Question | Type | Where it's dealt with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Is exploiting animals right?" | A question within ethics | Families A to N of this guide |
| "Why should I do what is right?" | A question about ethics, asked from outside it | This chapter |
| "Should everyone pursue only their own interest?" | An ethical theory, ethical egoism | [Ethical egoism and Objectivism](../politicas/egoismo-objetivismo.html) |

Whoever asks this chapter's question has usually conceded the first: they aren't saying exploiting animals is just, but that they see no benefit in stopping.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The honest concession: egoism isn't, in itself, irrational.** Singer examines the argument, from the Kantian tradition, that acting rationally is acting ethically, and rejects it. A pure egoist can't turn their principle into an ethical rule, because it doesn't hold from everyone's point of view; but they can adopt it as a rational basis for action, just as two salespeople competing for the same customer recognize that the other is acting rationally (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 12). That's why this guide doesn't say the egoist is incoherent. It says their question is a different one.

**2. The question doesn't change the moral answer.** If exploiting an animal harms them needlessly, it is wrong, even if the person doing it gains nothing by stopping. "What's in it for me?" asks whether the person will act, not whether the act is just. Someone who asks what they gain by not deceiving others doesn't thereby show that deceiving is acceptable.

**3. A life lived only for oneself tends not to satisfy.** Singer discusses the extreme case of people described as psychopaths, who say they enjoy life without caring about anyone, and grants that there's no way to prove they are wrong about their own happiness. But he notes that most reflective people want their lives to have some meaning, and that few would deliberately choose a life they thought empty. The so-called paradox of hedonism says that those who seek happiness directly rarely find it, while those who devote themselves to other aims find it along the way. Singer's example is Dennis Levine, a Wall Street banker in the 1980s who earned more and more, never felt satisfied and ended up in prison for insider trading (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 12). Singer warns that these ideas are speculative.

**4. Ethics offers a purpose that doesn't run out.** For Singer, taking up the ethical point of view, what Henry Sidgwick called "the point of view of the universe", is a way of giving life a meaning that goes beyond one's own states of consciousness. As long as there is avoidable suffering in the world, that purpose doesn't run out. He doesn't say that those who refuse this path are irrational: he says that, for those who reflect on what they want from life, ethics offers a meaning hard to match (ch. 12).

**5. What research has shown since 1993.** Singer regretted that there was little data on the link between acting ethically and being happy. Since then the research has grown, with modest and debated results. In a 2008 study, people given money to spend on others reported more happiness at the end of the day than those who spent it on themselves (Dunn, Aknin and Norton, 2008). A 2020 preregistered replication with larger samples confirmed the effect in two experiments but not in a third, and found small effects (Aknin et al., 2020). Another study found that helping others is more closely linked to feeling that life is meaningful than to feeling happy in the moment (Baumeister et al., 2013). The data don't prove that ethics always pays, but they don't support the idea that it only costs either.

**6. The benefit criterion turns against those who use it.** If one's own benefit were the only reason to respect someone, those who are stronger would have no reason to respect the interests of the person asking. Most people expect to be treated with consideration even by those who gain nothing from it. The attempt to base morality only on an exchange of benefits leaves out babies, future generations and everyone who can't reciprocate (A23).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: then ethics is just one preference among others

If there's no proof that acting ethically is rational, ethics seems to be just a personal taste, like collecting stamps. Those who like it follow it; those who don't have no reason to. Singer himself uses this example: for some people, collecting stamps is a perfectly adequate way of giving their lives a purpose (ch. 12).

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The question about motivation doesn't settle the question of right and wrong.** That someone doesn't feel motivated to act ethically doesn't make the harm acceptable. If the argument goes on to say there is no right or wrong at all, it becomes a different one, relativism, dealt with in E3.
2. **Society doesn't leave serious harms to individual taste.** Singer acknowledges that the question has no answer that will convince everyone, and that laws and social pressure will remain necessary against serious violations (ch. 12). No one treats the decision not to mistreat a dog as an optional hobby.
3. **Those who ask the question are usually those best placed to understand the answer.** Singer observes that people who reflect enough to ask why they should be ethical are also the ones most likely to see the reasons for taking up the ethical point of view. The question is a beginning, not an end.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E18 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the question (red herring) | Answers "what's in it for me?" when the question is "is it right?" |
| Moral | Self-interest as justification | Treats a reason of personal benefit as if it were an ethical reason |
| Epistemological | Unfounded certainty | Assumes it knows that acting ethically brings the agent nothing |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Asks what they gain by not harming animals.** And expects others not to harm them, even when those others gain nothing from it.
2. **Says they only act for benefit.** And does things for friends, children or pets that bring them no benefit at all.
3. **Admits the act may be wrong.** And treats that as irrelevant.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "I only have reason to do what benefits me".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Returning a found wallet when no one is watching | There'd be no reason to return it | Most people think there is |
| Caring for an elderly parent who can't repay | There'd be no reason to care for them | Almost no one accepts this |
| Not polluting a river that will only affect future generations | There'd be no reason not to pollute | See A23 and N22 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is right to ask for honesty: there's no proof that ethics always pays, and anyone who promises that promises too much. It is also right to recall that arguments alone move no one, and that changing has real costs.

But the lack of proof isn't proof of the opposite. The question "what's in it for me?" doesn't settle whether what is done to animals is just, and for many people who take it seriously the answer turns out to be more than they expected: a life more consistent with what they believe.
