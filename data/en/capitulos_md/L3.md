# L3. "Vegans are crazy; they have mental problems"

**Thesis:** calling someone crazy is a way of not answering what they say. If the vegan question is whether it's just to make animals suffer for a taste, the mental health of the person asking doesn't change the answer. The idea that vegans have "mental problems" mixes an insult with a misread statistical association, and it treats mental health as something to mock, which is unfair to anyone who lives with a disorder.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans are all crazy. You have to be nuts to cry over a chicken."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** People with mental disorders tend to have less reliable beliefs and behavior.
2. **P2.** Some studies associate vegetarian diets with more symptoms of depression and eating disorders.
3. **P3.** Caring intensely about farm animals is a sign of emotional imbalance.
4. **C.** So vegans' ideas don't deserve to be taken seriously.

P2 has a basis in some studies, with important caveats. The problem lies in P1 and P3: judging an idea by the health of whoever holds it is an attack on the person, and caring about others' suffering isn't a symptom of illness.

The argument is **invalid**: it's ad hominem. Even if the premise about health were true, it would say nothing about the animals.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "Vegans are all crazy." |
| Everyday conversation | "You have to be nuts to cry over a chicken." |
| Everyday conversation | "That's a psychological problem." |

| Technical version | "Vegetarian diets are associated with more depression." (which is G12) |

## 3. Answer for children

When someone doesn't know how to answer a question, sometimes they call the person who asked a name. But the name-calling doesn't answer anything. If a person asks "is it right to hurt the little pig?", calling them crazy doesn't change what the little pig feels.

## 4. Short answer

Calling someone crazy doesn't answer the question: is it just to make animals suffer for a taste? On mental health, some studies find an association between vegetarian diets and symptoms of depression, but the authors themselves say it isn't possible to know what causes what, and there are signs that many people stop eating meat after a diagnosis. And using mental disorders as an insult is unfair to those who live with them, whether they eat meat or not.

**Question to ask back:** "If a person with depression says two plus two is four, does the sum become wrong?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes three things:

| Claim | Type | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Vegans are crazy" | Insult | Not an argument |
| "There's an association between vegetarianism and some symptoms" | Empirical | Found in some studies, with uncertain cause |
| "So what they say about animals is wrong" | Logical | Doesn't follow: it's an attack on the person |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's ad hominem.** The truth of a claim doesn't depend on the health of whoever makes it. An animal's pain doesn't get smaller because the person pointing it out has depression, anxiety or any other condition.

**2. The association doesn't show cause.** A 2021 meta-analysis found higher depression scores in vegetarians, with great variation between studies, and the authors stress that the direction of the relationship can't be established. There are signs that many people start avoiding meat after a diagnosis, not the other way round (G12, G20).

**3. Sensitivity to suffering isn't illness.** Caring about others' suffering is one of the foundations of any morality. If it were a symptom, anyone who cries at an injured child would be ill too.

**4. The insult is unfair to people with mental disorders.** Using "crazy" or "mental problem" as an insult reinforces the stigma against people who already suffer, vegan or not.

**5. Prejudice against vegans is documented.** Research shows vegans are the target of negative attitudes equal to or greater than those directed at several groups that are commonly targets of prejudice. Pathologizing an opponent is a well-known way to dismiss them without discussing what they say.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Ocklenburg and Borawski, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2021.07.098) | Meta-analysis: vegetarians had higher depression scores, with great heterogeneity; the direction of the relationship can't be established | *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 294 |
| [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) | Omnivores evaluated vegetarians and vegans equally or more negatively than several common prejudice target groups | *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*, 20(6) |
| [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550611415695) | Meat eaters tend to disparage vegetarians because they expect to be judged by them | *Social Psychological and Personality Science*, 3(2) |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: obsession with the subject shows imbalance

The most serious version says: it isn't the idea, it's the intensity. People who talk about animal suffering all the time, are outraged at every meal and cut ties over it would be showing an unhealthy fixation.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Intensity doesn't decide truth.** A person can talk too much about a subject and still be right about it.
2. **Comparing with other causes helps.** People devoted to fighting child labor or hunger aren't called ill for it.
3. **Some people's behavior doesn't define the group.** If someone handles their indignation badly, that's a personal matter, not an answer to the question about animals (L1).

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if some vegan had a mental disorder, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die for the pleasure of taste? The answer doesn't depend on who asks it.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Ad hominem | Judges the idea by the health of whoever holds it |
| Logical | Correlation taken as cause | Uses a statistical association as proof |
| Epistemological | Hasty generalization | Attributes to all vegans what holds, at most, for some |
| Moral | Stigma | Uses mental disorders as an insult |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't reject the ideas of people with depression on other subjects.** A sum or a fact still holds.
2. **Admires people moved by the suffering of children or dogs.** But calls people moved by pigs ill.
3. **Condemns the stigma against mental disorders.** But uses the label as an insult.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L3 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do-gooder derogation | Dismissing those with a moral stance eases the feeling of being judged | [Minson and Monin, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550611415695) |
| Prejudice against vegans | The group is judged negatively before any argument | [MacInnis and Hodson, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1368430215618253) |
| Confirmation bias | Cases of vegans with problems confirm the opinion; the others are ignored | General literature on confirmation bias |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if whoever holds an idea has, or seems to have, a mental problem, the idea is wrong".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Science | Discoveries by scientists with depression would be false | Absurd |
| Humanitarian causes | Anyone devoted to a cause would be ill | No one accepts this |
| Any debate | Calling the other person crazy would be enough to win | It would be the end of rational discussion |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that mental health matters, that there are studies on the relationship between diet and depressive symptoms worth attention, and that vegans, like anyone, can suffer from indignation and need support. Looking after one's own mental health is part of sustainable activism.

But the health of whoever holds an idea doesn't decide whether it's true, and using mental disorders as an insult is unfair to everyone who lives with them.
