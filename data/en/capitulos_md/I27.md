# I27. "The cart is the carter's livelihood, and the horse is well cared for"

**Thesis:** the livelihood of people who work with carts is a real social issue, and any ban needs to come with alternative sources of income. But an urban draft horse usually works on asphalt, in the heat and in traffic, pulling heavy loads, and what happens to it when it gets sick or old is rarely seen. Cities such as Petrópolis, in Brazil, decided to replace horse-drawn carriages with electric vehicles: in a 2018 plebiscite, about 68% of voters rejected animal traction in tourist rides. The way forward that respects both sides is to replace the horse, not the worker.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You want to take away a poor carter's livelihood because of a horse? He looks after the animal; it's how he makes a living."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many poor families depend on carts to work, collecting recyclables or doing hauling.
2. **P2.** The owner has an interest in looking after the horse, his working tool.
3. **P3.** Banning carts takes income away from those who need it most.
4. **C.** So animal traction should continue.

P1 is true. P2 holds in part, but economic interest doesn't guarantee welfare. P3 is true if the ban comes without an alternative; it doesn't have to.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it treats the horse-drawn cart as the only way to keep that income.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people use horses to pull carts around the city, carrying heavy loads on hot asphalt and among cars. These people's work is important, but the same work can be done with motorized or electric carts. That way the person keeps working and the horse gets to rest.

## 4. Short answer

The carter's livelihood is a real social issue, and any change needs to come with alternative income. But an urban draft horse works on asphalt, in the heat and in traffic, pulling heavy loads, and what happens to it when it gets sick or old is rarely seen. Petrópolis, in Brazil, decided to replace tourist carriages with electric vehicles after a 2018 plebiscite. The way forward is to replace the horse, not the worker.

**Question to ask back:** "If the carter got a motorized vehicle and support to work, would he still need the horse?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Problem | An answer that respects both sides |
| --- | --- |
| The carter's income | Programs replacing carts with motorized or electric vehicles, registration and support |
| The horse's welfare | Retirement from work, referral to care or adoption |
| Tourist carriages | Electric carriages, as proposed in Petrópolis |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Economic interest doesn't guarantee welfare.** Caring enough for the animal to keep working is different from respecting its needs. Asphalt, heat, loads and traffic cause injuries, exhaustion and accidents.

**2. The choice isn't between the horse and the poor.** Brazilian cities that restrict animal traction combine the measure with vehicle replacement programs and support for workers. The social problem is the lack of an alternative, not the lack of a horse.

**3. Some places have already decided.** In Petrópolis, in a 2018 plebiscite, about 68% of voters rejected animal traction in tourist rides, and the city began replacing carriages with electric vehicles.

**4. Caring about the horse doesn't exclude caring about the carter.** The accusation assumes a competition that the combined solution removes (J16).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: replacement programs don't reach everyone

The most serious version points out that, in practice, many carters are left without an alternative when a ban arrives, and the measure ends up punishing the poorest.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a criticism of how the policy is done, not of its goal.** The right thing is to demand that replacement reach everyone.
2. **The two problems can be solved together.** Motorized or electric vehicles keep the income and take the horse out of suffering.
3. **Keeping the horse doesn't solve poverty.** Dependence on the cart is a symptom of lack of opportunity, not a solution.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair for an animal to pay, with its body, for the lack of alternatives of the people who work. A fair answer looks after both.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I27 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either the horse works, or the carter goes hungry |
| Epistemological | Economic interest as a guarantee | Assumes those who depend on the animal look after it well |
| Moral | The animal pays for inequality | The lack of human alternatives justifies the animal's suffering |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Defends the poor.** And doesn't demand programs that give them an alternative.
2. **Says the horse is well cared for.** And sees exhausted horses in traffic.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same with dogs.** Dogs pulling carts around the city would be seen as cruelty.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I27 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False dichotomy | Caring for the animal seems opposed to caring for the person | General literature on argumentation |
| Status quo bias | The cart seems the only possible way to work | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an animal is a poor person's livelihood, its use should continue".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dancing bears of poor families | They should continue | Programs replaced the bears with other sources of income |
| Children working to support their family | They should continue | The answer was to protect the child and support the family |
| Motorized vehicles for carters | They keep the income without the horse | This is the solution |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that carters' income is a serious social issue, that many are fond of the animal, and that bans without alternatives punish the poorest.

But the fair solution isn't to keep the horse on the asphalt; it's to guarantee people alternative work and the animals care.
