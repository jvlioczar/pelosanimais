# N19. "The future will certainly be better, so there's no need to worry so much"

**Thesis:** moral progress isn't automatic: it happens when someone makes it happen. What the last century shows is that power and knowledge grow far faster than consideration for those affected, and that this gap is precisely what produces harm on a new scale. Industrial animal farming is the example: better technology was used to create more victims, not fewer. For whoever is being harmed, indifference on the part of those deciding is already enough to produce immense damage, without anyone having to want to do harm.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** doubt about predicting the future is N18; this chapter is about swapping that doubt for optimism

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Humanity has always improved. A hundred years from now nobody will eat animals. This sorts itself out."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** History shows a continuous expansion of the moral circle and a decline in violence.
2. **P2.** Technology tends to replace cruel uses with better alternatives.
3. **P3.** These trends should continue.
4. **C.** So future suffering tends to fall by itself, and requires no special effort.

P1 describes something real but incomplete, and P2 confuses possibility with tendency. P3 turns an observation about the past into a law about the future.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3: the same technology that reduces one kind of suffering can multiply another, and nothing guarantees the observed direction continues.

## 3. Answer for children

The good things we have today didn't appear on their own: someone worked so that they would. And not everything got better. When humans learned to build enormous machines, they managed to keep far more animals shut in than before. Getting better isn't something time does: it's something people do.

## 4. Short answer

Moral progress doesn't happen by itself: every advance had people working for it, against resistance. And in the case of animals the recent trend runs the other way: technology multiplied the number of victims instead of reducing it. What decides the outcome isn't time but three things — the intentions of those deciding, the knowledge they have and the power they accumulate. More power with the same indifference gives a worse future, not a better one.

**Question back:** "If progress were automatic, why did someone have to fight for every right that now seems obvious?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The Senciência piece on the triad of influence offers a simple way to see why the optimism doesn't hold. The outcome of an agent's decisions depends on three factors, and not on the calendar.

| Factor | Question | What happens when it's missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intention** | Does the agent want to benefit, to harm, or is it indifferent to those affected? | Indifference is enough for gigantic harm, as a side effect of other goals |
| **Knowledge** | Does it know how to achieve what it intends? | Good intentions without knowledge produce large harms |
| **Power** | Can it carry this out? | Without power, intention doesn't materialise, for good or ill |

The uncomfortable point is indifference. Nobody has to want harm for harm to appear: it is enough that whoever decides doesn't take the affected into account, and has enough power. That is how industrial animal farming came about. Technology raised the "power" factor without touching the "intention" one.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The recent trend, for animals, is the opposite.** The number of animals farmed and killed each year grew through the twentieth century and keeps growing, especially with the expansion of aquaculture. If the criterion is "what has happened so far", the verdict goes against the optimism.

**2. Power grows faster than consideration.** The expansion of the moral circle is slow, contested and reversible; the technical capacity to breed, confine and process sentient beings grows fast. That gap is exactly what produces harm on a new scale.

**3. Indifference is enough.** Much of the suffering humans cause doesn't come from cruelty but from goals that simply don't include those affected. Infrastructure projects, habitat occupation and supply chains harm enormous numbers of animals without anyone intending to harm them.

**4. The progress that did happen was made by someone.** Abolition, suffrage, labour rights, civil rights: none was an effect of time. All had organised movements, strong resistance and setbacks along the way. Treating that as an automatic trend erases the work that produced it, and removes the reason to continue it.

**5. Technological substitution isn't automatic.** Technologies that would reduce animal use exist, but their adoption depends on price, regulation, acceptance and economic interest. There have been cases where the better alternative existed and did not prevail. Expecting technique to solve it is delegating the decision to a process nobody is steering toward that goal.

**6. The optimism is a long-term prediction, and a strong one.** Anyone who rejects long-term planning for lack of predictability (N18) while asserting that the future will be good is using uncertainty selectively: it blocks the concern and licenses the calm.

**7. The asymmetry between erring high and erring low.** If we worry for nothing, the cost is effort spent in vain. If we don't worry and we were wrong, the cost can be enormous and irreversible. Given that asymmetry, optimism is the more expensive bet.

### (d) The best reply: the expanding moral circle

The serious reply isn't "it'll all work out". It's the observation, supported by historical evidence, that the set of beings considered morally has been widening for centuries, and that the recognition of animals follows the same curve, with institutions, legislation and changing attitudes keeping pace.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The expansion is slow and contested.** Nothing in it guarantees a pace sufficient against a fast-growing capacity to cause harm.
2. **It already contains the effort.** The circle widened because people widened it. Using the result of that work as a reason not to work inverts the logic.
3. **There have been reversals.** The very history grounding the reply includes them, and the institutions that protect advances have to be built and defended.
4. **Circle expansion and victim reduction aren't the same thing.** More people can recognise animals morally while the number of animals exploited keeps rising. That is what has been happening.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in N19 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Weak induction | Turns an observed trend into a law about the future |
| Logical | Selective evidence | Uses the decline of violence among humans and ignores the growth of animal exploitation |
| Epistemic | Selective use of uncertainty | The future is unpredictable for the worry and predictable for the optimism |
| Moral | Justified omission | Expected improvement becomes a reason to do nothing |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It buys insurance and pays into a pension.** It doesn't trust "it'll be fine" when the risk is its own.
2. **It thinks other causes are worth fighting for.** Which would also "improve by themselves" by the same reasoning.
3. **It acknowledges that industrial animal farming is recent.** Which already shows the direction can worsen.
4. **It praises those who fought in the past.** And refuses the same logic in the present.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up N19 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if the historical trend is toward improvement, there is no need to act".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil rights | Waiting would have been enough, with no movements and no laws | Contrary to history |
| Climate change | Mitigation would be unnecessary, because technology would solve it | Contrary to the scientific consensus on the need for action |
| Safety of new technologies | Nothing would need regulating | Contrary to practice in almost every high-risk sector |
| Industrial animal farming itself | It would have been an impossible regression, since the world only improves | It exists, and it is recent |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets right that there has been real moral progress and that this is worth acknowledging: the circle of those considered has been widening, which is no small thing. It also gets right that catastrophism paralyses, and that a cause that speaks only of disaster loses people along the way. Expecting a better future is, besides being pleasant, part of what motivates action.

What doesn't hold is treating the improvement as automatic. It depends on three things that are not time: on whoever decides taking the affected into account, on knowing how to do it, and on having the power to. When power grows and consideration doesn't keep up, the result is a worse world for those left out of the account — and that is exactly how the largest source of human-caused suffering that exists today came about.
