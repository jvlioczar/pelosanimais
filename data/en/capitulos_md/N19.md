# N19. "Sentientists support population control and antinatalism for wild animals"

**Thesis:** this is partly true: many sentientists judge that, in species where almost all young die soon after birth, fewer births may mean less suffering, and fertility control is already used in some populations. But there is no ready-made answer. Everything depends on which animals would be harmed and which would benefit, case by case: fewer prey can mean hunger for predators, and fewer animals of one species can favor another. That's why the serious position is to research before acting, not to go around applying a rule. There is also a rights objection (sterilizing would violate a right to reproduce or to bodily integrity), which authors such as Luciano Carlos Cunha discuss and which remains a sensitive point in the debate.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** this chapter is about birth control. Predation is N17, and parasitism is N18

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "These sentientists want to sterilize the animals in the forest. They're antinatalists: they think it's better for animals not to be born at all."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Sentientists claim that, in the wild, most animals die soon after birth, suffering.
2. **P2.** If being born mostly leads to suffering, they conclude it's better for fewer animals to be born.
3. **P3.** A position that wants less life in nature is antinatalist and absurd.
4. **C.** So sentientism should be rejected.

P1 describes a real, well-grounded thesis about population dynamics. P2 is a conclusion some authors draw, with caveats. P3 treats an empirical, case-by-case question as if it were a general doctrine.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3: the serious position isn't "less life, always", but "assess who is harmed and who benefits".

## 3. Answer for children

In nature, many animals have hundreds of babies, and almost all of them die soon from hunger, cold or illness. Some people think that if fewer babies were born, fewer animals would suffer. But nature is full of connections: changing one thing changes others. That's why we need to study very carefully before deciding anything.

## 4. Short answer

You're partly right: in species where almost all the young die soon, many sentientists judge that fewer births may mean less suffering, and fertility control without killing is already used in some populations, such as the wild horses of Assateague Island, in the United States. But there's no ready-made answer: everything depends on which animals would be harmed and which would benefit in each case. That's why the serious position is to research before acting.

**Question to ask back:** "If reducing a population can either lessen or increase suffering, isn't that exactly why it has to be studied case by case?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The objection lumps together three different things:

| Idea | What it is | Who holds it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Many species produce far more young than survive | A fact of population dynamics | Biologists and ethicists |
| In some cases, fewer births may reduce suffering | A hypothesis to assess case by case | Some sentientist authors |
| All wild life should end | A general doctrine | Practically no one in the debate |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The premise about nature is serious.** In many species, each female produces hundreds or thousands of young, and in a stable population only a few reach adulthood. The philosopher Oscar Horta argued in 2010 that, for this reason, suffering and early death are the rule rather than the exception (N1).

**2. Controlling fertility isn't killing.** Wildlife contraception is already practiced. On Assateague Island, in the United States, experimental use of a contraceptive vaccine (PZP) on wild mares began in 1988 and went on to stabilize the population without culling.

**3. But the result depends on who is affected.** Fewer young of one species can mean fewer animals dying of hunger and disease, and also less food for predators, which may then go hungry, or more room for other species, with their own young. The right question isn't "less or more life?", but "which animals would be harmed and which would benefit?".

**4. That's why the serious position is one of research.** The proposal of a welfare biology exists precisely to measure these effects before acting at scale. What's defended today are forms of help with known effects: vaccinating, treating the injured, rescuing in disasters, controlling fertility in small, well-studied populations.

**5. "Antinatalist" is an imprecise label.** Philosophical antinatalism is the thesis that coming into existence is always a harm. Someone who assesses case by case whether a smaller population would suffer less doesn't have to accept that thesis.

**6. The rights objection: the right to reproduce.** A more serious version of the criticism says that sterilizing would violate a right of the animals. In the book *Razões para ajudar* (Reasons to Help, 2022, pp. 151–156), the philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha examines this objection. On the right to reproduce, he argues that even those who defend it don't treat it as absolute: a couple who knew their child would live only to suffer intensely would have strong reason not to have children. He proposes two conditions for that right to exist: that the harms to third parties, including those who will be born, stay below a certain threshold, and that there be a real interest in reproducing (p. 154). In nature, he notes, forced copulation is frequent, and what animals seek is sex, not necessarily offspring. And a right to reproduce gives permission, not an obligation; so it also wouldn't justify forcing animals to breed in conservation programmes (p. 152, n. 79).

**7. The right to bodily integrity isn't absolute either.** Sterilization is an intervention on the body without consent, and that carries weight. Cunha replies that this right usually gives way before the life and integrity of third parties, and that, in a species where each reproduction produces thousands or millions of young who die soon, the third parties affected are many. Appealing to the difference between acting and letting happen doesn't help, he says, because reproduction itself is also an action that causes harm (pp. 154–155).

**8. "So would you sterilize poor people?" This is the most delicate point.** Cunha distinguishes cases where it is the biology of reproduction that rules out a good life, as in species with millions of young, from those where the obstacle is social, as with poverty, which is addressed with resources, not sterilization. He proposes three conditions, valid for any species, for sterilizing someone without consent: a high probability that the offspring would live mostly in extreme suffering, a high probability of reproducing, and no other less harmful and equally effective means (p. 155). This part calls for care: history includes forced sterilizations of poor, Indigenous and disabled people, and the reproductive justice tradition rejects any principle of sterilizing human beings without consent. The comparison can be read as an echo of that past, and Cunha uses it precisely to separate the cases; anyone citing it should make the distinction clear and note that it is contested.

**9. "Why not feed the young, as we do with the poor?"** Cunha replies that, in species with many young, it would take an environment trillions of times larger than the planet to feed them all; that these species rarely care for their offspring; and that, if they survived, the young would reproduce in turn, exhausting the resources (pp. 155–156). The example he uses is the ocean sunfish: around 300 million eggs have been found in a single female, and, in a stable population, on average only two young per mother need to reach adulthood to replace the parents. The objection makes more sense for species with few young.

**10. It isn't the same as using animals in experiments.** Someone might say that, if it's right to sterilize to prevent harm to others, it would also be right to use test animals. Cunha points out two differences: in experimentation, the harm is caused by the disease, not by the animal used; and there are research methods that don't use animals, whereas the deaths that come from mass reproduction can't be avoided without preventing the reproduction (p. 156).

**11. Rights advocates already accept neutering dogs and cats.** Gary Francione, one of the leading animal rights advocates, accepts neutering domesticated animals, whose average litter is four or five young, because of the suffering uncontrolled breeding causes. Cunha notes that the reasons would be far stronger for species with millions of young (p. 156). That doesn't settle the question for those who reject intervening in nature, but it shows that sterilization as such isn't foreign to animal advocacy.

**12. If the goal is to reduce births, sterilizing females is more efficient.** A single male can fertilize many females, so sterilizing males may not reduce births; sterilizing females always does (Cunha, p. 231, n. 133). It's the same reasoning used in contraception programmes like Assateague's, which treat the mares.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: tampering with wild species' reproduction is arrogant and risky

The most serious version says that ecosystems are too complex for us to predict the effects of reducing a population, and that history is full of interventions that went wrong.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It reinforces the answer.** That's exactly why the serious position calls for research and small cases before any action at scale (N9).
2. **We intervene all the time already.** Cities, farming, hunting and "pest" control change wild populations every day, without asking about the animals' suffering. The difference is taking it into account.
3. **The risk doesn't make the question illegitimate.** Asking which animals would suffer more or less is the opposite of acting blindly.
4. **Many forms of help don't involve reproduction at all.** Vaccinating, treating, providing water and shelter and rescuing in disasters don't touch anyone's reproduction; the objection, even if sound, only reaches part of the proposals (Cunha, 2022, p. 152).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N19 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Straw man | A case-by-case assessment becomes a doctrine of "less life, always" |
| Epistemological | Oversimplifying complex systems | Assumes the effect of reducing a population is obvious, for good or ill |
| Moral | Omission bias | Not acting seems neutral, even in the face of much avoidable suffering |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts population control when it serves humans.** "Management" hunting, pest control and spaying stray dogs and cats are seen as normal.
2. **Supports spaying strays.** It's birth control to reduce suffering, by the same reasoning.
3. **Says nature is too complex to intervene in.** And accepts huge interventions when they bring economic benefit.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains N19 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "any proposal to reduce animal births is absurd antinatalism".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spaying and neutering street dogs and cats | It would be absurd antinatalism | It's public policy recommended by vets |
| Contraception for Assateague's wild horses | It would be absurd | It stabilized the population without culling |
| Ending all wild life | It would be absurd | Here the criticism is right, but no one in the debate holds this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that some sentientists conclude that, in certain cases, fewer births would mean less suffering, and that this conclusion sounds strange at first. It's also true that intervening in wild animals' reproduction carries serious risks, and that ecosystems are complex. And the rights objection touches a real point: sterilizing without consent is a serious intervention, and the comparison with human cases calls for great care, given the history of forced sterilizations.

But that calls for what the serious position already proposes: assessing case by case which animals would be harmed and which would benefit, researching first, and starting with forms of help whose effects are known. There's no ready-made answer, either for intervening or for never intervening.
