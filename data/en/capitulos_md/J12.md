# J12. "This is my living; it feeds my family"

**Thesis:** the concern of those who live from livestock farming is real, and deserves respect and concrete help: debts, land, know-how and a whole life built around the activity. But depending on an activity to make a living doesn't make it just, and the argument would hold for any activity that causes harm. The way out is a planned transition, and there are already livestock farmers who have made it, switching to growing mushrooms, grains or plant milks. Changes in consumption are gradual, and society can and should support those who need to change.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's easy for you to say. This is my living. This is what feeds my family, and it was the same for my father and my grandfather."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** My family and I depend on livestock farming to live.
2. **P2.** Giving up the activity would bring poverty, debt and the loss of everything we've built.
3. **P3.** No one is obliged to drive their own family into poverty.
4. **C.** So I'm not obliged to leave livestock farming.

P1 and P3 are true. P2 may be true if the change were sudden and unsupported. The problem lies in the conclusion: it treats economic dependence as if it justified the activity, and assumes the only alternative is to give everything up at once.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: dependence explains why change is hard, but it doesn't make the activity just, and there are paths to transition.

## 3. Answer for children

Some families earn money raising animals, and they need that money to live. No one wants them to go without. But there are other ways to make a living in the countryside, like growing beans, fruit or mushrooms. Some farmers have already changed, and others can change with help.

## 4. Short answer

Your concern is fair: it's your livelihood, and no one should leave you stranded. But depending on an activity doesn't make it just to the animals. Changes in consumption are gradual, and there are already livestock farmers who have made the transition, switching to growing crops, cultivating mushrooms or making plant milk. What I'm arguing for is that society help those who need to change, not that you be abandoned.

**Question to ask back:** "If there were support to switch to another kind of farming, with a guaranteed income, would you consider it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes two different questions:

| Question | What the argument answers |
| --- | --- |
| Is it hard for those who live from it to change? | Yes, and the difficulty is real |
| Does dependence make the activity just? | No; it explains, but doesn't justify |
| Does change have to mean giving everything up at once? | No; it can be gradual and supported |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Depending on an activity doesn't make it just.** Many people have lived from activities that caused harm, such as the trade in enslaved people, child labor, asbestos mining or whaling. The economic dependence was real, but it didn't make those activities acceptable. The same argument would hold for any of them.

**2. Change is gradual.** Consumption of animal products changes over years and decades. That lets producers adapt, diversify and change little by little, without losing everything at once.

**3. There are already livestock farmers who have made the transition.** In the United States, Craig Watts, who raised chickens for Perdue, switched to growing mushrooms and vegetables in the old sheds; the Transfarmation Project, run by the organization Mercy For Animals, helps other producers do the same, with technical guidance, market studies and investment in converting the sheds, and by 2024 it had worked with 12 producers. In New York, the Elmhurst dairy, which had been bottling milk since 1919, left cow's milk production in 2016 and switched to making plant milks. In England, livestock farmer Jay Wilde handed his herd over to a sanctuary and moved to vegan organic farming, a story told in the documentary *73 Cows*, which won the BAFTA for best short film in 2019.

**4. There are models of public support.** In 2020, the Netherlands created a program that paid pig farmers to shut down; 278 of them had signed up by 2021. The goal was environmental, not animal protection, but the program shows the transition can be funded by the state.

**5. The difficulties are real, and should be said.** Transition programs are still small, and debts on sheds and equipment are a big obstacle. Craig Watts himself says the number of producers that can be helped is limited. That's why the transition needs public policy, credit and markets, not just the goodwill of each producer.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: those who demand the change don't pay its price

The most serious version says: it's easy for people living in the city to demand that the producer change. The one who pays the price of change is the producer, with their own land, debts and family. Demanding without offering anything in return is unjust.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The counter-reply is right about the fairness of the transition.** The cost of change shouldn't fall on the producer alone. That's why it makes sense to argue for credit, technical support and public purchasing of plant foods.
2. **But that changes the how, not the whether.** The injustice of a badly handled transition is a reason to handle it well, not to keep the activity forever.
3. **The animals pay a price too.** And their price is their life. A just transition has to take both sides into account.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Even if change were hard for those who live from livestock farming, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die to generate income, when there are other ways to generate income? The answer calls for support for those who need to change, not for everything to stay as it is.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing explaining with justifying | Dependence explains why change is hard, but doesn't make the activity just |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either everything is kept, or everything is lost |
| Moral | Forgetting the one affected | Income is weighed, and the animal's life isn't |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts that other harmful activities have ended.** Even when many people lived from them.
2. **Says they take good care of their animals.** But the income depends on the animals dying.
3. **Many producers already diversify.** They plant, lease land, switch crops when prices fall.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo bias | The current activity seems the only possible one | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Motivated reasoning | Those who depend on an activity tend to see it as just | Linked to J11 |
| Rationalization through the 4Ns | "Necessary" and "normal" justify the activity | [Piazza et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2015.04.011) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone depends on an activity to make a living, the activity is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Child labor | It would be justified for the families that depended on it | It was banned, with support policies |
| Asbestos | It should continue, since it supported workers | It was banned in many countries |
| Whaling | It should continue wherever it supported families | It was restricted by international agreements |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that the dependence is real, that the producer can't be abandoned and that demanding change without offering support is unjust. It's also right that many livestock farmers have a relationship of care with the land and with their family, and that this deserves respect.

But depending on an activity doesn't make it just. The way forward is a planned, supported transition, and some people have already made it.
