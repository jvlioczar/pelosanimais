# J12. "I eat little meat; that's enough"

**Thesis:** eating less meat is better than eating more, and it deserves recognition. But cutting down doesn't justify what remains: if causing an animal's death without need is wrong, doing it less often is less wrong, not right. Cutting down can be an excellent path, as long as it's a path and not a destination.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I only eat meat on weekends. I do Meatless Monday. I'm flexitarian."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Reducing meat consumption reduces the number of animals killed and the environmental harm.
2. **P2.** A large reduction by many people can have more impact than total abstention by a few.
3. **P3.** Demanding total abstention puts people off.
4. **C.** So eating little meat is enough.

P1 is true. So is P2, in terms of aggregate impact. P3 has some strategic support. The problem lies in the conclusion: that reducing is good doesn't make the rest justified.

The argument is **valid** as a strategic argument. It fails as a moral justification of what remains.

## 3. Answer for children

Hurting someone less often is better than hurting them a lot. But the best thing is not to hurt them. Eating less meat already helps animals, and it's a good start. The next step can be gradually swapping the meals that still have meat.

## 4. Short answer

Eating less meat is good, and it deserves recognition: fewer animals die. But if killing an animal without need is wrong, doing it less often is less wrong, not right. Cutting down works very well as a path. The problem is when it becomes the destination and a justification for what remains. For every meal that still has meat, the question is the same.

**Question to ask back:** "If cutting down was good for the animals, why stop at cutting down?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes two kinds of claim:

| Claim | Type | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cutting down is better than not cutting down | Comparative | True |
| Cutting down is enough | Normative | Doesn't follow from the first |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Better isn't enough.** That an action reduces a harm doesn't mean the remaining harm is acceptable.

**2. The question applies to every meal.** Each animal killed for a remaining meal has the same interest in living as the animals spared.

**3. It's moral licensing.** Having done something good can make one feel entitled not to do more (E6).

**4. Strategy doesn't change ethics.** It may be good to encourage reduction as a communication strategy, but that doesn't make what remains morally neutral.

**5. Cutting down already shows it's possible.** Whoever manages days without meat has already shown that meat isn't necessary.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Our World in Data, less meat or sustainable meat](https://ourworldindata.org/less-meat-or-sustainable-meat) | Eating less meat almost always cuts emissions more than switching to "sustainable" meat | Shows the real value of cutting down |
| [Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)](https://campuspress.yale.edu/shellykagan/papers) | Each meat purchase has a real expected effect on production | Shows that remaining consumption counts too |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the perfect is the enemy of the good

The most serious version says that insisting on total abstention can discourage people who are cutting down, and that it's better to celebrate reduction than to criticize it.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Celebrating isn't closing the matter.** It's possible to recognize progress and invite the next step.
2. **The criticism is of the argument, not the person.** "That's enough" is a conclusion, and it can be discussed respectfully.
3. **Cutting down is the most common path to veganism.** Many vegans started by cutting down.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Cutting down implicitly recognizes that eating animals has a moral cost. If it does, the question doesn't disappear with the reduction: it still applies to every animal still on the plate.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | From comparative to sufficient | "Better" becomes "enough" |
| Moral | Moral licensing | The reduction authorizes the rest |
| Moral | False middle ground | The middle between eating a lot and eating none seems right |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Recognizes that cutting down is good.** And therefore that eating meat has a cost.
2. **Shows they can go without meat.** For most meals.
3. **Wouldn't accept "a little" for other harms.** Such as mistreating animals "only now and then".

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moral licensing | The good deed seems to make up for the rest | Literature on moral licensing |
| Meat paradox | Cutting down eases the tension without resolving it | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if I've reduced a harm, what remains is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drink-driving | Doing it only on weekends would be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Lying | Lying less would be enough | No one concludes this |
| Cruelty to animals | Mistreating them only sometimes would be acceptable | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Cutting down on meat is a real change, which spares animals and reduces environmental harm. Those who have cut down have done something valuable, and it's fair to recognize it. Strategically, encouraging reduction can reach many people.

But cutting down is a path, not a place to stop. The same reason that led to the reduction applies to what's still on the plate.
