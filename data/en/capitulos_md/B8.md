# B8. "Animal scientists take good care of the animals"

**Thesis:** many animal scientists genuinely care about animals, and much of the welfare improvement of recent decades has passed through their hands. But animal science is the science of raising animals for human use, and the welfare it measures is whatever fits inside that goal. Taking good care of a chicken who will be slaughtered at around six weeks old, at a stocking density that the European food safety authority itself considers harmful, is taking good care within a limit the profession doesn't set and doesn't question. And knowing how to raise animals isn't the same as knowing whether it is just to raise them to be killed.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR · **Note:** this chapter is about technical authority. The argument that economic self-interest guarantees care is B7, and that the law guarantees it is B9

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The people looking after the animals are animal scientists, trained for it. They know far more about animals than some vegan on the internet."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Animal scientists have technical training in animal behaviour, nutrition, health and welfare.
2. **P2.** They work directly on farms and are responsible for the animals' conditions.
3. **P3.** People with training and direct responsibility know better than laypeople what is good for the animals.
4. **C.** So animals raised under professional supervision are well treated, and the vegan critique doesn't stand.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 is true about many technical questions. The problem is that C is about something P1 to P3 don't cover: whether the system itself is acceptable.

The argument is **valid** as to what animal scientists know how to do. Its **soundness** fails at the leap to the conclusion: technical competence within a goal doesn't answer whether the goal is just.

## 3. Answer for children

An animal scientist studies a lot to learn how farm animals grow, eat and stay healthy. Many really like animals. But their job is to make the animals grow so they can become food. Knowing how to look after an animal until the day it will be killed doesn't answer whether it's right to kill it.

## 4. Short answer

Many animal scientists take good care of animals, within what their profession exists to do: raise animals for human use. That is why a "well cared for" broiler chicken is slaughtered at around six weeks, and why the European food safety authority concluded that the stocking densities used today impair the birds' ability to walk and cause foot lesions. Caring within a limit isn't questioning the limit. And knowing how to raise animals isn't knowing whether it's just to raise them to be killed.

**Question back:** "Can an animal scientist recommend that an animal not be slaughtered, if that is better for the animal?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes two questions that have different experts.

| Question | Who can answer it | Does the animal scientist answer it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How to reduce stress in transport, mortality, injuries? | Animal science and veterinary medicine | Yes, and often well |
| What conditions fit a system that has to be profitable? | Animal science, together with economics | Yes, and that is where the limit appears |
| Is it just to raise animals to kill them when we don't need to? | No one by technical authority: it is a moral question | No more than anyone else |

The first row is the argument's real strength. The third is the one it uses to close the conversation.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The profession exists for production.** In Brazil, the law regulating the profession defines the animal scientist's duties as including guiding the raising of domestic animals and promoting their production. There is nothing wrong in saying so: it is the job description. But it means welfare enters as part of production, not above it.

**2. "Well cared for" has limits that technique doesn't set.** The standard broiler chicken is slaughtered at around six weeks old. The scientific assessment by EFSA, the European food safety authority, concluded in 2023 that stocking densities above 11 kilograms per square metre increase foot lesions and reduce the ability to walk, and recommended limiting the growth rate of breeds. European rules allow up to 42 kilograms per square metre. Those conditions are designed and run by competent technical teams, within what is economically viable.

**3. Technical competence isn't moral authority.** A slaughter specialist knows how to slaughter with less suffering; that doesn't make them know whether slaughter is just. An animal scientist knows how to raise animals with less stress; that doesn't make them know whether raising them is justified. These are questions of different kinds, and the second doesn't belong to any profession.

**4. There is a structural conflict of interest.** This isn't about individual bad faith. The profession, its jobs and its training depend on the existence of the system it is being asked to assess. It would be odd to expect it to conclude against itself, which is why no technical field is the final judge of its own activity.

**5. Even perfect care doesn't resolve the death.** If every animal scientist achieved the best possible conditions, the animal would still be killed long before the end of its natural life, without necessity. The argument answers the question of how the animal lives and sets aside the question of whether it should die (B2).

**6. Many improvements came from outside the profession.** Bans on battery cages, stocking-density limits and environmental-enrichment requirements have often resulted from public pressure, campaigns and legislation. The profession applies the rules, but it isn't always the one asking for them, because the cost falls on whoever employs it.

**7. Some animal scientists agree with the critique.** The field has welfare researchers who argue for deep change, and professionals who left production for ethical reasons. The argument presents the profession as a single voice, and it isn't one.

### (d) The best reply: better with them than without them

The serious version doesn't say all is well. It says that, while farming exists, it is better run by professionals who understand welfare than by people who don't, and that the vegan critique sometimes overlooks this real work.

### (e) Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It concedes the thesis.** "Better with them than without them" is true and says nothing about whether farming is acceptable.
2. **It is a reason to reform, not to end the critique.** It values the work of reducing suffering, which anyone defending animals supports.
3. **It swaps the question.** It moves from "is this just?" to "can this be done with less harm?", and only the second has a technical answer.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it shows up in B8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to authority outside its field | Uses technical competence to settle a moral question |
| Logical | Overgeneralisation | Takes the good professional as a guarantee for the whole system |
| Epistemic | Ignored conflict of interest | Treats as neutral an assessment made from inside the activity |
| Moral | Swapped question | Answers how the animal lives and stays silent about whether it should die |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **It accepts that improvements came from outside pressure.** Banned cages, limited densities — which shows the previous standard was also under technical supervision.
2. **It doesn't accept "the doctor said so" as the end of any health conversation.** It seeks a second opinion, reads, asks questions.
3. **It doesn't apply the reasoning to other professions.** Nobody thinks the existence of safety engineers makes any construction project acceptable.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it props up B8 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority bias | Technical training closes the discussion before it starts | Literature on obedience and authority |
| The meat paradox | The idea that someone competent is looking after the animals eases the conflict between liking them and eating them | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| Motivated mind denial | Seeing the animal as a production unit makes it natural to measure welfare by yield | [Bastian et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211424291) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle under test is: "if competent professionals run an activity, the activity is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Animal testing | It would be acceptable for being done by scientists | That is exactly what is discussed in I1 |
| Rodeos | They would be acceptable for having vets on call | Technical care doesn't decide whether the practice is just |
| Any long-standing practice | Having specialists would be enough to end the critique | No reform would ever have happened |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets right something that deserves acknowledgement: many animal scientists care about animals, and much of the concrete welfare improvement has passed through their work. It also gets right that criticism without technical knowledge makes mistakes, and that those who defend animals should know what they criticise.

But competence within a goal doesn't answer whether the goal is just. Animal science knows how to raise animals with less suffering; it cannot, by being animal science, decide whether it is right to raise them to be killed. That question belongs to everyone.
