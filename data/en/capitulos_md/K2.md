# K2. "The animal is already dead"

**Thesis:** the animal on the shelf is already dead, but the purchase doesn't end with them: it signals to the market that stock needs replacing, and the replacement is the next death. A single purchase rarely decides on its own whether one more animal will be raised, but the average effect of each purchase is real (K1). No one buys the past; the purchase counts toward the next order.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's already in the store; it'll go to waste. I wasn't the one who killed it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** The animal whose meat is on the shelf has already died.
2. **P2.** Nothing I do now can bring them back.
3. **P3.** If I don't buy it, the meat will be wasted.
4. **C.** So buying that meat causes no harm.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: the market responds to purchases, and today's purchase influences tomorrow's production.

The argument is **valid** if the purchase didn't affect production. Its **soundness** fails because it does.

## 3. Answer for children

When you buy a packet of cookies, the store sees it was sold and orders another from the factory. Meat is the same: when someone buys it, the store orders more, and another animal is raised and killed to replace it. So the purchase isn't about the animal who already died, but about the next one.

## 4. Short answer

The animal on the shelf has already died, but the purchase signals to the market that stock needs replacing. The replacement is the next death. The store doesn't ask whether the animal was already dead; it counts how many products were sold and orders more. Each purchase counts toward the next order, not toward the animal who already died.

**Question to ask back:** "If no one bought the meat on the shelf, would the store order the same amount next week?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument looks only at the product in front of it, not at the cycle:

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Purchase | The product leaves the shelf |
| Stock | The store records the sale |
| Order | The store orders more from the supplier |
| Production | The slaughterhouse kills more animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The purchase funds the replacement.** The money and the record of the sale move the production chain.

**2. The market responds to demand.** Although each individual purchase has a small and uncertain effect, the expected effect is real (K1).

**3. The argument would apply to any product.** Anything on the shelf "has already been made", and buying it still influences future production.

**4. "I wasn't the one who killed it" is outsourced morality.** Paying for the act is taking part in it (K3).

**5. The leftovers case is different.** Eating something that would be thrown away, without creating demand, is a rare and distinct case (F10).

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)](https://campuspress.yale.edu/shellykagan/papers) | Each purchase has a real expected effect on production, even in chains with thresholds; the central example is buying chicken | Also used in K1 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the supply chain absorbs small variations

The most serious version points out that stocks, waste and long-term contracts can absorb small variations in demand, and that a single purchase may affect nothing.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The expected effect is still positive.** Even with absorption, there's a chance the purchase affects production (K1).
2. **The sum of purchases matters.** The market responds to the total, which is made of individual purchases.
3. **Responsibility doesn't depend only on effect.** Taking part in a harmful practice counts too.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The animal on the shelf can't be saved, but the next one can. The moral question isn't about the past; it's about what today's purchase brings about tomorrow.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epistemological | Not knowing how the market works | Ignores that the purchase triggers replacement |
| Logical | Focus on the past | Considers only the animal already dead, not the next one |
| Moral | Outsourced morality | Assigns responsibility only to whoever kills |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Knows stores replace what they sell.** For any other product.
2. **Boycotts products for other reasons.** Which shows they believe purchases have an effect.
3. **Wouldn't say the same about products of slave labor.** "It's already been made" wouldn't convince them.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the product already exists, buying it causes no harm".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Products of slave labor | Buying them would be fine | No one accepts this |
| Ivory | Buying pieces already made wouldn't encourage poaching | That's why the trade was banned |
| Meat | Buying it wouldn't cause harm | The store replaces its stock |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that the animal on the shelf can't be saved, and that a single purchase has a small and uncertain effect. In rare cases, such as leftovers that would be thrown away, the situation is different.

But the market works by replacement. Today's purchase is tomorrow's order, and the moral question is about the next animal.
