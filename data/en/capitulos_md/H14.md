# H14. "Eating insects is the sustainable protein of the future"

**Thesis:** farming insects may emit less and use less land than livestock farming, but it trades an environmental problem for an unprecedented number of victims. Because they're small, trillions of insects are needed to produce what a few large animals provide, and there is growing evidence that many insects are sentient. There are plant proteins just as efficient, with no one to suffer.

**Family:** H. Environment, ecology and agriculture · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Cricket flour is the future. Lots of protein, little land, almost no emissions. And insects don't feel anything."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Livestock farming is one of the main causes of emissions, deforestation and water use.
2. **P2.** Insects convert feed into protein much more efficiently than cattle or pigs, with fewer emissions and less land.
3. **P3.** Insects probably don't feel pain, or feel very little.
4. **C.** So replacing meat with insects is a sustainable and ethically acceptable solution.

P1 is true, and P2 has some support: in 2013 the FAO highlighted the feed efficiency of edible insects.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which runs against growing evidence, and the conclusion ignores plant alternatives that meet P2 without victims.

## 3. Answer for children

A cricket is really small. To make a cricket burger, you need lots and lots of crickets. Scientists are finding out that insects learn, remember things and might feel pain. So if we swap cows for crickets, we may not be helping animals: we might be making many more animals suffer. And beans, lentils and chickpeas have lots of protein too.

## 4. Short answer

Sustainable for whom? For the insects, it means trillions of deaths a year, counting those farmed for food and feed. And there's more and more evidence that many of them feel. If we want protein with little land and few emissions, beans, soy, lentils and chickpeas already do that with no one to suffer. Reducing environmental impact doesn't justify increasing the number of victims.

**Question to ask back:** "If there's a protein as sustainable as cricket protein with no victims at all, why choose the cricket?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Premise | What it claims | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | Livestock farming degrades the environment | True |
| P2 | Insects are more efficient than cattle | Partly: depends on the feed and the species |
| P3 | Insects don't feel | Contrary to growing evidence |
| Alternative ignored | Plant proteins | As efficient or more, without victims |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. There is evidence of sentience in insects.** Insects show flexible behavior, learning and integration of information. They have centralized brains with specialized structures, the mushroom bodies, with hundreds of thousands of neurons. That's enough, at the very least, to give them the benefit of the doubt, as we already do with octopuses and crustaceans (A6).

**2. The number of victims explodes.** According to estimates cited in *A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, between 2 and 3.2 trillion insects farmed for food and feed die each year, plus trillions of cochineal insects for dye and hundreds of billions of silkworms. Since each insect is tiny, substitution multiplies the victims.

**3. The killing isn't gentle.** The usual methods include freezing, immersion in water above 80 °C, microwaves and shredding, after 12 to 24 hours without food.

**4. A smaller brain doesn't guarantee less suffering.** In animals with fewer cognitive resources, pain may be the main driver of behavior, and would therefore need to be intense (A22).

**5. The plant alternative already exists.** Legumes meet the environmental goal without breeding anyone to die.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: even if they feel, they count far less

The most serious version grants that insects may feel, but says each one counts far less than a cow or a pig, because its mental life is much simpler. So swapping one cow for thousands of crickets would still be a moral gain.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Counting less doesn't license exploitation.** Even with a lower weight, another argument would be needed to justify breeding and killing.
2. **Scale cancels the discount.** If a human counted a thousand times more than an insect, a thousand insects would even it out, and the real numbers are in the trillions.
3. **The right comparison isn't with the cow.** It's with beans. Against an alternative without victims, any number of victims loses.

### (f) Back to the moral question

This chapter shows the risk of defending veganism on environmental grounds: insect farming can be great for the climate and terrible for animals. The question that matters isn't how much land a protein uses, but whether it's fair to breed sentient beings to die when we don't need to.

| Moral | Swapping the criterion | Environmental impact replaces the question about victims |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they want to reduce harm.** And multiplies the number of animals killed.
2. **Accepts that octopuses and crabs feel.** On the basis of evidence similar to what exists for insects.
3. **Champions efficiency.** And ignores legumes, which are more efficient than any farming of animals.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if it reduces environmental impact, it's acceptable, no matter how many die".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Sustainable" hunting | Killing wild animals would be acceptable if it reduced emissions | Disputed by animal advocates |
| "Sustainable" leather | Exploiting animals would be acceptable with a smaller footprint | The same logic |
| Insects | Farming trillions to eat would be acceptable | This is the argument's conclusion |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Livestock farming causes enormous environmental harm, and looking for alternatives is necessary. It's also true that there is real uncertainty about the sentience of many insect species.

But uncertainty calls for caution, not license. And the alternative without victims is already on many people's plates.
