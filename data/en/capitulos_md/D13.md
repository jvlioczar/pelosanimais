# D13. "Animal sacrifice in religious rituals is freedom of worship"

**Thesis:** religious freedom is a fundamental right, and in Brazil the Supreme Federal Court ruled in 2019 that a law allowing ritual sacrifice in religions of African origin is constitutional. But what the law allows doesn't answer the moral question, which is the same for every animal killed: is it fair to take the life of someone who doesn't need to die? That question applies equally to religious sacrifice and to commercial slaughter, which kills billions of animals a year. Attacking only the rituals of Afro-Brazilian religions, and not the rest, is inconsistent and is often religious racism.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's my religion. Sacrifice has been part of our worship for centuries, and the meat is eaten by the community. No one has the right to ban it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Freedom of worship is a fundamental right, protected by the Constitution.
2. **P2.** Animal sacrifice is an essential part of some forms of worship, and the meat is usually eaten by the community.
3. **P3.** Society accepts killing billions of animals for food without any religious reason.
4. **C.** So religious sacrifice is legitimate, and criticizing it is persecuting a religion.

P1 is true. P2 is true for several traditions. P3 is true and is the argument's strongest point. The conclusion, however, mixes two questions: whether the state should ban the rite, and whether killing the animal is morally justified. P3 shows that selective criticism is inconsistent, not that killing is fair.

The argument is **valid** on the legal question. On the moral question it **isn't sound**: freedom of belief doesn't settle whether the animal has an interest in staying alive.

## 3. Answer for children

Every family and every religion has its own celebrations and ways of praying, and that deserves respect. But the animal that is going to be killed also wanted to go on living, whether in a ritual or in a slaughterhouse. That's why many people, from many religions, prefer to offer flowers, fruit or food made without animals.

## 4. Short answer

Religious freedom is a right, and Brazil's Supreme Court ruled in 2019 that ritual sacrifice in religions of African origin is allowed. But the moral question is the same for any animal killed: it wanted to go on living. That question applies to the temple and to the slaughterhouse, which kills far more animals. Someone who attacks only Afro-Brazilian rituals and eats meat every day is being inconsistent. Someone who opposes all unnecessary killing can, respectfully, also invite religious traditions to consider bloodless offerings, as many communities already do.

**Question to ask back:** "If death in the slaughterhouse is a problem for the animal, why would death in a ritual be different for them?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument joins two questions that need to be separated:

| Question | Answer | Who decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can the state ban the rite? | In Brazil, no: the Supreme Court upheld the law that allows it (RE 494601, 2019) | The law |
| Is criticism aimed only at Afro-Brazilian religions consistent? | No: it ignores commercial slaughter, which is far larger | Consistency |
| Is killing the animal morally justified? | The question is the same as for ordinary slaughter | Ethics |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. What is allowed isn't automatically fair.** The Supreme Court's decision protects religious freedom against the state. It doesn't say the animal doesn't suffer or has no interest in living; the law also allows commercial slaughter, and that doesn't make it fair (see D9).

**2. Selective criticism is where the argument is right.** Religious sacrifice is a tiny fraction of animal deaths. Attacking only rites of African origin, while accepting industrial slaughter, treats the same death differently depending on the religion of whoever kills. Black movement organizations and organizations of religions of African origin rightly point out that this selectivity is religious racism.

**3. The vegan objection isn't selective.** Veganism calls for an end to all avoidable killing and exploitation of animals, in slaughter, fishing, rodeos and also in the rites of any religion. It doesn't ask the state to persecute a specific faith; it invites each person, from any tradition, to think about the animal.

**4. Traditions themselves change.** Many Umbanda houses don't sacrifice animals, and in Judaism the kaparot rite, done with chickens, is practiced by many with donated money instead of the animal. This shows the religious meaning can be kept without the killing, when the community so decides.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: criticizing the rite disrespects a persecuted faith

The most serious version recalls that religions of African origin have been persecuted in Brazil for centuries, that their temples are still attacked, and that criticism of sacrifice often serves as a pretext for that persecution.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's true about the persecution.** Criticism aimed only at these religions should be rejected, and respect for the people and their temples comes before any debate.
2. **But the animal doesn't change with the religion.** The question about the animal's death applies to everyone, including those who eat meat without any rite.
3. **Respecting a faith doesn't require agreeing with all its practices.** Members of these traditions themselves discuss and change practices; respectful dialogue is part of religious life.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Confusing the legal with the moral | "The Supreme Court allowed it, so it's fair" |
| Moral | Appeal to tradition | The age of the rite settles the question |
| Moral (on the other side) | Selective outrage | Condemning only the Afro-Brazilian rite and accepting the slaughterhouse |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Those who criticize only the rite.** And eat meat from animals slaughtered for no religious reason.
2. **Those who defend the rite because the meat is eaten.** And accept the same argument when it comes from the slaughterhouse.
3. **Those who recognize that the animal feels fear and pain.** And think the killer's motive changes what the animal feels.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains D13 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Religious prejudice | Makes someone else's rite seem crueler than ordinary slaughter | Literature on religious racism in Brazil |
| Familiarity | Invisible slaughter seems normal; a visible rite seems strange | General literature on normalization |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the practice is religious, killing the animal is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any killing with a religious motive | It would be justified | The faithful themselves disagree in many cases |
| Commercial slaughter with no religious motive | It would be less justified than the rite | Those who use the argument rarely accept that conclusion |
| Offerings without animals | They'd have the same religious value, according to many communities | Shows that killing isn't indispensable for everyone |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that religious freedom is a right, that the Supreme Court protected it, that religions of African origin suffer persecution, and that selective criticism of sacrifice is inconsistent and often racist.

But the question about the animal remains: they wanted to live, in the rite or in the slaughterhouse. The consistent answer is to reject all avoidable killing, without targeting any particular faith.
