# D13. "Animal sacrifice in religious rituals is freedom of worship"

**Thesis:** religious freedom is a fundamental right. In Brazil, the Supreme Federal Court ruled in 2019 that a law allowing ritual sacrifice in religions of African origin is constitutional; in the United States, the Supreme Court struck down in 1993 city ordinances designed to ban Santería sacrifices. But what the law allows doesn't answer the moral question, which is the same for every animal killed: is it fair to take the life of someone who doesn't need to die? That question applies equally to religious sacrifice and to commercial slaughter, which kills billions of animals a year. Attacking only one religion's rituals, such as those of Afro-Brazilian religions, and not the rest, is inconsistent and can be prejudice: religious racism, xenophobia or intolerance toward minorities, something that happens in Brazil and in other countries.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's my religion. Sacrifice has been part of our worship for centuries, and the meat is eaten by the community. No one has the right to ban it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Freedom of worship is a fundamental right, protected by the Constitution.
2. **P2.** Animal sacrifice is an essential part of some forms of worship, and the meat is usually eaten by the community.
3. **P3.** Society accepts killing billions of animals for food without any religious reason.
4. **C.** So religious sacrifice is legitimate, and criticizing it is persecuting a religion.

P1 is true. P2 is true for several traditions. P3 is true and is the argument's strongest point. The conclusion, however, mixes two questions: whether the state should ban the rite, and whether killing the animal is morally justified. P3 shows that selective criticism is inconsistent, not that killing is fair.

The argument is **valid** on the legal question. On the moral question it **isn't sound**: freedom of belief doesn't settle whether the animal has an interest in staying alive.

## 3. Answer for children

Every family and every religion has its own celebrations and ways of praying, and that deserves respect. But the animal that is going to be killed also wanted to go on living, whether in a ritual or in a slaughterhouse. That's why many people, from many religions, prefer to offer flowers, fruit or food made without animals.

## 4. Short answer

You're right that religious freedom is a right, and Brazil's Supreme Court ruled in 2019 that ritual sacrifice in religions of African origin is allowed. But think about it from the animal's point of view: the moral question is the same for any killing, because they wanted to go on living. That question applies to the temple and to the slaughterhouse, which kills far more animals. Someone who attacks only one religion's rituals, such as Afro-Brazilian ones in Brazil, and eats meat every day is being inconsistent, and that selective criticism can be religious, racial or anti-foreigner prejudice, as has also been seen in the United States and Europe. I oppose all unnecessary killing, and that's why I can, respectfully, also invite religious traditions to consider bloodless offerings, as many communities already do.

**Question to ask back:** "If death in the slaughterhouse is a problem for the animal, why would death in a ritual be different for them?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument joins two questions that need to be separated:

| Question | Answer | Who decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can the state ban the rite? | In Brazil, no: the Supreme Court upheld the law that allows it (RE 494601, 2019) | The law |
| Is criticism aimed only at one religion consistent? | No: it ignores commercial slaughter, which is far larger, and can be prejudice | Consistency |
| Is killing the animal morally justified? | The question is the same as for ordinary slaughter | Ethics |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. What is allowed isn't automatically fair.** The Supreme Court's decision protects religious freedom against the state. It doesn't say the animal doesn't suffer or has no interest in living; the law also allows commercial slaughter, and that doesn't make it fair (see D9).

**2. Selective criticism is where the argument is right.** Religious sacrifice is a tiny fraction of animal deaths. Attacking only rites of African origin, while accepting industrial slaughter, treats the same death differently depending on the religion of whoever kills. Black movement organizations and organizations of religions of African origin rightly point out that this selectivity is religious racism, a term also used by the Brazilian government, which keeps a guide for reporting it.

**3. This doesn't only happen in Brazil.** In 1987, the city of Hialeah, Florida (United States), passed ordinances against ritual sacrifice shortly after a church of Santería, a religion of Afro-Cuban origin, announced it would open there. The ordinances exempted kosher slaughter and left other ways of killing animals untouched. In 1993, the US Supreme Court unanimously struck them down (*Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah*) for having been designed to target a specific religion. In Europe, the Belgian regions of Flanders and Wallonia began requiring stunning before all slaughter from 2019, which affects traditional kosher and halal slaughter. The Court of Justice of the European Union (2020) and the European Court of Human Rights (2024) found the rule valid and non-discriminatory; the European Jewish Congress called the 2020 ruling "a heavy blow to Jewish life in Europe", and Jewish and Muslim groups feared it could feed hostility toward immigrants. The Belgian case differs from Hialeah, since the rule applies to all slaughter and rests on animal welfare grounds, and the debate over it continues. But both show the same risk: when unease about killing animals appears only in the face of a minority's rite, and not in the face of billions of ordinary slaughters, it can become a vehicle for prejudice.

**4. The vegan objection isn't selective.** Veganism calls for an end to all avoidable killing and exploitation of animals, in slaughter, fishing, rodeos and also in the rites of any religion. It doesn't ask the state to persecute a specific faith; it invites each person, from any tradition, to think about the animal.

**5. Traditions themselves change.** Many Umbanda houses don't sacrifice animals, and in Judaism the kaparot rite, done with chickens, is practiced by many with donated money instead of the animal. This shows the religious meaning can be kept without the killing, when the community so decides. History also shows how far the argument from tradition has gone. In the Inca Empire, in the 15th and 16th centuries, children aged 6 to 14 were sacrificed high in the Andes in the capacocha ritual, believed to win the favor of the gods in times of disaster or disease; a toxicological study of mummies published in 2026 showed that they were given cocaine, alcohol and ayahuasca before their deaths (Folha de S.Paulo/The New York Times, 2026). It was a central religious practice, and today nobody defends it in the name of freedom of worship. The comparison does not equate the two practices or the religions involved: it only shows that being part of a sacred rite is not, by itself, enough to justify killing someone, and that the question of who is killed still stands.

**6. The debate over stunning for all has arguments on both sides.** In countries that require animals to be stunned before all slaughter, the discussion takes a particular form worth knowing. On the side of a universal rule, the philosopher Brian Barry, in *Culture and Equality* (2001), and the political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane (2010, pp. 89–90) argue that requiring everyone to stun doesn't prevent anyone from practicing their religion: kosher and halal rules say how meat must be obtained, but don't oblige anyone to eat meat. They also argue that a law that applies to everyone doesn't discriminate; it imposes unequal burdens, as almost every law does, and those asking for an exemption must show why their burden is special. The philosopher Paula Casal (2003) adds that the hypocrisy of allowing intensive farming doesn't justify creating new exemptions, and that demanding full consistency before any reform would block all gradual change. On the other side, there are serious objections. For many Muslims, the sacrifice at the festival of Eid al-Adha is itself the religious act, not just a way of obtaining meat: the Hanafi school considers it obligatory for those with the means, and the other Sunni schools strongly recommended. Jewish and Muslim organizations also point out that in practice the rule falls only on them, and Poland's Constitutional Court struck down a ban on slaughter without stunning in 2014 in the name of religious freedom. And Cochrane himself adds a caveat that fits point 2: for change to be truly gradual, the state has to go on to tackle intensive farming, which causes far more suffering; until it does, minorities are right to point out the selectivity (2010, p. 91). Today, according to a survey by Eurogroup for Animals (2021), Sweden, Norway, Iceland, Denmark (since 2014), Slovenia and Switzerland require stunning before all slaughter, as do Flanders and Wallonia, in Belgium, since 2019; Austria, Estonia, Latvia and Slovakia require stunning immediately after the cut. In other countries, such as the United Kingdom, Germany, France, the United States and Brazil, the religious exemption remains. In Denmark, meat from animals slaughtered without stunning can still be imported, which shows the limits of these rules. For ethics, the consistent conclusion remains that of point 4: the question about the animal's death applies to all slaughter, with or without a rite.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: criticizing the rite disrespects a persecuted faith

The most serious version recalls that religions of African origin have been persecuted in Brazil for centuries, that their temples are still attacked, and that criticism of sacrifice often serves as a pretext for that persecution. The same holds, in other countries, for religious minorities and immigrants whose rites are treated as foreign.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's true about the persecution.** Criticism aimed only at these religions should be rejected, and respect for the people and their temples comes before any debate.
2. **But the animal doesn't change with the religion.** The question about the animal's death applies to everyone, including those who eat meat without any rite.
3. **Respecting a faith doesn't require agreeing with all its practices.** Members of these traditions themselves discuss and change practices; respectful dialogue is part of religious life.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Those who criticize only the rite.** And eat meat from animals slaughtered for no religious reason.
2. **Those who defend the rite because the meat is eaten.** And accept the same argument when it comes from the slaughterhouse.
3. **Those who recognize that the animal feels fear and pain.** And think the killer's motive changes what the animal feels.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the practice is religious, killing the animal is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any killing with a religious motive | It would be justified | The faithful themselves disagree in many cases |
| Commercial slaughter with no religious motive | It would be less justified than the rite | Those who use the argument rarely accept that conclusion |
| Offerings without animals | They'd have the same religious value, according to many communities | Shows that killing isn't indispensable for everyone |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that religious freedom is a right, that courts such as Brazil's Supreme Federal Court and the US Supreme Court have protected it, that religions of African origin and other religious minorities suffer persecution, and that selective criticism of sacrifice is inconsistent and often prejudiced.

But the question about the animal remains: they wanted to live, in the rite or in the slaughterhouse. The consistent answer is to reject all avoidable killing, without targeting any particular faith.
