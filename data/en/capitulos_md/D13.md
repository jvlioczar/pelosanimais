# D13. "I don't want to dishonor my parents and my ancestors"

**Thesis:** honoring someone doesn't require repeating everything that person did. Every generation revises the practices of earlier ones, and our ancestors acted with the knowledge and the options they had. Judging a practice isn't condemning the people who followed it. Many people honor their family precisely by keeping the best of it (the care, the full table, grandma's recipes) and changing only the ingredient that costs someone's life.

**Family:** D. Tradition, culture and religion · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "My family has always lived this way. Turning down my mother's food is disrespectful. Who am I to say my grandparents were wrong?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We owe gratitude and respect to those who raised us and to those who came before us.
2. **P2.** Food is one of the main ways a family passes on care, memory and identity.
3. **P3.** Refusing that food, or saying the family's practice is wrong, dishonors those people.
4. **C.** So, out of respect, I should keep eating the way my family eats.

The argument joins two motives: respect for ancestors and the fear of hurting living parents and relatives. P1 and P2 are true and deserve to be taken seriously. The problem lies in P3, which confuses honoring people with repeating their acts, and disagreeing with a practice with despising those who follow it.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Loving grandma very much doesn't mean doing everything exactly as she did. Grandma may not have worn a seat belt in the car, because long ago almost no one did. Today we wear one, and that isn't disrespectful to her. You can love your family, eat together, learn the recipes and, at the same time, choose not to hurt animals.

## 4. Short answer

Honoring my parents and grandparents means recognizing what they did for me, looking after them and carrying forward the best of what they taught me. It doesn't mean repeating everything they did. They acted on what they knew and with the options they had, and I don't condemn them for it. Every generation changes something: many grandparents were hit at school and didn't hit their grandchildren, and no one calls that dishonor. I can sit at the same table, cook my grandmother's recipes in a version without animals and still belong to the family.

**Question to ask back:** "Is there anything your grandparents did that you decided not to repeat? Do you feel you dishonored them by that?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The word "honor" is being used in two senses:

| Sense | What it requires | Does veganism prevent it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honoring the people | Gratitude, care, presence, memory, carrying forward their best values | No |
| Repeating the practices | Doing what they did, the way they did it | Only where it involves using animals |

The argument only works if the two senses are the same thing. They aren't: no one thinks honoring your grandparents obliges you to have the same job, the same religion or the same opinions as them.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Honoring isn't imitating.** We honor a person for what they were to us, not by copying each of their habits. A son who cares for his parents in old age and disagrees with them about politics doesn't dishonor them. The same goes for food.

**2. Judging a practice isn't condemning the people.** Saying that today there are good reasons not to eat animals isn't saying our grandparents were bad people. They grew up in a world where almost no one questioned it, often with few options and little information. We assess people by what they could know and do, and practices by what they cause (E14).

**3. Every generation revises the ones before.** Practices handed from parents to children have been abandoned without anyone seeing it as betrayal. Physical punishment of children, for example, was the norm for centuries; Sweden banned it by law in 1979, and dozens of countries have since done the same. Those who decide not to hit their children aren't accusing their own parents: they're using what was learned later (D1).

**4. The human test.** In China, mothers bound their daughters' feet for about a thousand years, convinced they were securing the girls' future. According to the political scientist Gerry Mackie, the practice ended in a single generation, when families pledged together not to bind their daughters' feet. No one says those families dishonored their ancestors. When a family tradition hurts someone, changing it tends to be remembered as a source of pride.

**5. Our ancestors broke with theirs too.** Our grandparents changed towns, jobs, customs and, often, food. Cheap everyday chicken and meat at every meal are habits a few decades old, not a legacy of centuries (D1). Changing is part of what we received from them.

**6. What the table passes on doesn't depend on the animal.** The care is in the person cooking, in the conversation and in the time together. Many people honor their family by adapting grandma's recipes: the same seasoning, the same beans, the same Sunday lunch, with a different filling. Asking for the recipe and cooking it together tends to be received as a tribute, not an offense (D8, M3).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: for my mother, food is love, and refusing it really hurts

The most serious version isn't about philosophy but about actual people. In many families, especially those that have known want, serving meat is a sign of having beaten hunger, and offering the best dish is the way of saying "I love you". Refusing that dish can hurt someone elderly, who won't understand and may not have many lunches ahead. Wouldn't it be kinder to give in?

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The hurt is real and deserves care, but it comes from the manner, not the choice.** Letting people know beforehand, explaining warmly, bringing a dish, praising what is already plant-based on the table and asking to cook together change the reception a great deal (M3).
2. **What the person is offering is care, and care can be accepted.** Accepting the company, the rice, the beans, the adapted dessert and the conversation is accepting what is really being offered.
3. **There is a third interested party.** Between the passing discomfort of a hard conversation and an animal's life, the weights aren't equal. Sparing the feelings of those we love matters, but not at the cost of someone who has no say.
4. **Families adapt.** They have already made room for those who changed religion, job or town. With time and patience, they almost always make room for those who changed their plate.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in D13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "honor" now as respecting people, now as repeating their acts |
| Logical | Appeal to tradition | Treats family custom as proof that it is right |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either I eat what my family eats, or I dishonor them |
| Epistemological | Confusing judging acts with judging people | Takes criticism of a practice as condemnation of those who followed it |
| Moral | Excluding those who pay the price | Weighs only the family's feelings, not what happens to the animal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Has already changed a lot without feeling like a traitor.** Job, town, way of raising children, sometimes religion.
2. **Wouldn't repeat everything the grandparents did.** Few people today defend the cane at school or arranged marriage, which were also family traditions.
3. **Admires the ancestors who broke with custom.** The great-grandfather who emigrated and the grandmother who insisted on studying are remembered with pride.
4. **Accepts that their children will do things differently.** Almost every parent says they want their children to be better than they were.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "doing things differently from your parents and ancestors dishonors them".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical punishment of children | Those who don't hit their children would dishonor parents who did | No one concludes this |
| Footbinding | The Chinese families who abandoned it would have betrayed their ancestors | Today the change is seen as progress |
| Women's education and work | The first daughter to study would have dishonored the mother who couldn't | She is usually the family's pride |
| Any change of custom | Every generation would dishonor the one before | The principle would make improvement impossible |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that gratitude to those who raised us is a real value, and that veganism can be lived in a way that wounds: refusing a dish with a look of disgust, lecturing at Sunday lunch or treating your parents like criminals really is disrespectful, and helps no animal. It's also right that family food carries memory and love, and that, for those who have known hunger, meat on the table means something that deserves to be understood.

But the concession ends where the victim begins. Honoring parents and ancestors calls for gratitude, care and tact, not for repeating what we now know causes harm. The most common tribute children pay their parents is to go a little further than they did, with what was learned later.
