# N8. "Using medicine and genetics to end suffering is playing God"

**Thesis:** we already use anaesthesia, painkillers, vaccines and antidepressants to reduce natural suffering, and no one calls that playing God. The idea of using biotechnology to reduce the suffering of humans and animals raises serious questions of safety, consent and justice, and they need answers. But "pain is natural" isn't one of them: if it were, anaesthesia would have been a mistake.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Messing with genes so no one suffers? That's playing God. Suffering is part of life."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Pain and suffering are products of evolution and serve important functions.
2. **P2.** Altering the biology of humans and animals to reduce them would have unpredictable consequences.
3. **P3.** Previous attempts to "improve" living beings through genetics, such as eugenics, led to terrible abuses.
4. **C.** So we shouldn't use biotechnology to reduce suffering.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and need to be taken seriously. The problem is the conclusion: the risks call for caution, consent and rules, not a ban on any attempt.

The argument is **invalid** in its strong form: from the risks of a technology it doesn't follow that every use is wrong, as medicine itself shows.

## 3. Answer for children

When you go to the dentist, they use a medicine so you don't feel pain. That's good, isn't it? Long ago that medicine didn't exist, and people suffered a lot. Some people thought it was wrong to invent medicine for pain, but today almost everyone is grateful. Using science carefully so that people and animals suffer less is a bit like that.

## 4. Short answer

Anaesthesia also tampered with something natural, and many people are grateful. Using science to reduce suffering isn't new; it's what medicine has done for centuries. The risks are real, which is why what's defended is careful, voluntary, regulated research, not imposition. What doesn't work as an objection is saying pain is natural.

**Question to ask back:** "Would you refuse anaesthesia for surgery because pain is natural?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument bundles very different objections:

| Objection | Type | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Pain is natural" | Appeal to nature | None: we treat natural pain all the time |
| "It could go wrong" | Risk | Real: calls for research, trials and caution |
| "It's like eugenics" | Historical abuse | Real: calls for consent, freedom and justice |
| "Pain has a function" | Biological | Real: pain protects; eliminating it entirely would be dangerous |

The last three are reasons to do it well, not reasons not to do it.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. We already do it.** Anaesthesia, painkillers, vaccines, antidepressants and treatments for chronic pain are ways of altering biology to reduce suffering. When anaesthesia arrived in childbirth, in the mid-nineteenth century, there were religious objections to it; today almost no one defends them.

**2. Pain can be regulated without being eliminated.** People born unable to feel pain face serious risks, which shows pain protects. But there are rare cases of people with greatly reduced pain and anxiety who live well, such as the Scottish woman studied by researchers in 2019, who carries a variant in the FAAH-OUT gene. The serious proposal is to reduce useless suffering while preserving pain's warning function.

**3. Eugenics is something else.** Historical eugenics was coercive, discriminatory and based on hierarchies of value between people. A voluntary therapy to reduce the pain of those who choose it has none of these features. Past abuse calls for rules and rights, not a ban on all genetic medicine.

**4. The human test.** If there were a safe, voluntary treatment that reduced the chronic pain of millions of people, no one would refuse it by saying "it's playing God". Refusing the same logic for animals, whose suffering is immense and who can't seek treatment, is using species as the criterion.

**5. Tools are already being tested.** Gene drive techniques are being studied to reduce populations of malaria-carrying mosquitoes. This shows that genetic interventions in wild populations are already the subject of serious scientific debate, with all the caution they require.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: suffering gives life meaning

The most serious version says: overcoming difficulties, feeling losses and facing pain are part of what makes life meaningful. A life without suffering would be shallow, and a humanity that eliminated pain would lose something valuable.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **No one proposes eliminating all challenges.** The proposal is to reduce involuntary and extreme suffering, such as chronic pain, severe depression or the starvation of young animals, not to make life challenge-free.
2. **Meaning doesn't depend on extreme pain.** We can find meaning in loving, learning and creating without needing agony.
3. **The argument doesn't apply to animals.** A young animal that starves on its first day gains no meaning from it.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | If pain is natural, it shouldn't be altered |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Equates voluntary therapy with coercive eugenics |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either we accept all suffering, or we eliminate all pain |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts reducing human pain with technology and refuses the same for animals |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Uses anaesthesia and painkillers.** Which alter biology to reduce pain.
2. **Supports vaccines.** Which tamper with the immune system to prevent natural suffering.
3. **Accepts treatments for genetic diseases.** Which are already genetic medicine.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains N8 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appeal to nature | The natural seems sacred or untouchable | Linked to C1 and N2 |
| Status quo bias | The current level of suffering seems normal | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Omission bias | Not acting seems safer than acting | [Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2891%2990011-T) |
| Ambiguity effect | The unknown outcome of a new technology is scarier than the known suffering of today | [Ellsberg, 1961](https://doi.org/10.2307/1884324) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "we shouldn't use science to alter suffering that is natural".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anaesthesia | Surgery should be done without it | No one accepts this |
| Depression | Antidepressants would be wrong | Rejected |
| Genetic diseases | We shouldn't treat them | Contradicts current medicine |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets a lot right: pain has vital functions, biotechnology carries enormous risks, and the history of eugenics shows the danger of using science to impose ideals. Any proposal in this area needs consent, freedom, justice and careful research. It's also legitimate that many people, including vegans, distrust these ideas.

But none of these concerns comes from the idea that pain is natural and therefore untouchable. They call for doing it well, not for not doing it. And if we accept reducing human pain with science, it remains to be explained why animals' pain wouldn't count.
