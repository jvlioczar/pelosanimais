# I17. "Rodeos, bullfights and circuses are culture"

**Thesis:** it's the appeal to tradition (D1) applied to suffering imposed for entertainment. Many countries have already decided that being cultural doesn't put a practice beyond the reach of laws against cruelty: England and Wales banned hunting with dogs in 2004 and wild animals in circuses in 2020; Brazil's Supreme Court banned the *farra do boi*, a bull-chasing festival, and cockfighting, and in 2016 ruled the *vaquejada*, a rodeo in which riders pull bulls down by the tail, unconstitutional for cruelty. Tradition explains why a practice exists; it doesn't justify the suffering in it. A culture can also be cruel, as many traditional practices were and are, and unnecessary suffering isn't justified by being cultural.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Rodeo is cultural heritage. They want to end the tradition. The bull doesn't even feel it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Rodeos, bullfights and other festivals with animals are part of many communities' identity.
2. **P2.** These practices are recognized as cultural heritage.
3. **P3.** Banning them would destroy part of the culture and harm those who make a living from them.
4. **C.** So these practices should be kept.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 has a point about economic impact. The problem lies in the conclusion: cultural value doesn't authorize the suffering imposed on the animals.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because cultural value doesn't answer the question of cruelty.

## 3. Answer for children

At some festivals, people pull bulls down by the tail, fight bulls in an arena, or ride animals that buck out of fear and pain. This is called tradition. But hurting animals for fun doesn't become right just because it has gone on for a long time. Many cruel things used to be traditions and stopped being so. Festivals can carry on with music, food and dancing, without hurting anyone.

## 4. Short answer

Being a tradition doesn't put a practice beyond laws against cruelty. A culture can be cruel, as many past and present practices are, and unnecessary suffering isn't justified by being cultural. England and Wales banned hunting with dogs in 2004 and wild animals in circuses in 2020. Brazil's Supreme Court banned the *farra do boi* and cockfighting, and in 2016 ruled the *vaquejada* unconstitutional for cruelty; a constitutional amendment then tried to protect it. Festivals can keep their music, food and gathering, without the animals' suffering.

**Question to ask back:** "If other traditions involving animals have been banned for cruelty, why should this one be different?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses cultural value to close the discussion about cruelty:

| Practice | What happens to the animal | Legal situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hunting with dogs (England and Wales) | Foxes and other mammals chased and killed by dogs | Banned by the Hunting Act 2004 |
| Wild animals in circuses (England) | Confinement, transport and training for performances | Banned since 2020 |
| Bullfighting (Spain) | The bull is wounded and usually killed in the arena | Banned in Catalonia in 2010; the ban was overturned by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2016 |
| *Farra do boi* and cockfighting (Brazil) | Bulls chased and beaten; birds fighting to injury or death | Banned |
| *Vaquejada* (Brazil) | The bull is pulled down by the tail | Ruled unconstitutional in 2016; later protected by Constitutional Amendment 96 |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the appeal to tradition.** The age of a practice says nothing about whether it's just (D1).

**2. A culture can be cruel.** Being part of a culture explains why a practice exists and why people are attached to it, but it doesn't show that it's good. Many cultural practices, past and present, impose suffering. Setting dogs on chained animals, as in bear-baiting and bull-baiting, was popular entertainment in England for centuries, until it was banned by law in 1835. Among humans, binding girls' feet in China was a custom for nearly a thousand years, a mark of status and beauty, until it was banned in 1912. Today almost everyone recognizes these practices as cruel, and recognizing this isn't contempt for the cultures in which they existed. If the suffering is unnecessary, being cultural doesn't justify it; at most, it explains why it's hard to give up.

**3. The law has already recognized the cruelty.** Parliaments and courts in several countries have banned traditional practices with animals because of the suffering involved. Where they were later protected, as with the *vaquejada* in Brazil or bullfighting in Spain, the reason given was culture, not the absence of suffering; Brazil's amendment even made the practice conditional on animal welfare.

**4. "The bull doesn't even feel it" is false.** Cattle are sentient, and being pulled down by the tail can cause fractures, dislocations and serious injuries. In bullfighting, wounding the bull is part of the spectacle.

**5. Cultures change.** Many traditional festivals keep their value without using animals, and many places have already replaced cruel practices.

**6. The suffering is imposed for entertainment.** Unlike food, here there isn't even a claim of necessity.

**7. There can be harm even without visible pain.** The defense often says animals in the arena or the circus don't suffer. On the philosopher Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach, that doesn't settle the matter: a bear dressed in human clothes and trained to perform tricks would, even without suffering, be prevented from living as a bear. The example comes from Suzanne Cataldi (2002) and is discussed by the political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane (2010, pp. 42–43), who also points out the approach's difficulties, such as deciding which capabilities deserve to be promoted (pp. 43–44). See I20.

**8. Entertainment weighs little against suffering.** Peter Singer places bullfights and circuses alongside zoos, hunting and the fur trade among the practices to which the same principles he uses for food and experiments apply (*Practical Ethics*, ch. 3). By the principle of equal consideration of interests, the suffering of the bull or the circus animal must be weighed like that of any being who feels. On the other side of the scale is an interest in entertainment, which can be met in other ways (point 6).

**9. The four questions about ends and means.** The philosopher Nathan Nobis proposes asking, about any use of animals justified by an end: is the end legitimate? Is using animals an effective means to it? Are there better means, without harm? And what are the best reasons to think that this end justifies a harm we would never accept imposing on humans? (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, pp. 70–71; see I12). Here the stated end is entertainment, or preserving a tradition. Entertainment is already a debatable end when it costs someone suffering, as Nobis himself notes. For both ends there are means without harm: the festival, the music, the riders' skill and the traditional food carry on without the animal thrown down or injured, and the Ringling circus, which closed in 2017 in the United States and returned in 2023 without animals, shows that the show survives (point 5). And no one would accept hurting a person to entertain an audience, which is the standard the fourth question asks to be justified.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: communities live off these festivals

The most serious version points out that many communities depend economically on these festivals, and that banning them without alternatives would harm real people.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Economic impact calls for a transition, not preservation.** Festivals can transform and keep their audience.
2. **Economics doesn't justify cruelty.** The same argument would defend dogfighting.
3. **There are examples of change.** Festivals that replaced cruel practices and kept drawing crowds.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Here there isn't even a claim of necessity: the suffering is imposed for entertainment. The moral question becomes even clearer: does the pleasure of a spectacle justify hurting and frightening animals?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts the bans on other traditions.** Such as dogfighting or cockfighting.
2. **Wouldn't accept the same practice with dogs.** The criterion is species.
3. **Recognizes welfare rules.** Which admits there's a risk of suffering.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a practice is cultural, the animals' suffering in it is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hunting with dogs | It should have continued | Banned in England and Wales |
| Dogfighting and cockfighting | They should continue | Banned in many countries |
| Bear-baiting | It should have continued | Banned in Britain since 1835 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Traditional festivals have real cultural and social value, and many communities identify deeply with them. Changes imposed without dialogue can breed resentment and harm people who make a living from these festivals.

But the value of a festival lies in the gathering, the music, the food and the history, not in the animals' suffering. Traditions can change and still be traditions.
