# E3. "Morality is relative"

**Thesis:** people who say morality is relative almost never live that way. No one thinks slavery was right "for those who practiced it", or that a mugging is wrong only "in the victim's opinion". Relativism tends to appear selectively, when criticism reaches one's own habit. And saying "that's your truth" confuses taste, which is personal, with harm to another, which isn't.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this chapter also answers "Everyone has their own truth", which was merged into it.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Morality is a social construct. Right and wrong depend on who's looking. That's your truth; I have mine."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Cultures and people disagree deeply about moral questions.
2. **P2.** There's no method everyone accepts for resolving these disagreements.
3. **P3.** If disagreements can't be resolved, there's no objective moral truth.
4. **C.** So condemning the eating of animals is just one opinion among others.

P1 and P2 are largely true. The problem lies in P3: disagreement doesn't prove there's no truth, and anyone who accepts P3 would have to accept that nothing is really wrong, not even what they themselves condemn.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, and the conclusion is applied selectively.

## 3. Answer for children

Liking chocolate or strawberry ice cream is a matter of taste: everyone picks their own. But hurting someone isn't a matter of taste. If someone pushes you, it doesn't help for them to say "in my opinion, that didn't hurt". When someone suffers, it isn't just an opinion.

## 4. Short answer

Almost no one really believes morality is relative. You wouldn't think a mugging is wrong only "in the opinion" of the person mugged, or that slavery was right for those who practiced it. Relativism shows up when criticism reaches one's own habit. And there's a difference between taste and harm: preferring chocolate ice cream is taste; making someone suffer isn't.

**Question to ask back:** "Is there anything you think is really wrong, and not just in your opinion?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument mixes three different ideas:

| Idea | Is it true? |
| --- | --- |
| People disagree about morality | Yes, it's a fact |
| Some questions are a matter of personal taste | Yes: favorite food, style, music |
| Every moral question is just opinion | Doesn't follow from the above, and almost no one accepts it |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's self-refuting.** "There's no moral truth" or "no one should impose their morality" are claims meant to hold for everyone, which contradicts relativism itself.

**2. It's usually applied selectively.** Those who use the argument against veganism rarely use it against theft, racism or violence against children. If relativism were taken seriously, those condemnations would also be "just opinion".

**3. Disagreement isn't the absence of truth.** People disagreed about the shape of the Earth and about slavery. The existence of disagreement doesn't show that all positions are equally valid.

**4. Taste and harm to others are different things.** Matters of taste affect only the one choosing. When there's a victim, the choice stops being merely personal (E1).

**5. Relativism would rule out moral progress.** If there's no right and wrong, the abolition of slavery wasn't progress, just a change of opinion. Almost no one accepts that.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Rachels, *The Elements of Moral Philosophy*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Moral_Philosophy) | Presents and criticizes cultural relativism: disagreement doesn't prove there's no moral truth | McGraw-Hill; chapter on the challenge of cultural relativism |
| [PhilPapers Survey 2020](https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/all) | Most philosophers accept or lean toward moral realism | Shows relativism is a minority position among experts |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: sophisticated anti-realism

The most serious version isn't the relativism of "everyone has their own truth", but philosophical anti-realist theories that say morality depends on human attitudes, without that making everything arbitrary.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **These theories don't make everything permissible.** Those who hold them still condemn cruelty and injustice.
2. **They also value consistency.** If our attitudes condemn unnecessary suffering, they should condemn it in animals too.
3. **The debate changes level, not outcome.** Even under anti-realism, there are better and worse reasons to act.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Self-refutation | Universally asserts that there are no universal truths |
| Logical | Selective application | Relativism only applies to one's own habit |
| Epistemological | From disagreement to no truth | Concludes there's no answer because there's disagreement |
| Moral | Confusing taste with harm | Treats as a preference a choice that affects others |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Condemns injustices without relativizing.** Racism, violence, corruption.
2. **Would feel wronged if they were the victim.** And wouldn't accept that it's "just the attacker's opinion".
3. **Argues about morality all the time.** Someone who thinks everything is relative shouldn't be outraged by anything.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there's moral disagreement, everyone has their own truth".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slavery | It was right for those who practiced it | No one accepts this |
| Domestic violence | It would be wrong only in the victim's opinion | No one accepts this |
| Racism | It would be one truth among others | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There's something true and important here: moral questions are hard, reasonable people disagree, and it takes humility not to treat one's own convictions as absolute certainties. It's also true that many questions are matters of taste and shouldn't be moralized.

But humility isn't relativism. Recognizing that we may be wrong is a reason to examine our practices more closely, not to declare them all equally valid.
