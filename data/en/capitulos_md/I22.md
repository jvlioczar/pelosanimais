# I22. "Horses like working and being ridden"

**Thesis:** it's a convenient projection by those who benefit. Horses are prey animals, and most riding teaches them to obey through pressure, discomfort and repetition; their docility is trained, not chosen. Even the bit, the metal piece placed in the mouth, tends to cause discomfort and often leaves injuries: in a Finnish study, 84% of trotters examined after a race had lesions in the bit area. Many also suffer from spurs, excessive loads and work on hot asphalt, like cart and carriage horses in cities. That an animal gets used to something doesn't mean it likes it.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Horses were born to be ridden. The carriage driver depends on it."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Horses were domesticated thousands of years ago for work and riding.
2. **P2.** Many horses seem calm and cooperative while working.
3. **P3.** Well-treated horses have a partnership with their carers.
4. **C.** So horses like working, and using them isn't exploitation.

P1 is true. P2 and P3 are true in some cases. The problem lies in the conclusion: seeming calm isn't consenting, and behavior is shaped by training.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because it reads trained obedience as preference.

## 3. Answer for children

Horses learn to obey because they're trained, sometimes with things that bother them, like the bit: a metal piece that sits inside the mouth and is pulled by the reins. Imagine someone pulling a little metal bar pressed against your gums: it hurts, and sometimes it injures. They can get used to it, but that doesn't mean they chose it. Horses that pull carts in the streets often work in the hot sun and carry a lot of weight. Loving horses means taking good care of them, not making them work for us.

## 4. Short answer

Horses are prey animals, and most riding teaches them to obey through pressure, discomfort and repetition. Seeming calm isn't consenting: it's having learned. Even the bit, a metal bar pressed against the gums and tongue, causes discomfort and often injuries: 84% of trotters examined after races in Finland had mouth lesions. Many also suffer from spurs, excessive loads and work on hot asphalt, like city cart and carriage horses. Saying they were "born for this" is attributing to the animal the purpose we gave it (C10).

**Question to ask back:** "How do you know the horse likes it, if it never had the option to refuse?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument reads trained behavior as preference:

| Behavior | Convenient interpretation | More careful interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The horse accepts the saddle | "It likes it" | It learned that resisting brings discomfort |
| The horse obeys the bit | "Partnership" | Pressure in the mouth is uncomfortable and can injure |
| The horse works all day | "It's in its nature" | It has no choice |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Trained obedience isn't consent.** Horse training uses pressure and release: the animal learns to yield so the discomfort stops.

**2. Horses are prey animals.** They often hide signs of pain and fear. Seeming calm doesn't guarantee wellbeing.

**3. Even the bit hurts.** The bit is a metal piece that sits in the horse's mouth, resting on the tongue and on the gums of the toothless gap in the lower jaw, an area rich in pain receptors. When the reins are pulled, the pressure is concentrated on a small area. Examinations confirm the damage: in Finland, 84% of trotters examined right after a race had acute lesions in the bit area, 20% of them severe, and there was almost never visible blood outside the mouth; in Iceland, studies of competition horses found lesions in roughly a third to more than half of the animals, depending on the year. Veterinary scientist David Mellor suggests a simple test to give an idea of the sensation: press the barrel of a pen against your own gums, below the front teeth, gradually increasing the force. The comparison is imperfect, because anatomy and sensitivity differ, but it helps explain why many horses open their mouths, toss their heads or put their tongues over the bit. Experts debate how much of the problem comes from bit type, fit and the rider's hands, and bitless bridles are a studied alternative, though direct comparisons are still few.

**4. There's real suffering in many uses.** Cart horses in cities work with excessive loads, on hot asphalt and without adequate rest. Several cities have banned or restricted horse-drawn carts and carriages for this reason, from Brazilian cities such as Belo Horizonte and Recife to Chicago.

**5. "Born for this" is an imposed purpose.** It's the purpose we assign, not a trait of the animal (C10).

**6. Economic dependence is real, and calls for a transition.** Cart drivers need alternatives, and programs replacing carts with motor vehicles already exist in some cities.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some horses seem to seek contact

The most serious version points out that some horses approach their carers spontaneously and seem to enjoy activities, and that there are more respectful forms of riding.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Liking the carer isn't liking the work.** The bond can be real without riding being wanted.
2. **More respectful forms are the exception.** Most use of horses is commercial and intensive.
3. **The relationship can exist without exploitation.** It's possible to live with horses without riding them or making them work.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument attributes to the horse the desire that suits whoever uses it. The moral question is whether it's fair to use an animal who can't consent, especially when the use causes suffering and there are alternatives.

2. **Wouldn't accept dogs pulling carts.** The criterion is tradition.
3. **Knows the horse is trained.** Which contradicts the idea of choice.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an animal obeys, it likes what it does".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circus animals | They'd like performing | Many places have banned them |
| Fighting dogs | They'd like fighting | It's a crime |
| Cart horses | They'd like pulling loads on hot asphalt | Several cities have banned it |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There are real bonds of affection between horses and humans, and many carers treat their animals with devotion. The economic dependence of cart drivers and ranch hands is real and must be considered with respect.

But affection doesn't prove consent, and obedience is learned. Loving horses can mean caring for them without requiring them to work for us.
