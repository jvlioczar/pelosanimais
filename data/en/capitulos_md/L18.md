# L18. "If vegans eat fake meat, they want meat"

**Thesis:** veganism's problem was never the taste of meat, but the victim. Many vegans liked the taste and texture of meat; what they refuse is what has to be done to the animal to get it. Wanting the taste without the death is exactly veganism's consistency. Analogues also have a practical role: because of the emotional memory attached to food, they ease the transition for people who want to stop causing animals' suffering and death, and help those who are still cutting down. But taste isn't fixed: it changes with repeated exposure, and it can be broadened. And names such as "burger" or "sausage" describe shapes and culinary uses, not animal origin.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Why call it a burger? A vegan sausage is a contradiction."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Products that imitate meat seek to reproduce its taste and texture.
2. **P2.** Those who eat these products want the experience of meat.
3. **P3.** If they want meat, their refusal is inconsistent or artificial.
4. **C.** So vegans are inconsistent.

P1 and P2 are true for many. The problem lies in P3: wanting a taste doesn't imply accepting what's done to obtain it.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Many vegans used to like the taste of meat, but they don't want animals to die. So they eat foods that remind them of meat, made from plants. That way they enjoy the taste without hurting anyone. That isn't a contradiction: it's a solution. And taste changes too: foods that seem strange at first can become favourites after you try them a few times.

## 4. Short answer

Veganism was never against the taste of meat, but against what's done to the animal. Many vegans liked meat, as perhaps you do; what they refuse is the death. Wanting the taste without the victim is exactly veganism's consistency. Analogues make the change easier because food carries memories and feelings; and, over time, taste itself changes. And names such as "burger" or "sausage" describe shapes and uses: you don't think a "hamburger" requires meat from Hamburg, or that "coconut milk" comes from a cow. For the animal, the difference between the two burgers is everything: in one of them it died; in the other, it didn't.

**Question to ask back:** "If it's possible to have the taste without killing the animal, what's the problem?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses what is being rejected:

| What the argument assumes the vegan rejects | What the vegan rejects |
| --- | --- |
| The taste of meat | Raising and killing the animal |
| The texture of meat | The suffering involved |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The problem is the victim, not the taste.** Veganism is a position about what we do to animals, not about tastes.

**2. Wanting the taste without the death is consistent.** It's precisely what plant-based products offer. The same reasoning appears in the debate about cultivated meat; see [The definition of veganism, debated](../alem/o-que-e-veganismo.html).

**3. The names describe shapes and uses.** "Burger", "sausage", "coconut milk" and "peanut butter" are culinary names, not promises of animal origin.

**4. Analogues help the transition and the cutting down.** Food isn't just nutrition: it's tied to childhood, family, celebrations and identity, and that emotional memory is one of the things that most binds people to meat dishes. A plant-based burger, sausage or cheese lets people keep those dishes and rituals while they stop causing animals' suffering and death, and makes the change less abrupt, both for those who want to stop altogether and for those who are cutting down. It's worth being honest about the reach: at market level, the evidence that analogues replace meat is still weak. In a natural experiment in a UK supermarket chain during Veganuary 2021, sales of plant-based products rose by around 57%, but meat sales didn't fall significantly. Analogues ease the choice of those who have already decided to change; on their own, they aren't enough to persuade.

**5. Taste can be broadened.** Taste preferences aren't fixed. In a classic study, adults who cut the salt in their diet for five months came to prefer less salty food, while the control group didn't change. A 2018 systematic review of 43 articles concluded that repeated exposure to one vegetable, and to a variety of vegetables, increases liking and intake, although the effects are modest and most studies were done with children. Analogues are a useful bridge, but nothing stops taste, on the other side of it, from changing and coming to enjoy beans, grains, vegetables and spices for what they are, not only for what they imitate.

**6. The argument is used against any vegan choice.** If the vegan eats salad, it's "rabbit food" (J7); if they eat a plant burger, they "want meat".

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: analogues are ultra-processed

The most serious version says that, even if wanting the taste is consistent, meat analogues are ultra-processed and aren't the best choice.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** From consistency to health (G18).
2. **Analogues are optional.** A vegan diet doesn't depend on them.
3. **Processed meat is ultra-processed too.** And classified as carcinogenic.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument unintentionally confirms the central point: taste isn't the problem. If it's possible to have the taste without the death, the moral question becomes even clearer: why choose the version that requires a death?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Uses "coconut milk" and "peanut butter".** Without finding them contradictory.
2. **Criticizes vegans who eat salad and those who eat burgers.** Any choice is criticized.
3. **Values alternatives in other areas.** Such as decaf coffee or alcohol-free beer.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone wants the taste of something, they should accept how it's produced".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohol-free beer | Whoever drinks it "wanted alcohol" and should drink regular beer | Absurd |
| Decaf coffee | Whoever drinks it "wanted caffeine" | Absurd |
| Plant burgers | Whoever eats them "wanted meat" and should eat meat | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that many vegans like the taste of meat, and that analogues are made to reproduce it. It's also true that the names can confuse some consumers, and that clear labeling matters.

But wanting the taste without the victim isn't a contradiction: it's exactly the point. Veganism was never about taste, but about the animal.
