# J10. "Vegans are against farmers"

**Thesis:** the phrase treats "farming" (or "Big Ag") as a single block. Veganism opposes animal farming, fishing and aquaculture, that is, everything that involves breeding, catching and killing animals for consumption. Those activities are one part of agriculture. The other part is growing crops, which is where vegan food comes from, and veganism has nothing against it. There are also good human and environmental reasons to criticize the animal products sector, but the central reason is a different one: even an animal industry that was clean and fair to people would go on killing the animals.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You want to put farmers out of business. Without farmers, you wouldn't even have your salad."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Agriculture produces everyone's food, including the rice, beans, soy and vegetables that vegans eat.
2. **P2.** Vegans campaign against agriculture and the people who work in it.
3. **P3.** Attacking the sector that feeds you is inconsistent and ungrateful.
4. **C.** So the vegan position is inconsistent and can be dismissed.

P1 is true. P3 would be reasonable if P2 were true. The problem lies in P2: veganism opposes one part of the sector (the part that breeds and kills animals), not the whole sector.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P2 only looks true because "farming" is used in two senses.

## 3. Answer for children

In the countryside, some people grow beans, corn and fruit, and some people raise cows and chickens to be turned into meat. Vegans are very fond of the people who grow plants: that's where their food comes from. What they don't want is for the animals to be killed. So they aren't against farms. They're against one thing that happens on some of them.

## 4. Short answer

I'm not against farmers: I eat what farming produces. I'm against breeding and killing animals, which is one part of the sector. You can criticize a part without attacking the whole, the way people criticize cigarettes without being against crop growing. And if the world ate more plants, the people who grow beans, grains, fruit and vegetables would have a bigger market, not a smaller one.

**Question to ask back:** "When you say 'farmers', do you mean people who grow food or people who raise animals for slaughter? I'm only against the second."

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The word "farming" covers very different activities:

| Part of the sector | What it does | Veganism's position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crops for people | Grows grains, pulses, fruit, vegetables, coffee | In favor: it's the source of vegan food |
| Crops for feed | Grows soy and corn to feed farmed animals | Depends on animal farming, and would shrink with it |
| Animal farming, fishing and aquaculture | Breed, catch and kill animals | Against |

Brazil, one of the world's largest food exporters, shows the proportions. According to its national farm confederation and the research center Cepea, agribusiness accounted for 23.2% of the country's GDP in 2024. Of that total of 2.72 trillion reais, about 1.9 trillion came from the crop branch and 819 billion from the livestock branch. The part veganism criticizes is the smaller one, although the crop branch also includes feed.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The part isn't the whole.** Criticizing animal farming isn't attacking crop growing, just as criticizing the tobacco industry isn't attacking people who grow food. The phrase only works if the two are treated as one thing (J9 makes the same mistake in the opposite direction).

**2. A more plant-based world needs more farming for people.** Beans, lentils, chickpeas, vegetables, fruit and nuts would be in greater demand. Today about 77% of the world's soy becomes animal feed (H1). The transition changes what is grown and for whom, which is why it needs planning and support for the people who work in the sector (J11).

**3. The animal products sector has costs for people.** Four of them are well documented. Work: Human Rights Watch reported in 2019 that workers in US meat and poultry plants face high rates of serious injury and chronic illness, and a survey in Brazil found that slaughterhouse workers suffer four times more accidents than the national average. Land: pasture was responsible for about 41% of tropical deforestation between 2005 and 2013, and Brazil's Pastoral Land Commission recorded 2,185 rural conflicts in 2024, the second-highest figure since 1985 (those conflicts involve several activities, not only cattle). Antibiotics: about 73% of the antimicrobials sold in the world are used in animals raised for food, and bacterial resistance was directly responsible for about 1.27 million human deaths in 2019. Disease: a report by the UN Environment Programme and the research institute ILRI lists rising demand for animal protein and unsustainable intensive farming among the factors that favor the emergence of zoonoses.

**4. The sector has political weight of its own.** A study published in 2023 in the journal *One Earth* calculated that, in the United States, animal products received about 800 times more public funding and 190 times more lobbying money than plant-based and cultivated alternatives; in the European Union, about 1,200 times more public funding. Defending interests before lawmakers is legitimate. The figures only show that criticizing this sector isn't kicking an underdog.

**5. These costs aren't the main reason.** They are additional reasons, and they vary from place to place. The reason behind veganism is what happens to the animals (see part f).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: in practice, it's all one chain

The most serious version says: you can't separate them. The same farmer grows soy and raises cattle, manure fertilizes the fields, the same companies and ports serve both. Attack animal farming and crop farming falls with it.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Integration isn't permanent dependence.** Integrated sectors have changed products many times. Farming without farmed animals exists and works, including for fertilization (H10).
2. **Whoever grows feed can grow food.** The change takes credit, technical support and time, which is why people speak of a just transition (J11 and J12).
3. **Integration doesn't answer the question.** That two activities are linked doesn't show that both are just.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Imagine an agricultural sector that was perfect for people and for the environment: high wages, no accidents, not one hectare deforested, no antibiotic out of place. In it, the cows, pigs, chickens and fish would still be killed young and healthy. That is what veganism opposes. The question that remains is whether an animal's interest in living and not suffering is worth less than a product that can be replaced by another.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Farming" means now the whole sector, now animal farming |
| Logical | Straw man | Attributes to veganism an opposition to crop growing that it doesn't have |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either you accept the whole sector or you're against all of it |
| Moral | Changing the question | Swaps "is it just to kill animals?" for "are you grateful to those who feed you?" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Criticizes parts of other sectors without opposing the whole.** Someone who criticizes illegal mining isn't against mining.
2. **Says vegans depend on farmers.** And so admits that vegan food is already produced by them.
3. **Defends the farmer.** But rarely mentions the slaughterhouse worker, who is also part of the sector.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J10 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity-protective cognition | Criticism of animal farming is felt as an attack on the countryside and those who live from it | Linked to J18 and J13 |
| System justification | The dominant sector seems necessary and untouchable as a whole | Linked to J9 |
| Stereotyping | The vegan is imagined as a city person who looks down on rural life | Linked to L1 |
| Reactive devaluation | The proposal is rejected because it comes from someone seen as an opponent | [*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. X, ch. 6](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v10/06-some-biases-that-can-influence-the-choice-of-strategies.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever criticizes one part of a sector is against the whole sector".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People who criticize cigarettes | They would be against agriculture | No one concludes this |
| People who criticize forced labor on farms | They would be against farmers | The criticism targets a practice |
| People who criticized the slave trade | They would have been against trade | The comparison is only of the argument's structure |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that vegan food comes from the land and that those who grow it deserve respect. It's also right that part of vegan talk refers to "Big Ag" in a generic and hostile way, as if every farmer were a villain, and that is unfair and pushes away people who could be allies. Many livestock farmers inherited the activity and see no other way out.

But the concession stops there. Being grateful to those who grow food doesn't oblige anyone to approve of slaughter, and criticizing slaughter isn't looking down on those who grow food. Veganism asks for the end of one practice within the sector, with support for those who depend on it.
