# M10. "I was vegan and started getting too radical, too rigid"

**Thesis:** becoming rigid, seeing the world in black and white and losing friends and family because of it is a real and common experience among people deeply committed to a cause, vegans included. But rigidity is a way of living the principle, not the principle itself. Not exploiting animals, as far as is possible and practicable, doesn't require intolerance, perfectionism or breaking with people. The manner can change, with firmness about the goal and flexibility about the means, without giving up the reason for the animals.

**Family:** M. Ex-vegans: why I gave up · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I got more and more rigid and saw the world only in black and white. I lost friends and even family because of my radicalism. I didn't recognize myself anymore: I'd become a pain. I'd rather be a normal person."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** As a vegan, I became rigid, intolerant and unable to see nuance, and it drove away people I loved.
2. **P2.** A practice that turns someone into a person they don't recognize, and that wrecks their relationships, can't be demanded of anyone.
3. **P3.** That rigidity is what veganism does to those who take it seriously.
4. **C.** So leaving veganism was the right decision.

P1 describes something that really happens. P2 is true: relationships and identity matter, and "practicable" includes what a person can bear. The problem lies in P3: the rigidity came from the way veganism was being lived, and that way can be swapped for another without ceasing to spare the animals.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P3 blames the principle for what was style and method.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine you love taking care of animals and you start fighting with everyone who doesn't do it your way. Your friends move away, and you end up alone and sad. That doesn't mean that caring for animals was wrong. It means the way you talked to others needed to change. You can keep caring for them and still be kind to people, even those who haven't learned yet.

## 4. Short answer

Thank you for telling this, and it isn't rare: many people devoted to a cause go through it. Losing friends and family hurts, and noticing what was happening shows awareness, not weakness. But look at what you described: the tone, the all-or-nothing, the break. None of that is part of not exploiting animals. You can keep the decision and change the manner: more flexible about the means, more patient with people, with breaks, and without giving up what happens to the animals.

**Question to ask back:** "What do you want to let go of: not eating animals, or the rigid, combative way of living it? Can you swap the second without losing the first?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The account mixes things that can be separated:

| What weighed | Is it part of the principle? | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not exploiting animals, as far as is possible and practicable | Yes, it's the core | Keep it |
| Seeing everything in black and white and demanding purity of oneself and others | No: it goes beyond "possible and practicable" (L5, M8) | Loosen up |
| A tone of reproach, contempt and fights | No: it's a manner of speaking, not a rule | Replace it with conversation and listening |
| Cutting ties with people who don't think the same | No: the principle doesn't require it | Reconnect, at whatever pace is possible |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The experience is real and common.** People who get deeply involved in a cause sometimes harden: the world splits into those inside and those outside, and every concession feels like betrayal. This happens in many causes, and the vegan movement is no exception. A 2019 study of 17 animal rights activists in the United States, by Paul Gorski, Stacy Lopresti-Goodman and Dallas Rising, points to factors inside the movement among the causes of burnout, such as hostility among activists and a culture that demands sacrifice and looks down on self-care. It is a small qualitative study: it shows the problem exists, not how often.

**2. Firmness about the goal doesn't require rigidity about the means.** What defines veganism is the goal: ending the exploitation of animals. The currents of the movement differ on the means (strategies, alliances, reasons), and no disagreement about means takes anyone out of the movement. The guide's page on the currents of the vegan movement lays out this map, and the "Stop saying this" list criticizes precisely the phrases that demand purity and invented rules, such as the claim that people who favor step-by-step change aren't really vegan. Being firm about not exploiting animals is compatible with being flexible about how to get there and with whom to walk.

**3. Rigidity is a way of living veganism, not a consequence of it.** Some vegans live it lightly, and some people become rigid through perfectionism, anxiety or group pressure, with or without veganism. What the person went through was a style, and styles can change. See M8 on guilt and perfectionism, M6 on hostile groups and M7 on the label: the focus here is different, the hardening of one's own thinking and its cost to relationships.

**4. Breaking with people is optional too.** The principle doesn't tell anyone to cut people off. In a 2014 Faunalytics survey, 84% of former vegetarians and vegans weren't involved in any group or organization, and in a later study of 222 people in transition, social strategies helped mainly those who knew few vegetarians or vegans. What is often missing is good company. Rebuilding bridges with family and friends is part of sustaining veganism in the long run.

**5. Some things help.** Support groups with a welcoming tone, instead of spaces of reproach. Breaks from activism and from heavy content. A focus on concrete actions (cooking for other people, supporting campaigns, donating) instead of endless debates. Relationships outside activism, where nobody asks what you eat. And learning from more pragmatic, evidence-based approaches, which ask what actually reduces animal suffering, not who is purer. If the distress is intense, seek professional help too.

**6. Abandoning the principle doesn't solve the problem.** Someone who hardened because they cared so much about animals doesn't stop caring by going back to funding exploitation. The conflict with others is traded for a conflict with oneself, and rigidity, if it came from temperament, may resurface in another cause.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: taking the principle seriously leads to radicalization

The most serious version says: if animals are victims of an enormous injustice, calm is complicity, and indignation is what one should expect. Someone who doesn't harden may not be taking the matter seriously. On this view, rigidity would be a sign of consistency, and dropping it would mean dropping the cause.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Indignation and rigidity are not the same thing.** One can be outraged by what happens to animals and still listen, adjust one's tone and accept partial progress. What the principle requires is consistency in one's own conduct, not contempt for others.
2. **Rigidity often undermines the goal it claims to serve.** If the aim is for fewer animals to suffer, what matters is the effect of what one does, and pushing people away rarely helps. That is why the pragmatic and evidence-based currents ask what works. This conclusion is about method, not about the principle.
3. **Even if rigidity were unavoidable for some, that wouldn't make exploitation fair.** The person could change roles (cooking, donating, living veganism quietly) instead of abandoning the practice.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The hardening, the fights and the lost relationships say a lot about how the person was living veganism, and nothing about the animals. They go on feeling pain and fear whether their defenders are gentle or impossible. The moral question is the same: is it fair to make an animal suffer and die when it can be avoided? The reason for the animals stays independent of the style of those who defend it, including the style the person themselves once had. Someone who recognizes that they became rigid has a reason to change the manner, not to go back to funding what wasn't fair.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in M10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either I'm rigid and vegan, or I'm flexible and go back to eating animals |
| Logical | False cause | Blames veganism for what came from the way it was lived |
| Epistemological | Hasty generalization | Concludes from one's own experience that veganism makes people radical |
| Moral | Changing the question | Swaps "what is fair to the animals?" for "what kind of person did I become?" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Recognizes that the rigidity did harm, yet treats veganism as if it required it.** Leaving veganism for this only makes sense if veganism demanded the rigidity, and it doesn't.
2. **Criticizes black-and-white thinking, yet uses it: either radical or out.** A middle path exists, and many people walk it.
3. **In other areas, the person would change the manner.** Someone who notices they've become obsessive about work or sport looks for balance, and doesn't conclude they should never work or exercise again.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains M10 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity-protective cognition | Someone whose identity was tied to the role of "the radical" feels that leaving it means dropping the principle too | General literature on identity-protective cognition |
| Motivated reasoning | The relief of stopping is read as proof that stopping was right | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Availability bias | The most painful conflicts are the best remembered | General literature on heuristics |
| In-group bias | Inside the group, the split between "us" and "them" encourages hardening | General literature on intergroup relations |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if living by a principle made me rigid and drove me from people, the principle should be abandoned".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Someone who became intolerant in an environmental cause | They could start polluting freely | The way of defending the cause changes, and the value stays |
| A believer who became a fanatic and lost their family | They could drop the ethics the religion taught | Many people keep the values and drop the fanaticism |
| Someone who became obsessive about sport and got injured | They could never exercise again | They look for a healthy pace |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that radicalization of attitude exists, that moral rigidity can wreck relationships and that losing friends and family over a cause is a real loss. It's right that someone who notices what is happening and wants to change deserves a warm welcome, not an accusation. And above all it's right that rigidity is a real problem to be faced, by the person who feels it and by the movement, which should welcome people instead of hardening.

But what hardened was the manner, and the question about the animals hasn't changed. The way out is to keep firmness about the goal and gain flexibility about the means: seek support, take breaks, concentrate on concrete actions, cultivate ties outside activism and learn from pragmatic approaches. That way the person comes to recognize themselves again, and the animals don't end up paying for the style of someone who tried to protect them.
