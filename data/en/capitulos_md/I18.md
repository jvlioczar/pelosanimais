# I18. "Wildlife sanctuaries protect animals"

**Thesis:** the word "sanctuary" is not regulated, and many places that use it are zoos or wildlife parks: they breed, buy and display animals, and sell photos and contact with them. In Australia, several "wildlife sanctuaries" are attractions of this kind. Real sanctuaries exist and help animals, but they can be recognized by simple criteria: they rescue, they don't breed or sell animals, and they don't turn them into a product for visitors.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's not a zoo, it's a sanctuary. The animals are protected there, and you can even hold a koala."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Sanctuaries exist to protect animals.
2. **P2.** This place is called a sanctuary.
3. **P3.** Visiting and paying helps keep that protection going.
4. **C.** So visiting this place, and interacting with the animals, is good for them.

P1 holds for real sanctuaries. P2 is just a name: it doesn't say what the place does. P3 depends on what the money pays for.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2: the name doesn't guarantee the practice.

## 3. Answer for children

A real sanctuary is like a shelter for animals that were hurt or can't go back to the wild. They live there in peace, not to be held by visitors. If a place makes babies be born so the public can see them, it's more like a zoo than a shelter.

## 4. Short answer

Don't trust the name. The word "sanctuary" isn't regulated, and many places that use it are zoos: they breed and buy animals and charge for photos and contact. In Australia, Lone Pine Koala Sanctuary let visitors hold koalas until 2024. Want to know if it's a real sanctuary? Check whether it rescues animals that can't go back to the wild, doesn't breed or sell them, and doesn't let the public handle them. Those deserve support. The others are zoos by another name, and the question stays the same: is it fair to turn an animal into a tourist attraction?

**Question to ask back:** "If the animals were rescued, why are babies born there every year?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses the name with the practice. A few criteria tell the two kinds of place apart:

| Question | Real sanctuary | Zoo called a sanctuary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where do the animals come from? | Rescues, seizures, animals that can't return to the wild | Own breeding, purchase, swaps with other parks |
| Does it breed animals? | No | Yes, often with babies as an attraction |
| Does it sell or trade animals? | No | Yes |
| Can the public hold or touch them? | No | Yes, often for an extra fee |
| Is the animal at the center? | Yes: welfare decides what is done | No: the visitor experience decides |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The name isn't regulated.** There is no general law defining "sanctuary". The Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries, the main body that accredits sanctuaries, defines a true sanctuary by the absence of exploitation: no commercial trade in animals, no invasive research and no unescorted public contact with wild animals.

**2. In Australia, the term is used by wildlife parks.** Lone Pine Koala Sanctuary, in Brisbane, is a wildlife park opened in 1927 that let visitors hold koalas and pose for photos. The practice ended in July 2024, and other Queensland parks still offer it.

**3. Breeding is not rescuing.** Organizations such as IFAW point out that a true sanctuary does not breed animals. When there are babies every year, they are the product, not the rescue.

**4. The contact serves the visitor.** Being held, passed from hand to hand and photographed is stressful for wild animals. The contact exists because it sells, not because the animal needs it.

**5. Real sanctuaries prove the point.** In a survey of wildlife tourism, the only types of attraction judged positive for both welfare and conservation were genuine sanctuaries. The difference lies in the practice, not the name.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some of these parks do conservation

The most serious version points out that some parks called sanctuaries take in injured animals, run veterinary hospitals and take part in conservation programs for endangered species.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The good work doesn't need the bad part.** Hospitals and rehabilitation don't depend on selling koala photos or breeding animals for display.
2. **Conserving the species is not respecting the individual.** The animal on display gets nothing from being the face of the campaign (A10).
3. **The criterion still applies.** Where there is real rescue, support it; where there is breeding, trade and paid contact, the name doesn't change what the place is.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question isn't whether the place has a nice name, but whether the animals are there for themselves or for the public. A true sanctuary exists for the animals; a zoo, for its visitors.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I18 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Sanctuary" is used in two senses: the shelter and the brand |
| Epistemological | Trust in the label | The name replaces checking the practice |
| Moral | Welfare subordinated to the experience | The contact that pleases the visitor outweighs the animal's stress |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the animals were rescued.** And watches babies being born for display.
2. **Says the place protects the animals.** And pays to handle them.
3. **Would reject the same practice with children.** No one calls a place a shelter if it charges to hold the children it takes in.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I18 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Framing effect | The word "sanctuary" changes how the same place is judged | [Tversky and Kahneman, 1981](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7455683) |
| Motivated reasoning | Wanting the photo makes the animal's stress invisible | General literature on motivated cognition |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the place is called a sanctuary, what it does is good for the animals".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A park that breeds tigers for photos | It would be protection | It's a trade in cubs |
| A breeder that calls itself a "refuge" | It would be a shelter | The name doesn't change the practice |
| A sanctuary that only rescues and doesn't display | It would be protection | Here the name matches the practice |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Real sanctuaries exist, do valuable work and deserve support: they take in animals that can't return to the wild or that were rescued from exploitation, including farmed animals. And some parks do real rehabilitation.

But the name isn't enough. The criteria are simple and public: where the animals come from, whether there is breeding, whether there is trade, whether there is paid contact.
