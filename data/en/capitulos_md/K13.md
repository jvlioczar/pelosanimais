# K13. "Everyone helps the cause they care about; you can't compare them"

**Thesis:** we compare causes all the time when resources are limited: hospitals triage, health systems compare the cost of each treatment, families decide where to spend. There are well-known criteria for this, such as the scale of a problem, how neglected it is and how much of it can be solved. Applied without bias, they point to animals: the victims number in the tens of billions a year and the resources devoted to them are tiny. Choosing a cause out of affinity can be legitimate and keeps people going, but it doesn't make comparison impossible. And the opposite extreme, "always help the most serious problem", is also wrong, because it forgets what can actually be solved and how much each extra resource still achieves.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Everyone helps the cause they like. You prefer animals, I prefer children. You can't compare."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Different causes involve different kinds of victims, suffering and values.
2. **P2.** There is no precise common measure for comparing these kinds of harm.
3. **P3.** Without a common measure, any ranking of causes is arbitrary.
4. **C.** So each person should help the cause they like, and no choice is better than another.

P1 is true. P2 is partly true: precision really is hard. The problem lies in P3: from "there is no precise measure" it doesn't follow that "no comparison is possible". Many comparisons are clear even without exact numbers, such as helping a thousand victims versus helping ten in the same situation.

The argument is **valid** in form, but P3 is false, so it isn't **sound**.

There is also an opposite version that shows up in the same debate: "Always help the most serious problem first". It accepts comparison, but uses a single criterion and ignores the others.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "To each their own cause." |

| Donations | "I give to what touches my heart." |

| Disguised version | "Comparing causes disrespects other people's pain." |

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine you have a bucket of water and there are two fires. At one, almost everyone is already throwing water. At the other, which is much bigger, nobody is helping. Both matter, but your water makes more difference where nobody is helping. You can like one side more and still think about where your help does the most good.

## 4. Short answer

I agree that everyone has their own affinities, and that helping a cause you care about is better than helping none. But saying you can't compare doesn't match what we do every day. A hospital triages. A health system decides which medicines to pay for. We compare because time and money are limited. And when we compare, animals stand out: tens of billions are killed every year on land alone, more than a trillion fish, and almost no resources go to them. That doesn't oblige anyone to drop their own cause. It just shows that comparison is possible, and worth doing.

**Question to ask back:** "If you had to choose where your help prevents the most suffering, would you still find it impossible to compare?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Question | What the objection assumes | What it leaves out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can causes be compared? | No, they're incomparable | We compare all the time when resources are scarce |
| What guides the choice? | Personal affinity | Number of victims, severity, neglectedness and tractability |
| Does affinity matter? | It's the only criterion | It matters as motivation, but doesn't set the size of the problem |
| And the opposite version? | Only severity counts | What can be solved and how much each extra resource still achieves |

The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha lists three common answers to the question of which causes to support: prioritize human causes; choose the cause one most sympathizes with; or treat all causes as equally important. He proposes a fourth: aim for the greatest good possible with the resources available, which requires comparing causes, problems and strategies (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VIII, ch. 1).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Comparing is what we do when resources are scarce.** Triage in an emergency room decides who is seen first. NICE, the body that assesses treatments for the UK's public health service, compares the cost of each treatment with the quality-adjusted life years it brings, using reference values of £25,000 to £35,000 per quality-adjusted life year. Nobody says these decisions are easy, but nobody concludes they are impossible.

**2. There are well-known criteria.** Cunha sets out the main ones: the severity of the situation, measured by the number of victims and how bad each one's situation is; tractability, that is, how much of the problem can be solved with the resources available; the size of the benefit each resource produces; long-term effects; tackling causes, not just symptoms; and the degree of neglectedness (vol. VIII, ch. 1; vol. XII, ch. 9). The organization 80,000 Hours sums up the three central ones as scale, neglectedness and tractability, and treats their combination as an estimate of the good done per unit of resources (Wiblin, 2016).

**3. Lack of precision isn't impossibility.** Cunha acknowledges that the criteria can point in opposite directions and that there are several methods for weighing them. But he notes that attempting these assessments, even without precision, tends to give better results than deciding at random. 80,000 Hours itself warns that its estimates are highly uncertain and should be read with common sense. Uncertainty calls for care, not for giving up. Demanding perfect precision before comparing is a form of the [nirvana fallacy](../falacias.html#nirvana-fallacy).

**4. Applied to animals, the criteria weigh heavily.** More than 80 billion land animals are slaughtered for meat each year, and an estimated 1.1 to 2.2 trillion wild fish are caught each year (Mood and Brooke, 2024). According to Animal Charity Evaluators, more than 99% of the animals killed by humans are farmed animals, and only about 3% of donations to animal charities in the United States go to organizations devoted to them; the organization itself says these figures are broad indicators, not precise statistics. Cunha adds that most donations go to human causes and concludes that the animal cause meets the priority criteria very well (vol. VIII, ch. 2).

**5. Neglectedness multiplies the effect of each contribution.** When almost no one works on a problem, there are still many easy, cheap opportunities to make a difference. As more people join, the harder tasks are what's left, and each additional person achieves less. This is called diminishing marginal returns (vol. VIII, ch. 1.7). Cunha also notes that the most neglected problems are often ones most people don't even know exist, because the media and social movements talk about the ones that already get attention.

**6. Affinity is legitimate, but it doesn't measure the problem.** Caring about a cause helps sustain effort for years, and 80,000 Hours counts personal fit, meaning one's skills, connections and passions, as a factor in choosing a career. But affinity says something about the helper, not about the size of the problem. A person can stay with their cause and still recognize that another one has more victims and less help. Nobody needs to prove they chose the most serious cause in the world; the point is only that comparison exists (J20).

**7. The opposite version is also wrong.** "Always help the most serious problem" uses severity alone. Cunha gives an example: problem A has 900,000 victims and problem B has 500,000, all in equally bad situations. By severity, A comes first. But if the available resources can help only 400,000 victims of A, or 450,000 of B to the same degree, then B is the choice that helps more. The same goes for the size of the benefit: relieving many victims a little can achieve less than relieving slightly fewer victims a lot. And if everyone rushes to the most serious problem, the second most serious is left with no one (vol. VIII, ch. 1.3, 1.4 and 1.7; vol. XII, ch. 9).

**8. Comparison also applies within the animal cause.** The same criteria help choose problems and strategies among animals. Cunha concludes that the most serious and most neglected problems are those of animals raised for food, especially aquatic animals and invertebrates, those of wild animals suffering from natural processes, and those concerning the future (vol. VIII, ch. 3). Many people who love animals think first of dogs and cats. And helping wild animals is a topic some vegans treat as "a different cause" (N14). The guide discusses the general framework on the page about [Effective altruism](../politicas/altruismo-eficaz.html).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the calculation is cold and leaves things out

The most serious version comes from critics of effective altruism. The philosopher Iason Gabriel (2017) brings together the main objections: effective altruism would be insensitive to justice, failing to give due weight to equality, urgency and rights; its methods would carry a bias that leads to mistaken conclusions, for example in favor of what is easy to measure; and it would struggle to bring about systemic change. Besides, comparing suffering across species involves a lot of uncertainty.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It criticizes a method, not comparison.** The objections call for better criteria, ones that include rights, equality, urgency and structural change. None of them shows that comparing is impossible.
2. **Cunha's criteria already include these concerns.** He allows methods that give more weight to those who are worse off and values tackling causes, such as speciesism, not just symptoms.
3. **Uncertainty cuts both ways.** And the difference in scale is so large that the animal cause stays relevant even with heavy discounts on each animal's weight (J20).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | Since comparison isn't perfect, it concludes that comparison is impossible |
| Logical | Priority fallacy | In the opposite version, only the most serious problem would deserve help |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either we compare with total precision, or the choice is mere personal taste |
| Epistemological | Ignoring tractability | Counts the size of the problem and forgets what can be solved |
| Moral | Relativism | Treats the size of suffering as a matter of preference |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says causes can't be compared.** But compares all the time when deciding where to spend their own time and money.
2. **Accepts triage in a hospital.** But rejects the same reasoning when the comparison includes animals.
3. **Says all causes are equal.** But rarely chooses the animal cause, which has more victims and fewer resources.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains K13 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope insensitivity | Billions of victims weigh no more than a thousand when choosing | Literature on numerical insensitivity |
| Proportion bias | Prefers solving much of a small problem to a small part of a huge one | Linked to J20 |
| Salience bias | Picks the causes that appear in the media, which are already the least neglected | Cunha, vol. VIII, ch. 1.7 |
| Collapse of compassion | Numerous, faceless victims move us less than one identifiable victim | Literature on compassion and numbers |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "when there is no precise measure, no choice between ways of helping is better than another".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency room | Seeing patients in order of sympathy would be as good as triage | No one accepts this |
| Public health | Funding an expensive, barely effective drug would be as good as a cheap vaccine that saves thousands | No one accepts this |
| Disasters | Sending all aid to the most famous city, rather than the hardest hit, would be just as good | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that affinity matters: people who care about their cause help for longer and with more energy. It's right that comparing causes is hard, that there is a lot of uncertainty and that no one should be shamed for having chosen to help someone. And the opposite version is right that severity counts.

The mistake is concluding that comparison is impossible. It is imperfect, but possible, and, done without excluding animals in advance, it shows that they are among the most numerous and most forgotten victims.
