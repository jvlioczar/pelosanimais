# I24. "Animals in films, ads and videos are trained, well-cared-for actors"

**Thesis:** the "no animals were harmed" credit doesn't guarantee it. In 2013, an investigation by The Hollywood Reporter reported injuries and deaths of animals in productions that received the credit, such as the deaths of 27 animals on a farm used during the filming of The Hobbit, and a tiger nearly drowning on Life of Pi. Outside film, social media videos of "cute" exotic animals can fuel the trade in those animals. Digital effects and footage of free animals make using animals on set less and less necessary.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The end of the film says no animals were harmed. They're trained actors and well cared for."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Audiovisual productions follow animal protection rules.
2. **P2.** There are bodies that monitor and certify how the animals are treated.
3. **P3.** Animals trained with positive reinforcement don't suffer at work.
4. **C.** So using animals in films and advertising is acceptable.

P1 and P2 are partly true. P3 may hold for some animals. The problem is that monitoring has failed in documented cases, and the use has consequences off the set.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and in the conclusion.

## 3. Answer for children

Many films use real animals, and sometimes they get hurt, even when the film says they didn't. Today it's possible to make amazing animals with computers, as in many animated films. And videos of exotic pets can make more people want to buy those animals, which is bad for them.

## 4. Short answer

Do you trust the "no animals were harmed" credit? It doesn't guarantee it: in 2013, an investigation by The Hollywood Reporter reported injuries and deaths of animals in certified productions, such as the deaths of 27 animals on a farm used during the filming of The Hobbit. And outside film, those videos of "cute" exotic animals going around social media can fuel the trade in those animals. Digital effects and footage of free animals make using animals on set less and less necessary. If it can be filmed without them, why put an animal at risk for a scene?

**Question to ask back:** "If a whole lion can be created by computer, why put a real lion on a set?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Situation | What can happen to the animal |
| --- | --- |
| Filming on set | Stress, transport, risky scenes, long waits |
| Between shoots | Kept at trainers' facilities, not always monitored |
| Social media videos | Exotic animals kept as pets, in inadequate conditions |
| Effect on the public | Increased demand for animals seen in films and videos |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The credit has documented failures.** The Hollywood Reporter's 2013 investigation reported that the organization behind the credit failed to protect animals in several productions, with injuries and deaths. The organization disputed the report and said it had changed its program.

**2. Monitoring doesn't cover everything.** The credit covers filming time; breeding, transport and training off set are largely outside it.

**3. The screen influences the trade.** Videos of exotic animals in "cute" situations make keeping them as pets seem normal, which can increase demand for them.

**4. Technology already substitutes.** Digital effects create realistic animals, and carefully made footage of free animals shows natural behavior without training for the camera.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [The Hollywood Reporter, 2013](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/news/animals-were-harmed-hollywood-reporter-investigation-on-set-injury-death-cover-ups-659556) | Investigation into injuries and deaths of animals in certified productions | The certifying organization disputed the report |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: well-trained animals live well with their trainers

The most serious version says many animal actors, such as dogs, live with trainers who treat them well, and work for short periods with positive reinforcement.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It may hold for some domesticated animals.** But not for wild animals, such as tigers, primates and exotic animals, who shouldn't live to entertain.
2. **The effect on the public remains.** Even an animal well treated on set may encourage people to buy others, in worse conditions.
3. **The alternative exists.** When the scene can be made without animals, the use stops being necessary.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to use animals to entertain, when technology plays the same role and the use encourages other forms of exploitation.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Trusts the credit.** Without knowing it has been questioned by investigations.
2. **Finds the video cute.** And doesn't ask how the exotic animal lives.
3. **Accepts digital effects for dragons.** And demands real animals for scenes with lions.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I24 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority bias | An organization's credit conveys safety | General literature on authority |
| Baby schema | The cute look hides the animal's suffering | [Glocker et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1439-0310.2008.01603.x) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there's a monitoring credit, using animals on set is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Productions with documented deaths | They'd be acceptable | The credit didn't prevent them |
| Videos of primates as pets | They'd be acceptable | They encourage a harmful trade |
| Films made only with digital effects | They serve the same function | Without animals on set |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that there are rules and monitoring, that many trainers take good care of their animals, and that films and videos can awaken affection for animals.

But monitoring has documented failures, the use encourages buying exotic animals, and technology allows the same stories to be told without animals on set.
