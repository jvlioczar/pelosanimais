# E15. "I know it's wrong, but I won't change"

**Thesis:** this is the most honest answer in the catalog, and therefore the most important. It concedes the moral point and refuses the consequence. There's no argument to refute: the person already agrees. What remains is the gap between knowing and doing, which philosophy calls akrasia, or weakness of will. The conversation doesn't end here; it changes in nature: it stops being about whether it's wrong and becomes about how to change.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR · **Root chapter:** one of the three roots where conversations end, along with A8 and J5 · **For those who defend animals:** why facts and logic rarely suffice to change behaviour, and what else helps, is in [When logic isn't enough](../alem/quando-a-logica-nao-basta.html)

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "You're right, but I like it too much. Deep down I know. I'll stop someday."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** I recognize that eating animals causes harm I can't justify.
2. **P2.** Changing deep habits is hard, and I don't have enough motivation right now.
3. **P3.** No one lives up to all of their values.
4. **C.** So I keep eating animals, without pretending it's right.

The strong version has a rare virtue: it's honest. It doesn't invent justifications. The problem lies in the conclusion: recognizing that something is wrong and carrying on doesn't make the practice acceptable, only transparent.

The argument **isn't valid** as a justification: the premises explain the behavior but don't justify it. It is, in fact, a confession.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes we know something isn't nice, but we keep doing it because it's hard to stop, like biting our nails or leaving our room messy. Knowing it's wrong is already an important step! The next step is to look for help and change little by little, until it gets easier.

## 4. Short answer

Thank you for your honesty: you already agree with the most important part. The gap between knowing and doing is human, and it happens to almost everyone on some subject. But it doesn't have to be permanent. You don't need to change everything at once: you can start with one meal, one day a week, one product. What makes the change hardest? Maybe we can work that out together.

**Question to ask back:** "If you already know it's wrong, what would make the change easier for you?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument justifies nothing; it separates two things:

| What the person admits | What the person refuses |
| --- | --- |
| Eating animals causes unjustified harm | Changing their own behavior |
| The opposing arguments are better | Acting on them |

This is what Aristotle called *akrasia*: acting against one's own best judgment.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Explaining isn't justifying.** The difficulty of changing explains why the person carries on, but doesn't make what they do acceptable. For the animal, the honesty of whoever eats them changes nothing.

**2. The gap between knowing and doing is common.** Even ethics specialists, most of whom consider eating mammal meat morally bad, eat meat almost as much as other professors. This shows the problem isn't one of argument, but of practice (E6).

**3. Dissonance demands a way out.** Psychology shows that conflict between values and behavior creates discomfort, and that people tend to resolve it in two ways: by changing the behavior or by distorting the belief. Whoever admits the wrong is at the point where the first way out is still open.

**4. "Someday" rarely comes without a plan.** Changes postponed without a date tend to be postponed forever. A small, concrete step is worth more than a big, vague intention.

**5. This is the root where the conversation changes in nature.** There's nothing left to refute. What helps now is practical support: recipes, substitutions, company, small goals.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: honesty is already a value

The most serious version says admitting the contradiction is more dignified than inventing justifications, and that no one should be criticized for being honest about their own failings.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's right about honesty.** Admitting is better than rationalizing, and deserves recognition.
2. **But honesty is a beginning, not an end.** Recognizing a wrong and keeping it up indefinitely is different from recognizing it and working to change.
3. **The best response is to help.** Those who defend animals should welcome this honesty and offer paths forward, not guilt.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Admits the wrong and keeps it up.** This is the contradiction in its most open form.
2. **Has changed other hard habits.** Most people have already changed something they thought impossible.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same answer from someone who harmed them.** "I know it's wrong, but I won't stop" satisfies no victim.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains E15 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cognitive dissonance | The discomfort is tolerated or eased without changing the practice | [Benningstad and Kunst, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31830517/) |
| Present bias | Immediate pleasure weighs more than a future commitment | General literature on temporal discounting |
| Status quo bias | Keeping the habit seems like the natural path | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Sunk cost and effort justification | Changing would mean admitting that lifelong habits were wrong | [Questão de Ética, vol. X, ch. 4](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v10/04-cognitive-biases-and-their-influence-on-our-decisions.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "admitting something is wrong is enough, even if I carry on".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drink-driving | Admitting it's wrong would be enough | No one accepts this |
| Betraying someone's trust | Frankness would solve the problem | No one accepts this |
| A polluting company | Acknowledging the harm would exempt it from fixing it | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The honesty is real and valuable. Whoever says "I know it's wrong" has already taken the hardest step intellectually, and deserves respect for not hiding behind justifications. The difficulty of changing is real too: eating habits are tied to affection, routine and identity.

That's why this is the root where the conversation stops being a debate and becomes an invitation. There's no argument left to win, only a change to build, ideally together and one step at a time.
