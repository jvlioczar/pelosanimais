# I12. "Bees make extra honey"

**Thesis:** honey is the bees' food reserve for periods without flowers, not a surplus. In beekeeping it's taken and often replaced with sugar syrup, which doesn't provide what honey provides. The hive is managed like a herd: queens are replaced and killed, and may have their wings clipped or be artificially inseminated. Honeybees are kept like domestic animals and can compete with native bees, which are the main pollinators in many ecosystems; there are some 20,000 bee species worldwide. And there is growing evidence that bees have experiences, which the New York Declaration recognizes as a realistic possibility.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Honey is natural. Without beekeepers, bees disappear. Bees don't even feel anything."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Bees produce more honey than they consume in good seasons.
2. **P2.** Beekeeping helps maintain bee populations, which are important for pollination.
3. **P3.** Bees are insects, with little or no capacity to suffer.
4. **C.** So eating honey causes no harm and even helps bees.

P1 is partly true. P2 confuses honeybees, which are kept animals, with native bees, which are in decline. P3 is increasingly disputed by science.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Bees make honey so they have food in winter, when there are no flowers. When people take the honey, they often give the bees sugar water instead, which doesn't feed them as well. Beekeepers also clip the queen's wings so she can't fly away, and swap an old queen for a new one, killing the old one. Besides, there are lots of wild bees that live freely and help plants. They're the ones that need protecting.

## 4. Short answer

Extra for whom? Honey is the bees' food reserve, not a surplus; in beekeeping it's often replaced with sugar syrup, which doesn't provide the same nutrients. The hive is managed like a herd: queens replaced and killed, wings clipped, artificial insemination. And if you care about bees, the ones in decline are wild native species, not honeybees, which are kept like domestic animals and can compete with them for flowers and spread disease. Buying honey doesn't help native bees. And there's growing evidence that bees have experiences: if they can feel, why treat them as production machines?

**Question to ask back:** "If the problem is the decline of native bees, does keeping more honeybees help?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two groups of bees:

| Group | Situation | Relationship to honey |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honeybees (*Apis mellifera*) | Kept by beekeepers, like domestic animals | Produce commercial honey |
| Native bees (around 20,000 species worldwide) | Many in decline from habitat loss and pesticides | Not helped by buying honey |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Honey is a reserve, not a surplus.** Bees store honey for periods without flowers. Beekeeping removes that store and often replaces it with sugar syrup; extension guides recommend feeding hives syrup when honey runs short, including in winter. Syrup isn't equivalent to honey: a 2014 study found hundreds of differences in the expression of genes linked to metabolism and to defence against oxidative stress in bees fed sucrose or corn syrup, compared with honey. The authors caution that the effect on bee health still needs to be checked.

**2. Honeybees are kept animals.** They aren't threatened with extinction for lack of beekeeping. The decline that worries scientists is that of native bees.

**3. Honeybees can harm native bees.** A 2017 review found that most competition studies indicated negative effects of managed bees on wild bees, and that a large share of the disease studies pointed to transmission risks. The evidence is still debated, but the claim that honey "saves the bees" doesn't hold up.

**4. Bees may have experiences.** The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) recognizes a realistic possibility of consciousness in insects. Research shows bees learn, play and respond flexibly to stimuli.

**5. The hive is managed like a herd, and that kills and maims bees.** Bees die when honey is harvested and when hives are transported. Many beekeepers replace the queen every one or two years with a bought or specially reared queen; extension guides advise killing the old queen, and one says many prefer to pinch her head off. Some clip part of the queen's wings to make it harder for a swarm to leave; the practice is controversial among beekeepers themselves, can injure the queen if done badly and often doesn't prevent swarming. In artificial insemination, used to select breeding lines, the queen is anaesthetized with carbon dioxide and held still, and semen is obtained by squeezing drones' abdomens until their reproductive organ is exposed. None of this is part of the life of a free colony: it's the management of a production animal.

**6. Beekeeping can take resources from other pollinators.** Large numbers of hives concentrated in one area compete for nectar and pollen with native bees and other pollinators, and can pass diseases on to them (point 3). In a 2018 study in a protected area in southern France, apiaries with many hives reduced the presence of wild bees by about 55% and their nectar-foraging success by about 50%, within roughly 600 to 1,100 metres; the authors propose minimum distances between apiaries in protected areas. The result applies to that environment, but it shows that more hives doesn't mean more bees in the sense that matters for conservation.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: beekeeping helps crop pollination

The most serious version points out that many crops depend on pollination by honeybees, and that beekeeping is important for food production.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Pollination doesn't depend on eating honey.** It's a service that can be paid for separately, as already happens.
2. **Native bees pollinate too.** And often more efficiently for certain crops.
3. **It doesn't answer the question about honey.** Buying honey isn't the same as supporting pollination.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Honey is a borderline case, and there are honest debates about insect experience. But the argument used to defend it, that it saves the bees, is false. If there's uncertainty about what bees feel, and easy alternatives to honey, caution recommends leaving it to them.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they want to save the bees.** But doesn't support native bees, which are in decline.
2. **Accepts that honey is the bees' food.** And replaces it with sugar.
3. **Doesn't eat other insect products.** The "they don't feel" criterion is used only here.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a species is kept in large numbers, it's protected".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chickens | They'd be protected, because there are billions | This is the argument of B7 |
| Honeybees | They'd be helping bees in general | They can harm native bees |
| Farmed animals in general | They'd be fine, because they're numerous | Ignores the individual |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The decline of bees is a real and serious problem, and pollination is essential for food production. Well-run beekeeping can play a role in agriculture, and insect experience is indeed an open question.

But buying honey doesn't protect the bees that are in decline, and may even harm them. Protecting bees means protecting their habitats, reducing pesticides and planting flowers, not eating their food reserve.
