# I12. "Zoos conserve species and educate"

**Thesis:** most species kept in zoos aren't threatened. A 2013 study using data from zoos around the world found that about 23% of the land vertebrate species in their collections are threatened, and few take part in reintroduction programs. The most effective conservation happens through habitat protection. And the "education" of seeing a confined animal may teach that confining it is normal. Finally, a consistency test: anyone who defends zoos by saying the animals live well or better there is using the welfare of each individual as the criterion, not just the survival of the species. That criterion applies to every zoo animal and also to wild animals, whose suffering in nature rarely enters the calculation.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Without zoos, children wouldn't know animals. They live longer in the zoo."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many species are threatened with extinction.
2. **P2.** Zoos maintain reserve populations and take part in breeding programs.
3. **P3.** Zoos educate the public about conservation.
4. **C.** So zoos are important for conservation, and keeping animals in them is justified.

P1 is true. So is P2, for some species. P3 has mixed evidence. The problem lies in the conclusion: it justifies the whole zoo with a small part of what it does.

The argument is **valid** for conservation programs. It fails as a justification for confinement in general.

## 3. Answer for children

In zoos, many animals live in small spaces, far from where they were born. Most of them aren't in danger of disappearing from the wild. To really protect animals, the most important thing is to look after the forests, rivers and seas where they live. And you can learn a lot about them from documentaries, books and visits to sanctuaries. People who say animals live well in zoos care about how the animals live; so, to be fair, they should also care about how animals living free in nature live.

## 4. Short answer

Most zoo species aren't threatened: a 2013 study found that about 23% of the land vertebrate species in zoo collections are threatened, and few take part in reintroduction. The most effective conservation is protecting habitats. Wide-ranging animals, such as elephants and big cats, suffer greatly in confinement. And seeing an animal locked up can teach that locking it up is normal. If the defence is that animals live well in zoos, the criterion is each individual's welfare, and it applies to wild animals too.

**Question to ask back:** "How many of the zoo animals you know will ever go back to the wild?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument uses a small part to justify the whole:

| Claimed function | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Conserving threatened species | About 23% of species in collections; few reintroduced |
| Education | Mixed evidence on learning and change of attitude |
| Entertainment | The main reason for visits |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Most species aren't threatened.** A 2013 study found that about 23% of the land vertebrate species in zoos are threatened, and that zoos hold about one seventh of the world's threatened species.

**2. Few species are reintroduced.** Successful reintroduction programs exist, but they involve a small fraction of the animals kept.

**3. Conservation depends on habitat.** Without protected forests, rivers and grasslands, there's nowhere to reintroduce animals to. Most effective conservation happens *in situ*.

**4. Wide-ranging animals suffer.** Elephants, big cats and other animals that travel long distances develop stereotypic behaviors in captivity, signs of suffering.

**5. The "education" may teach the opposite.** Seeing confined animals as entertainment can normalize confinement.

**6. A consistency test: if the criterion is welfare, it applies to everyone.** A common version of the argument says animals "live longer" or "live better" in zoos, away from predators, hunger and disease. In part, this has a basis: a 2016 study of more than 50 mammal species found that most live longer in zoos than in the wild. But there are important exceptions: zoo elephants, for example, lived much shorter lives than wild ones according to a 2008 study. And living longer isn't the same as living well. The point here, though, isn't to settle who is right about the numbers, but to notice what this argument presupposes. Whoever uses it adopts the welfare of each individual as the criterion, not the mere existence of the species. If that is the criterion, two things follow:

1. **It applies to every zoo animal.** The zoo is then judged by the life of each animal, including those who suffer from confinement, and not by the number of species preserved.
2. **It also applies to wild animals.** If hunger, disease and predation in nature count against life in the wild, then the suffering of the billions of animals living in nature should matter too, and not only when it serves to defend captivity. This is the problem of [wild animal suffering](../alem/animais-selvagens.html).

This isn't about accusing anyone of bad faith, but about proposing a test: those who take the welfare of zoo animals seriously are invited to take seriously the welfare of those living outside zoos too. If someone only invokes suffering in nature to justify confinement, the real criterion may not be welfare.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some programs have saved species

The most serious version cites real cases, such as the California condor and the golden lion tamarin, where captive breeding helped prevent extinction.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **These cases are exceptions.** They justify conservation centers, not display zoos.
2. **The golden lion tamarin depends on Brazil's Atlantic Forest.** The success came together with protecting the habitat.
3. **Sanctuaries and rehabilitation centers can do this work.** Without displaying animals as entertainment.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Conserving species is important, but the animal living in the zoo is an individual, not a species. The moral question remains: what justifies confining someone for their whole life so that others can look at them?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't support habitat protection with the same emphasis.** Which is the most effective conservation.
2. **Goes to the zoo for the outing.** Not for conservation.
3. **Wouldn't accept similar confinement for dogs.** Without space or stimulation.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an institution does some good, everything it does is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circuses with animals | They'd be justified, because they "educate" | Many have been banned |
| Roadside zoos | They'd be justified | No one defends them |
| Zoos | They'd be justified by a few programs | The good doesn't justify the rest |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Some zoos do serious conservation, research and rehabilitation work, and there are real cases of species saved with the help of captive breeding. Concern about extinction is legitimate and urgent.

But conservation can be done without displaying animals, and most zoo animals aren't part of these programs. Protecting species starts with protecting habitats and individuals.
