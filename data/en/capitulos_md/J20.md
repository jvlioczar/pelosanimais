# J20. "There are more important human problems"

**Thesis:** it's the fallacy of priority. Eating plants takes no time, money or energy away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. Besides, livestock farming is linked to serious human problems, such as deforestation, climate change, pandemics and dangerous working conditions. And if the idea of giving priority to the worse off is taken seriously and applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals, who are the most numerous victims and among those who suffer most intensely.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "With so many children going hungry... Fight for people first."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There are serious human problems, such as hunger, war and poverty.
2. **P2.** Moral resources and attention are limited.
3. **P3.** Human problems should have priority.
4. **C.** So concern for animals distracts from what matters more.

P1 is true. So is P2, in part. The problem lies in the conclusion: no longer eating animals doesn't use up resources that would go to other causes.

The argument is **valid** if veganism competed with other causes. Its **soundness** fails because it doesn't.

## 3. Answer for children

You can help people and animals at the same time. Eating beans instead of meat doesn't stop anyone from donating food, helping a friend or studying to change the world. You don't have to pick just one good thing to do. And if we should help those who suffer most first, many farm animals are among those who suffer most.

## 4. Short answer

Eating plants takes no time or money away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. And livestock farming is linked to serious human problems: deforestation, climate change, pandemic risk and dangerous work. Caring for animals is often caring for people too. And if the rule is to give priority to the worse off, animals raised for food, killed by the billions and often in intense suffering, are near the front of the line.

**Question to ask back:** "What would you stop doing for people if you ate beans instead of meat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assumes causes compete:

| Action | Does it use up resources from other causes? |
| --- | --- |
| No longer eating animals | No: it's a swap on the plate, often cheaper |
| Campaigning for animals | Some time, like any cause |
| Donating to fight hunger | Not prevented by veganism |
| Giving priority to the worse off | If applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the fallacy of priority.** That there are more serious problems doesn't make smaller ones irrelevant. If it held, almost no cause could be defended.

**2. Not causing harm isn't a competing cause.** No longer eating animals is an abstention, not a project that takes up time.

**3. Livestock farming affects humans.** Deforestation (H1), emissions (H7), antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases that pass from animals to humans are human problems.

**4. The argument often serves to end the conversation.** Even when it comes from someone devoted to another cause, it's still a false choice: not eating animals doesn't compete with any other struggle.

**5. Many people champion several causes.** Animal advocates also work for human rights, against hunger and for social justice.

**6. Taken seriously, priority may point to the animals.** There is a moral theory that gives the idea of priority a precise form: prioritarianism, associated with the philosopher Derek Parfit, who presented it in 1991 in the lecture "Equality or Priority?". On this theory, a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient is. It doesn't say "humans first", but "the worse off first". What counts is the level of well-being, not species. If the principle is applied to all sentient beings, priority may fall on the animals: more than 80 billion land animals are killed for food every year, plus a very uncertain number of fish, estimated in the hundreds of billions or more, and many of them spend their lives in confinement, in intense pain and deprivation. There is disagreement here: prioritarianism is usually formulated for human persons only; the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* acknowledges that this restriction is problematic because it excludes animals and treats the extension as an open question; comparing well-being across species is hard; and some authors defend different weights for humans and animals. But whoever appeals to priority can't, without further argument, stop at the species line. The philosopher Catia Faria, for example, argues that egalitarians and prioritarians are committed to rejecting speciesism and to giving priority to the interests of the animals who are worst off.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: animal activism takes energy

The most serious version grants that eating plants doesn't compete with other causes, but says animal activism takes time and money that could go to human causes.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** The question was about eating animals, not about activism.
2. **All causes compete for time.** And no one demands that only the most serious be championed.
3. **Animal suffering is enormous.** Billions of sentient beings a year, which makes the cause relevant. By the standard of giving priority to the worse off, it may even be among the most urgent.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument doesn't say animals don't matter; it says other things matter more. But no longer paying for animals to be killed takes nothing away from those other things. The moral question remains: if it harms no one, why not stop?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J20 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Fallacy of priority | If there's something more serious, the rest doesn't matter |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either humans or animals |
| Epistemological | Ignoring connections | Doesn't see the link between livestock and human problems |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Devotes time to things less serious than hunger.** Such as hobbies, sport or local politics.
2. **Isn't fighting hunger.** Uses the argument without acting.
3. **Cares about cruelty to dogs.** Without thinking it distracts from human causes.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J20 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | The priority of other causes excuses change | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Speciesism | Human interests always seem more important | [Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29517258/) |
| Zero-sum bias | Caring for animals is seen as taking something from humans, though the two do not compete for the same resources | [Meegan, 2010](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00191) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "as long as there are bigger problems, smaller ones don't matter".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fighting racism | It wouldn't matter while there's hunger | No one accepts this |
| Protecting children | It wouldn't matter while there are wars | No one accepts this |
| Cruelty to dogs | It wouldn't matter | Those who use the argument disagree |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Human problems are serious and urgent, and no one should be indifferent to hunger, poverty and violence. It's also true that some people present the animal cause in ways that seem to disdain humans, which is a mistake.

But no longer eating animals doesn't compete with any of those causes. It's possible to care for people and animals at the same time, and often one helps the other.
