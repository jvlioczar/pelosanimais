# J20. "A vegan world is utopian"

**Thesis:** the argument confuses the feasibility of a total, immediate change with present individual duty. Perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared. But that doesn't change what each person should do today. Many moral changes seemed utopian before they happened, and the relevant question isn't whether everyone will change, but whether I should.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That will never happen. It's a hippie dream."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** A fully vegan world would require enormous changes in cultures, economies and habits.
2. **P2.** Those changes are unlikely in the foreseeable future.
3. **P3.** It makes no sense to guide conduct by an unattainable ideal.
4. **C.** So veganism is a utopia and shouldn't guide behavior.

P1 and P2 may be true. The problem lies in P3: individual duty doesn't depend on everyone fulfilling it.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

Maybe not everyone in the world will stop eating meat. But that doesn't change what each of us can do. Not everyone is kind, and it's still worth being kind. Every animal who stops suffering matters.

## 4. Short answer

Perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared. But each person's duty doesn't depend on everyone fulfilling it. Abolishing slavery, giving women the vote and protecting children from labor all once seemed utopian. The relevant question isn't whether everyone will change, but whether I should. And each person who changes spares real animals, today.

**Question to ask back:** "If the world will never be perfect, does that excuse you from doing what's right?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two questions:

| Question | Type | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Will the whole world go vegan? | Prediction | Uncertain |
| Should I stop eating animals? | Moral | Doesn't depend on the prediction |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Duty doesn't depend on everyone joining in.** We don't stop condemning violence because it will never end.

**2. Many changes seemed utopian.** The abolition of slavery, women's suffrage and the protection of children were treated as dreams before they happened.

**3. Individual impact is real.** Each person who changes reduces demand and spares animals (K1).

**4. The world can change partially.** Even without an entirely vegan world, the reduction in animal consumption could be enormous.

**5. The argument changes the question.** It answers a prediction, not the moral question.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)](https://campuspress.yale.edu/shellykagan/papers) | Each purchase has a real expected effect on production | Shows the value of individual action |
| [Our World in Data, land use and diets](https://ourworldindata.org/land-use-diets) | A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75% | Shows the change is feasible in principle |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: unrealistic ideals breed frustration

The most serious version says that championing an unattainable ideal can frustrate people and put them off, and that more modest goals would be more effective.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a strategic question, not a moral one.** The best way to communicate can be debated without denying the duty.
2. **Individual duty is attainable.** Each person can stop eating animals, even if the world doesn't change.
3. **Ideals guide change.** Even when they're never fully realized.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question about the world's future is interesting, but it doesn't replace the question about the present: today, for those who can choose, what justifies paying for animals to be killed?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J20 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the question | Answers a prediction, not a moral question |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | If not everyone can reach the ideal, nothing need be done |
| Moral | Duty conditional on universal adherence | It would only make sense to act if everyone acted |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Does the right thing on other matters, even if others don't.** Such as not stealing.
2. **Admires historical changes that seemed utopian.**
3. **Knows they can change their own plate.** Regardless of the world.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains J20 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo bias | The current world seems unchangeable | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |
| Diffusion of responsibility | If not everyone changes, no one needs to | General literature on diffusion of responsibility |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a goal won't be reached by everyone, no one has a duty to pursue it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ending violence | No one would need to stop being violent | No one accepts this |
| Honesty | No one would need to be honest | No one accepts this |
| Abolishing slavery | It shouldn't have been pursued | Absurd |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that an entirely vegan world seems distant, and that cultural changes of this size are slow and uncertain. Being realistic about the pace of change helps plan better strategies.

But the distance from the ideal doesn't change what each person can do today. Individual duty doesn't wait for the world to change.
