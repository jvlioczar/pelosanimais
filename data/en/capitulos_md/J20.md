# J20. "There are more important human problems"

**Thesis:** it's the fallacy of priority. Eating plants takes no time, money or energy away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. Besides, livestock farming is linked to serious human problems, such as deforestation, climate change, pandemics and dangerous working conditions. And if the idea of giving priority to the worse off is taken seriously and applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals, who are the most numerous victims and among those who suffer most intensely.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "With so many children going hungry... Fight for people first."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There are serious human problems, such as hunger, war and poverty.
2. **P2.** Moral resources and attention are limited.
3. **P3.** Human problems should have priority.
4. **C.** So concern for animals distracts from what matters more.

P1 is true. So is P2, in part. The problem lies in the conclusion: no longer eating animals doesn't use up resources that would go to other causes.

The argument is **valid** if veganism competed with other causes. Its **soundness** fails because it doesn't.

## 3. Answer for children

You can help people and animals at the same time. Eating beans instead of meat doesn't stop anyone from donating food, helping a friend or studying to change the world. You don't have to pick just one good thing to do. And if we should help those who suffer most first, many farm animals are among those who suffer most.

## 4. Short answer

Does eating plants take time or money away from any other cause? You don't have to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. And livestock farming is linked to serious human problems: deforestation, climate change, pandemic risk and dangerous work. Caring for animals is often caring for people too. And if the rule is to give priority to the worse off, animals raised for food, killed by the billions and often in intense suffering, are near the front of the line.

**Question to ask back:** "What would you stop doing for people if you ate beans instead of meat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assumes causes compete:

| Action | Does it use up resources from other causes? |
| --- | --- |
| No longer eating animals | No: it's a swap on the plate, often cheaper |
| Campaigning for animals | Some time, like any cause |
| Donating to fight hunger | Not prevented by veganism |
| Giving priority to the worse off | If applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's the fallacy of priority.** That there are more serious problems doesn't make smaller ones irrelevant. If it held, almost no cause could be defended.

**2. Not causing harm isn't a competing cause.** No longer eating animals is an abstention, not a project that takes up time.

**3. Livestock farming affects humans.** Deforestation (H1), emissions (H7), antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases that pass from animals to humans are human problems.

**4. The argument often serves to end the conversation.** Even when it comes from someone devoted to another cause, it's still a false choice: not eating animals doesn't compete with any other struggle.

**5. Many people champion several causes.** Animal advocates also work for human rights, against hunger and for social justice.

**6. Taken seriously, priority may point to the animals.** There is a moral theory that gives the idea of priority a precise form: prioritarianism, associated with the philosopher Derek Parfit, who presented it in 1991 in the lecture "Equality or Priority?". On this theory, a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient is. It doesn't say "humans first", but "the worse off first". What counts is the level of well-being, not species. If the principle is applied to all sentient beings, priority may fall on the animals: more than 80 billion land animals are killed for food every year, plus a very uncertain number of fish, estimated in the hundreds of billions or more, and many of them spend their lives in confinement, in intense pain and deprivation. There is disagreement here: prioritarianism is usually formulated for human persons only; the *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* acknowledges that this restriction is problematic because it excludes animals and treats the extension as an open question; comparing well-being across species is hard; and some authors defend different weights for humans and animals. But whoever appeals to priority can't, without further argument, stop at the species line. The philosopher Catia Faria, for example, argues that egalitarians and prioritarians are committed to rejecting speciesism and to giving priority to the interests of the animals who are worst off.

**7. Historically, the causes have gone together.** In the 19th century, William Wilberforce, who fought slavery, was among the founders of the British animal protection society that would become the RSPCA, and Lord Shaftesbury, who championed the laws protecting factory workers, also campaigned against cruelty to animals. The Humanitarian League, founded by Henry Salt, defended social reform and compassion for all sentient beings at the same time. John Stuart Mill, Gandhi and Salt himself show that compassion can extend to several causes without contradiction (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 102, 104–105). The opposite criticism also has a history: part of the left, such as the Marxist philosopher Howard Parsons (1977), saw animal protection as a diversion of energy that should go to human struggles. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane replies that real conflicts between the vital interests of humans and animals are rare, and that the greatest animal suffering, that of intensive farming, can be tackled without sacrificing any vital human interest (pp. 104–105).

**8. Even giving humans much more weight, scale matters.** Luciano Carlos Cunha proposes a test: suppose a human being's suffering or death counted ten times more than an animal's. About 60 million people die each year worldwide. Animal farming alone slaughters more than 80 billion land animals a year, and fishing catches, on average, 1.1 to 2.2 trillion wild fish a year (Mood and Brooke, 2024). With numbers like these, even a tenfold weight for humans isn't enough to make animals a lower priority, if what counts is how much harm can be prevented. In this sense, even those who accept that humans count more would have reasons to pay far more attention to animals than is paid today (Cunha, 2022, pp. 127–129). The reasoning doesn't say each person should drop other causes, and point 2 still holds: not eating animals takes resources from no cause.

**9. Proportion bias.** We tend to prefer solving a large fraction of a small problem to solving a small fraction of an enormous one, even when the second option prevents far more suffering. Solving 1% of a problem that affects trillions may mean more victims spared than solving all of a problem that affects thousands. What matters is how much harm is prevented, not what percentage of the problem is solved (Cunha, 2022, p. 230).

**10. Singer opened his chapter on animals with this objection.** In *Practical Ethics*, Peter Singer begins the discussion of equality for animals with the question of how anyone can spend time on animals when equality is denied to so many humans. He likens that attitude to the prejudice that led slaveholders not to take seriously the interests of the people they enslaved (ch. 3). He also holds that changing our attitude toward animals would reduce total suffering so much that it's hard to imagine any other moral change with a comparable effect, a claim that depends on scale and on comparing suffering across species, and that is contested by those who give priority to humans. Singer also links the two causes: in the chapter on rich and poor, he notes that the grain and soy fed to animals would be enough to end hunger if distributed, but adds that hunger is mainly a problem of distribution and poverty, not of production (ch. 8). Today's data keep the picture: about 36% of the calories from the world's crops go to feed (Cassidy et al., 2013), and about 673 million people faced hunger in 2024, according to the annual report by FAO and other UN agencies. Changing diets doesn't end hunger on its own, since hunger depends on income, conflict and access to food, but it doesn't compete with fighting it either.

**11. Only one cause gets asked.** People devoted to the elderly don't usually hear "why aren't you saving children?", and people who work with children aren't told they should be fighting malaria. Among human causes, it is accepted that each person chooses theirs out of affinity, talent or life history, and nobody demands proof that they picked the most serious one in the world. The demand shows up almost only when the cause is animals. It is the [fallacy of priority](../falacias.html#priority-fallacy), also called relative privation, applied with a double standard: in conversation it usually takes the form of whataboutism, where instead of answering the question, someone points to another. One plausible explanation is that the question starts from a speciesist premise, that animal suffering counts for little or nothing, and often works as self-defense: if the animal cause is disqualified, there is no need to think about what one eats. This is what psychology describes as motivated reasoning in the face of the meat paradox (Bastian and Loughnan, 2017). It doesn't apply to everyone: some people ask in good faith, out of real dedication to another cause, and for them points 2 and 5 apply. The same uneven demand appears in attacks on vegans (L1) and in the war version (J21).

**12. By number of victims, animal exploitation is by far the largest oppression caused by humans.** To the numbers in point 8 we must add farmed fish: an estimated 78 to 171 billion were killed for food in 2019, with a midpoint of 124 billion, not counting those that die during rearing (Mood et al., 2023). Adding land animals, farmed fish and wild fish, that comes to somewhere between 1.3 and 2.5 trillion animals killed per year, most of them fish, in a world of just over 8 billion people. No other form of violence or domination carried out by humans comes close to this scale in number of victims. As for total suffering, it is plausible that it is also the largest, but here the comparison is more uncertain: measuring the suffering of individuals of different species depends on assumptions about each one's capacity for good and bad experiences, and projects that try to estimate these "welfare ranges", such as Rethink Priorities' Moral Weight Project (2023), work with very wide margins of uncertainty and contestable philosophical assumptions. The point is not that human victims matter less, nor to set up a competition between victims; it is to recall the scale, which the phrase "there are more important problems" usually ignores.

**13. Human crises and animal exploitation are linked, but animals count in themselves.** The philosopher Jeff Sebo, in *Saving Animals, Saving Ourselves* (2022), shows that destroying habitats, selling live animals and using antimicrobials on farms increase the risk of pandemics, and that animal exploitation contributes to climate change, which in turn harms humans and animals. That is why many measures serve both at once. But Sebo insists that animals should be included for their own sake too, not only as a means of protecting humans: public health is one more reason, not the main one, and using it alone falls into the mistake of "Going vegan prevents pandemics", in Stop saying this.

**14. "But what about protein? And the price? And the climate?"** These questions have answers, and the guide answers each of them in other chapters. The problem is when they take the place of the main one: is it fair to make animals suffer and die for this? Luciano Carlos Cunha shows that debates about animals tend to drift toward health, economics and the environment, and that speciesism, the underlying question, is almost never discussed ([Questão de Ética, vol. VIII, ch. 6](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v8/06-shifting-the-focus-in-debates-why-is-speciesism-rarely.html)).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: animal activism takes energy

The most serious version grants that eating plants doesn't compete with other causes, but says animal activism takes time and money that could go to human causes.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** The question was about eating animals, not about activism.
2. **All causes compete for time.** And no one demands that only the most serious be championed.
3. **Animal suffering is enormous.** Billions of sentient beings a year, which makes the cause relevant. By the standard of giving priority to the worse off, it may even be among the most urgent.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument doesn't say animals don't matter; it says other things matter more. But no longer paying for animals to be killed takes nothing away from those other things. The moral question remains: if it harms no one, why not stop?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J20 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Priority fallacy | If there's something more serious, the rest doesn't matter |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either humans or animals |
| Logical | Double standard | Only animal advocates are asked to justify their choice of cause |
| Epistemological | Ignoring connections | Doesn't see the link between livestock and human problems |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Devotes time to things less serious than hunger.** Such as hobbies, sport or local politics.
2. **Isn't fighting hunger.** Uses the argument without acting.
3. **Cares about cruelty to dogs.** Without thinking it distracts from human causes.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "as long as there are bigger problems, smaller ones don't matter".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fighting racism | It wouldn't matter while there's hunger | No one accepts this |
| Protecting children | It wouldn't matter while there are wars | No one accepts this |
| Cruelty to dogs | It wouldn't matter | Those who use the argument disagree |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Human problems are serious and urgent, and no one should be indifferent to hunger, poverty and violence. It's also true that some people present the animal cause in ways that seem to disdain humans, which is a mistake.

But no longer eating animals doesn't compete with any of those causes. It's possible to care for people and animals at the same time, and often one helps the other.
