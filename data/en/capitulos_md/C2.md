# C2. "Lions eat other animals"

**Thesis:** lions aren't moral agents and have no alternative. They don't deliberate, don't understand consequences and would die without hunting. We deliberate and have an alternative. We don't take our ethics from lions in anything else: they also kill the cubs of other males, and no one uses that as an example.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's how nature works. Are you going to ask the lion to go vegan?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many animals kill and eat other animals.
2. **P2.** We don't morally condemn those animals for it.
3. **P3.** Humans are animals too.
4. **C.** So there's no reason to condemn humans for eating animals.

The strong version draws on a real intuition: it would be strange to condemn a lion for hunting. The problem lies in P3, which treats the fact that we're animals as if it erased the difference that justifies not condemning the lion.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because the reason we don't condemn the lion, its lack of deliberation and of an alternative, doesn't apply to us.

## 3. Answer for children

The lion doesn't know there's another option. It doesn't go to the store, can't cook beans and needs to hunt to survive. You're different: you can choose what to eat and understand what happens to animals. No one gets angry at a baby who knocks over a cup, because the baby doesn't understand. It's the same with the lion: it doesn't choose. We do.

## 4. Short answer

The lion has no choice: it's an obligate carnivore, it doesn't deliberate and it would die without hunting. We can live well without eating animals and we understand what we do. That's why we don't judge the lion, and it's for the same reason that we can be judged. Besides, we don't copy the lion in anything else: male lions kill the cubs of other males, and no one draws a moral rule from that.

**Question to ask back:** "In what other area of life do you use the lion as an example of what's right?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The difference between the lion and the human isn't whether they are animals, but two capacities:

| Capacity | Lion | Human |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliberating about one's own actions and their consequences | No | Yes |
| Living well without killing animals | No: it's an obligate carnivore | Yes, for anyone with access to a varied diet |

These two capacities are exactly what make someone a **moral agent**. The lion is a moral patient, who can be harmed, but not an agent who can be held responsible (A7).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The lion can't choose.** It doesn't know alternatives exist, can't weigh consequences and wouldn't survive without hunting. Moral responsibility requires the capacity to choose.

**2. We can.** Well-planned vegan diets are appropriate for humans at all stages of life, according to the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics. The condition that excuses the lion doesn't apply to us.

**3. We don't copy the lion in anything else.** Male lions that take over a pride often kill the cubs of the previous males. No one draws permission for humans from that. Choosing to imitate the lion only in diet is convenient selection.

**4. It's an appeal to nature.** The argument assumes that what happens in nature is a moral model, which chapter C1 shows to be false.

**5. Lions don't confine or breed animals for slaughter.** Even the descriptive comparison fails: industrial animal farming looks nothing like predation.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: we're natural predators

The most serious version doesn't say we should imitate the lion. It says that we, like the lion, are part of a chain of predation, and that meat is part of human history and biology. Asking us to stop eating animals would be asking us to stop being the kind of animal we are.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It turns into another argument.** It becomes C1 ("it's natural"), C3 ("we have canine teeth") or C4 ("we're at the top of the food chain"), each with its own answer.
2. **Being able to do something doesn't oblige us to do it.** We're able to do many things we choose not to do.
3. **Our history includes ethics too.** The capacity to question what we do is as much part of who we are as the capacity to eat meat.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | Takes another species' behavior as a moral model |
| Logical | False analogy | Compares someone with no choice to someone with a choice |
| Epistemological | Convenient selection | Imitates the lion only where it confirms the habit |
| Moral | Confusing agent and patient | Treats beings without the capacity to choose as a reference for those who have it |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't copy the lion in anything else.** Not in violence, not in infanticide, not in social life.
2. **Uses medicine, clothes and cooking.** None of that is what a lion would do.
3. **Wouldn't let their cat decide the household's ethics.** Animal behavior is never a criterion on other matters.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what other animals do is allowed for us".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infanticide | It would be allowed, because lions practice it | No one accepts this |
| Violent territorial disputes | Killing intruders would be legitimate | Contradicts all of law |
| Cannibalism | It would be acceptable, because it occurs in several species | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The intuition that it makes no sense to condemn a lion for hunting is correct. It shows something important: moral responsibility depends on the capacity to choose and to understand. It's also true that we're animals and that our evolutionary history includes eating meat.

But it's precisely that intuition that brings the argument down. We don't condemn the lion because it can't choose. We can, and that's why the question applies to us.
