# C2. "We have canine teeth; we are omnivores"

**Thesis:** being able to eat meat doesn't mean needing to eat it, let alone being obliged to. Humans are omnivores: we can digest both plants and animals. It's exactly that flexibility that makes it possible to live well on plants alone. Anatomy says what we can do, not what is right to do. And we're able to think about our choices: we can avoid suffering that doesn't need to be caused and include in our moral concern beings who have done no one any harm.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Look at my teeth. We have canines; we're omnivores. We were made to eat meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Human anatomy and physiology make it possible to digest and use animal foods.
2. **P2.** These traits evolved because our ancestors ate meat.
3. **P3.** What the body is adapted to is legitimate.
4. **C.** So eating meat is legitimate for humans.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3, which moves from describing the body to a rule of conduct.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3: it's an appeal to nature (C1) and a move from "is" to "ought".

## 3. Answer for children

Your teeth are for biting lots of things: apples, carrots, bread, nuts. Having strong teeth doesn't mean you have to bite everything you can. We can also climb trees, and that doesn't mean we have to live in them. The body shows what we're able to do, and our mind helps us choose what's good to do. If we can eat well without hurting any animal, why hurt one?

## 4. Short answer

We're omnivores, and that's exactly why you can choose: you can live well on plants or on animals. Being able to eat meat isn't needing to, and needing to isn't being obliged to. Your canines are small compared with a carnivore's and are also used for biting fruit and vegetables. And anatomy gives no moral orders. Besides, you're able to think about what you do: you can avoid suffering you don't need to cause and include in your moral concern animals who have done no one any harm.

**Question to ask back:** "If we're omnivores, doesn't that mean we can also live on plants alone?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument runs three different claims together as if they were one:

| Claim | True? |
| --- | --- |
| We can digest meat | Yes |
| We need to eat meat | No, for anyone with access to a varied diet |
| We ought to eat meat | It doesn't follow from either of the above |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Omnivore means flexible.** Being an omnivore means being able to get nutrients from varied sources. That includes the possibility of getting them from plants alone, as the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics recognizes in considering well-planned vegan diets adequate.

**2. Can isn't ought.** The human body is capable of many things we choose not to do. Anatomy describes abilities, not obligations. It's the move from "is" to "ought" (C1).

**3. We're also able to think about what we do.** Anatomy doesn't show the capacity we rely on most to decide how to act: examining our reasons. We can ask whether a choice causes suffering that doesn't need to be caused, and change course when it does. It's also by thinking this way that we've come to include in our moral concern those who were once left out. The image of a moral circle that widens comes from the Australian philosopher Peter Singer, in the book *The Expanding Circle* (1981), who credits that widening largely to our capacity to reason. Nothing prevents that circle from including animals who can suffer and who have done no one any harm. Canines show that we can bite into meat; thinking is what lets us ask whether we should.

**4. Human canines aren't a carnivore's.** Human canines are small and used for biting a variety of foods. Other primates with large canines, like gorillas, eat a mostly plant-based diet.

**5. We eat meat cooked and processed.** Most of the meat we eat goes through fire, knives, seasoning and industry. The anatomical argument talks about the body but describes a practice that depends on technology, not on anatomy.

**6. The argument ignores today's context.** The question isn't what our ancestors could eat, but what we, with supermarkets and alternatives, ought to eat.

**7. You can't get an ought from an is.** From the fact that the body can digest meat, the argument concludes that eating it is right. But a fact, by itself, doesn't say what is right: reaching a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, and that premise is what needs defending. This leap is [Hume's law](../falacias.html#hume-s-law-from-is-to-ought); see also [Describing is not evaluating](../comece-aqui.html#descrever), in Start here.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: adaptation points to the ideal diet

The most serious version says anatomy indicates the diet the body is best adapted to, and that ignoring it can carry health risks. A diet without meat would need supplements, like B12, precisely because it isn't the "natural" diet.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It changes the subject.** It becomes a health argument (family G), and the evidence shows well-planned vegan diets are adequate.
2. **Supplementing doesn't invalidate it.** Farmed animals also get supplemented B12 in their feed, and we use supplements and fortified foods in many other situations (G4).
3. **Adaptation explains; it doesn't oblige.** Even if meat were ideal for the body, that wouldn't say whether it's just to get it at the cost of others' lives.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hume's law (from is to ought) | From "the body can" it concludes "it's right" |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "omnivore" as if it meant "carnivore" |
| Epistemological | Misread anatomy | Takes small canines as proof of a carnivorous diet |
| Moral | Appeal to nature | Takes biology as a moral rule |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't follow anatomy in anything else.** We can do many things we don't do.
2. **Doesn't eat raw meat.** The human body depends on fire and cooking to eat meat safely.
3. **Recognizes that omnivores can choose.** No one thinks an omnivore is obliged to eat everything.
4. **Uses reason to decide everything else.** No one picks a job, friends or laws by looking at their own teeth.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what the body can do is legitimate".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical violence | It would be legitimate, because the body can do it | No one accepts this |
| Eating human flesh | It would be legitimate, because the body can digest it | No one accepts this |
| Plant-based diets | They'd also be legitimate, because the body can manage them | The argument doesn't exclude a vegan diet |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The description is correct: humans are omnivores, can digest meat, and our anatomy carries the history of a varied diet. It's also true that nutrition matters and that a diet without meat takes some planning.

But anatomy says what we can do, not what we should. And, ironically, being an omnivore is exactly what makes it possible to choose to live on plants alone.
