# E2. "The state can't ban what I eat"

**Thesis:** the argument changes the question. The debate was "is eating animals wrong?", and it becomes "should the state ban it?". They're different questions: many wrong things aren't banned, and the answer to the second doesn't answer the first. Besides, the state already regulates what we eat, and the liberal harm principle, which protects individual freedom, was formulated precisely for cases where there's no victim.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans want to ban meat. Vegan dictatorship. The state doesn't get to run my plate."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In a free society, the state shouldn't interfere with personal choices that don't harm other people.
2. **P2.** What each person eats is a personal choice.
3. **C.** So the state can't ban eating meat.

P1 is a version of John Stuart Mill's harm principle, a pillar of liberalism. The problem lies in P2: eating meat affects animals and, in several ways, other people. And even if the conclusion were accepted, it wouldn't answer whether eating meat is morally right.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, and the argument answers a different question from the one being debated.

## 3. Answer for children

Some things aren't against the law but still aren't nice to do, like lying to a friend. The question "is it forbidden?" is different from "is it right?". When we talk about animals, the first question is whether it's right to hurt them. Whether the law should change is another conversation.

## 4. Short answer

No one here is talking about arresting meat eaters. The question was whether it's right, not whether it should be banned. Many wrong things aren't banned, like lying to a friend. And the state already regulates what we eat: there are banned foods, health rules and laws against animal cruelty. The liberal principle that protects freedom, the harm principle, applies to choices that don't harm others, and animals are those others.

**Question to ask back:** "Leaving the law aside: do you think it's right to make an animal suffer when there's an alternative?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument swaps one question for another:

| Question | Type | Example of an answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is eating animals wrong? | Moral | The subject of this guide |
| Should the state ban it? | Political and legal | Depends on many factors: feasibility, transition, consensus |

Answering "no" to the second doesn't answer the first. Many animal advocates don't even call for bans, but for voluntary change, subsidies and gradual transitions.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It changes the subject.** The moral debate doesn't depend on the legal one. Lying, cheating or being cruel with words are often legal and wrong.

**2. The state already regulates food.** There are health bans, labeling rules, bans on eating certain animals, such as protected wild species, and laws against animal cruelty (D9). The idea that one's plate is untouchable doesn't describe reality.

**3. The harm principle favors animals.** Mill argued that individual freedom may only be limited to prevent harm to others. The argument assumes eating meat harms no one, but the animals are harmed, and there are also effects on the climate, public health and the emergence of diseases (family H).

**4. "Vegan dictatorship" is a straw man.** It attributes to vegans a proposal for an immediate ban that many don't support, and answers that instead of the moral argument. And even for those who do call for laws, the moral question comes first.

**5. Protections have always limited freedoms.** Laws against cruelty to dogs limit what owners may do. No one calls that a dictatorship.

| Source | What it says | Note |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: change should be voluntary

The most serious version grants that eating animals may be wrong, but says change should come from individual conscience, not the force of the state, because bans breed resistance and black markets.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It accepts the moral question.** If change should be voluntary, it should happen, and it's up to each person to make it.
2. **It's a discussion of strategy, not justification.** One can debate the best means without denying the end.
3. **Voluntary isn't neutral.** States today subsidize animal farming; removing subsidies isn't banning anything.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Changing the subject | From "is it wrong?" to "should it be banned?" |
| Logical | Straw man | Attributes to vegans a proposal for an immediate ban |
| Epistemological | Not knowing how food is regulated | Ignores that the state already regulates food |
| Moral | Freedom without a victim | Applies the harm principle while ignoring who is harmed |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports laws against cruelty to dogs.** Which limit owners' freedom.
2. **Accepts health rules.** Which limit what can be sold and eaten.
3. **Doesn't think everything legal is right.** The moral question remains open.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "the state can't interfere with what I do with my money and time, even if there's a victim".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dogfighting | It couldn't be banned | It's a crime |
| Buying protected wild animals | It would be free | It's a crime |
| Child labor | The state couldn't ban those who hire children | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Concern for individual freedom is legitimate, and authoritarianism is a real risk. Ill-conceived bans can be unjust and ineffective, and cultural change tends to last longer when it's voluntary.

But the question "should it be banned?" doesn't replace "is it right?". Even those who defend total freedom of choice have to decide what they will choose.
