# C6. "It's the circle of life"

**Thesis:** "circle of life" is a euphemism. Breeding, confining and slaughtering billions of animals a year is an industry, not a natural cycle. That everyone dies someday doesn't make it acceptable to kill someone before their time: all humans die too, and that has never justified killing anyone.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's the circle of life. Everything dies someday; it's part of life."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In nature, the life of some depends on the death of others, in a cycle of birth, death and renewal.
2. **P2.** Eating animals is taking part in that cycle.
3. **P3.** What is part of the natural cycle of life is not morally wrong.
4. **C.** So eating animals is not wrong.

The strong version has an almost spiritual appeal: there is something true in the idea that life and death are linked. The problem lies in P2, which describes animal farming as if it were nature, and in P3, which is an appeal to nature.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

All living things are born, grow and one day die. That's the circle of life. But it doesn't mean someone can decide when another will die. Farm animals don't die because they got old: they're raised to die while they're still young. That isn't nature's cycle; it's a choice people make.

## 4. Short answer

That everyone dies someday doesn't make it acceptable to kill someone before their time: all humans die too, and that has never justified killing anyone. And animal farming isn't a natural cycle: animals are artificially inseminated, raised in sheds, bred to grow fast and slaughtered young, by the billions every year. Calling that the circle of life is giving a pretty name to an industry.

**Question to ask back:** "If we're all going to die someday, does killing a person stop being wrong?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument describes animal farming in the language of nature:

| The natural cycle | Animal farming |
| --- | --- |
| Spontaneous births | Artificial insemination and hatcheries |
| Death from age, disease or predation | Scheduled slaughter, while still young |
| Balance between populations | Billions of animals bred to meet demand |
| Animals adapted to their environment | Animals bred to produce, with the health problems that follow (B7) |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The inevitability of death doesn't justify killing.** We all die, and killing someone is still one of the worst harms. What matters isn't whether death happens, but when, and by whose decision (B2).

**2. Animal farming isn't natural.** Farmed animals were shaped by artificial selection, are born by insemination, live in built environments and die on an industrial schedule. In 2021, about 73.8 billion chickens were slaughtered worldwide. None of this exists in nature.

**3. It's a euphemism.** Words like "cycle", "processing" and "harvest" soften the description of what happens. The argument works less as a reason and more as verbal anesthesia.

**4. It's an appeal to nature.** Even if animal farming were natural, that wouldn't make it right (C1).

**5. In the natural cycle, death doesn't have to be caused by us.** Today we can live well without taking part in that "cycle" as predators.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: taking part in nature with respect

The most serious version comes from traditions that see hunting and husbandry as respectful relationships with nature, in which the animal is thanked and nothing is wasted. The problem would be the industry, not taking part in the cycle.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It isn't what people buy.** Supermarket meat comes from industry, not from a ritual relationship with nature (B4).
2. **Respect doesn't undo the harm.** Thanking the animal doesn't change what happens to it.
3. **For those with an alternative, taking part is a choice.** And choices can be morally assessed.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Persuasive definition | Calls an industry a "cycle" |
| Logical | Non sequitur | From "everyone dies" it concludes "killing is allowed" |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | Takes the natural cycle as a moral rule |
| Epistemological | Wrong description of animal farming | Presents as natural something built by technology |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept someone killing a person because everyone dies.**
2. **Grieves the early death of loved ones.** Even knowing death is inevitable.
3. **Doesn't live the natural cycle in anything else.** Medicine, houses and supermarkets take human life far from the natural cycle.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "since everyone dies, causing death is part of the cycle".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Humans | Killing a person would be part of the circle of life | No one accepts this |
| Pets | Killing a healthy dog would just be part of the cycle | No one accepts this |
| Any violence | It would be natural, because death is natural | The principle dissolves all of ethics |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There's something true and even beautiful in the idea of a cycle: life depends on other lives, death is part of existence, and accepting one's own mortality is a sign of maturity. Many traditions express this with depth.

But accepting that death exists isn't accepting causing unnecessary deaths. Nature's cycle doesn't need slaughterhouses, and the wisdom of accepting death doesn't extend to deciding the deaths of others.
