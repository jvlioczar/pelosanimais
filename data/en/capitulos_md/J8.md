# J8. "I don't want to feel left out; eating together is how you belong"

**Thesis:** the need to belong is real, and eating together is one of the rituals that bind people most. But what binds is the table, the conversation and the time spent together, not identical contents on every plate. You can be present with different food. The discomfort of being "the odd one out" exists, is greatest at the start and fades with time and support. And it doesn't compare with what the meal costs the animal, who loses their life.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I don't want to be the odd one out at the table. Eating is what we do together. If I don't eat the same thing, I'm on the outside."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Belonging to a group is a basic human need, not a whim.
2. **P2.** Eating together is one of the main rituals through which groups form and hold together.
3. **P3.** Anyone who refuses the group's food risks being seen as an outsider, and research shows that vegetarians and vegans are evaluated negatively.
4. **C.** So, to protect my belonging, I'm right to keep eating what the group eats.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and supported by research. The problem lies in the step to the conclusion: belonging doesn't require eating the same dish, and the social cost, however real, is weighed alone, without what is on the other side.

The argument is **invalid**: what follows from the premises is that the social cost must be taken seriously, not that it settles the question.

## 3. Answer for children

At snack time, everyone opens their own lunchbox. One brought a sandwich, another brought fruit, another brought cake. No one stops being friends because of that. What makes a group a group is sitting together, talking and laughing. You can be part of the group while eating something different.

## 4. Short answer

Wanting to belong is human, and I want it too. But what brings us together is the table, not matching plates. I still go to the barbecue, the birthday and the Sunday lunch, with my own food. It's uncomfortable at first, then it passes, and people get used to it. And there's someone in this sum who isn't heard: for the animal, the cost of the meal is their whole life.

**Question to ask back:** "If a friend of yours stopped drinking, would you drop them from the group or just order them a juice?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument bundles three things that can be separated:

| What the group offers | Does it depend on eating the same dish? |
| --- | --- |
| Being together, talking, laughing, sharing memories | No |
| Taking part in the ritual (Sunday lunch, the barbecue, the party) | No: you can take part with different food |
| Not standing out, not being the subject of remarks | Partly, especially at the start |

Only the third item is tied to the plate, and it is the one that has to be weighed against what happens to the animal.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The need to belong is real.** In 1995 the psychologists Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary gathered evidence that the need for bonds is a strong and general human motivation. And the British anthropologist Robin Dunbar found, in a national survey in the United Kingdom, that people who eat with others more often say they are happier and more connected to their community. There is nothing trivial about this concern.

**2. The stigma is real too.** In 2017 the psychologists MacInnis and Hodson showed that omnivores evaluated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups that are common targets of prejudice. In 2019 the sociologists Kelly Markowski and Susan Roxburgh ran focus groups with university students in the United States: the non-vegans expected to be judged and excluded if they changed, and the vegans confirmed that this had happened to them. It's a small, qualitative study, but it shows clearly the fear this argument speaks of.

**3. Belonging doesn't require the same dish.** What studies of eating together measure is company. People with diabetes, allergies, celiac disease or religious restrictions sit at the same table every day (J4 covers the practical side: restaurants, travel and parties).

**4. The discomfort fades, and support makes a difference.** In the Faunalytics survey of former vegetarians and vegans in the United States, 63% disliked that their diet made them stick out, and 84% took part in no vegetarian or vegan group or community. A later study by the same organization, which followed people at the start of the change, found that social strategies (such as asking friends and family for support or joining a community) were the most useful against the widest range of barriers. Having a group helps, and that group can be a new one (M3 covers the case of family).

**5. The costs aren't the same size.** On one side there are remarks, jokes and some awkwardness, which tend to fade. On the other there is the life of a being who feels. Comparing the two and choosing the first only seems natural because the second isn't at the table to object.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: for some people, the group is all they have

The most serious version says: not everyone can choose another group. In small towns, in closed families, in certain workplaces or in cultures where refusing food is a serious offense, standing out can cost a great deal, and loneliness does real harm.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That calls for care in the how, not for giving up.** Veganism speaks of doing what is possible and practicable. Someone in a hard situation can go slowly, pick their battles and look for support elsewhere.
2. **The hard case isn't the common case.** Most people who use the argument have friends who would accept the change after a few jokes.
3. **Groups change from within.** The person who stays at the table, without fighting and without giving in, is often the one who most changes what the others eat. Leaving the group or eating the same aren't the only options.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument is about what a person feels at the table. It's a legitimate feeling, but the conversation about animals isn't about it. On the other side of the meal there is someone who also wanted to stay with their own, and who was killed to be on the plate. The question is whether the fear of being seen as different is enough to justify that. When the same fear shows up elsewhere (laughing at a cruel joke so as not to stand out, for example), we usually say that it explains the behavior but doesn't justify it.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either I eat the same as everyone, or I'm left out |
| Logical | Appeal to popularity and normality | What the group does becomes the criterion for what I should do |
| Epistemological | Overestimating the cost | Imagines exclusion as certain and permanent |
| Moral | Social comfort as justification | The discomfort of the eater weighs more than the life of the eaten |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't exclude friends who eat differently.** The colleague with an allergy, the friend who doesn't drink and the cousin who fasts are still in the group.
2. **Has already stood apart from the group over smaller things.** Football team, religion, vote.
3. **Says friendship is what matters.** But assumes it wouldn't survive a different plate.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if refusing a group practice threatens my belonging, I'm right to keep it up".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laughing at racist jokes in the group | It would be justified, so as not to stand out | No one accepts this as a justification |
| Drinking unwillingly because everyone drinks | It would be the right thing to do | Today people who don't drink are respected |
| Violent hazing of newcomers | Taking part would be justified, in order to belong | We understand the fear, but don't approve of the act |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that belonging is a need, not fussiness, and that the table is one of the places where it is met. It's right that stigma against vegans exists and has been measured. And it's right to point out that changing costs more for those with a thinner support network: anyone who answers "just don't care" hasn't understood the problem.

But the limit lies in what is asked in exchange. The social cost falls on the person who changes, tends to fade and can be shared with others. The cost to the animal is total and can't be undone. Being part of the group is possible without someone dying for it.
