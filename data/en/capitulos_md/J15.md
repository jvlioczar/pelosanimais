# J15. "Veganism has become big business"

**Thesis:** there is indeed a market for vegan products, with marketing, high prices and meat companies among the owners. But the fact that someone profits from an idea says nothing about whether it is true or false. Veganism is the principle of not exploiting animals, not a product catalog: rice, beans and vegetables aren't a luxury niche. And if the problem is that there's a business, the business of exploiting animals is incomparably larger. In 2024 a single meat company took in more than twice the entire world's retail sales of plant-based alternatives.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That's just a business now. It's marketing to sell expensive plant burgers to people with money."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There is now an industry of vegan products, with brands, labels, investors and advertising.
2. **P2.** Many of those products are expensive and aimed at higher-income consumers.
3. **P3.** Many of the companies that sell them are the same ones that sell meat, and a vegan label doesn't guarantee an ethical supply chain.
4. **P4.** A movement driven by commercial interests doesn't deserve moral trust.
5. **C.** So veganism needn't be taken seriously as an ethical position.

P1, P2 and P3 are largely true. The problem lies in P4 and the conclusion: the origin or commercial use of an idea doesn't settle whether it is right, and veganism isn't reducible to the products sold in its name.

The argument is **invalid**: from "someone profits from this" it doesn't follow that "this is false".

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "It's just marketing to sell expensive products." |
| Everyday conversation | "Veganism is a market niche for people with money." |

## 3. Answer for children

Some shops sell T-shirts with a cute animal and a nice slogan just to make money. That doesn't mean caring for animals is a lie. Caring for animals is one thing, selling T-shirts is another. And to stop eating animals, no one needs to buy anything expensive: rice, beans, bananas and carrots will do.

## 4. Short answer

There is business around veganism, true, and there are expensive products I don't even buy. But an idea doesn't become wrong because someone makes money from it: there's business around health, education and recycling too. Veganism means not exploiting animals, and you can do that with rice and beans. And if profit is the problem, the meat business is far bigger: a single meat company takes in more than twice the world's entire retail market for plant-based alternatives.

**Question to ask back:** "If tomorrow no one made a profit from vegan products, would killing animals for food become more right or more wrong than it is today?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The criticism comes from several directions. In Brazil, criticisms like this appear in the comments of figures such as the philosopher and newspaper columnist Luiz Felipe Pondé, who said on a radio show in 2019 that veganism today is "business, like Louis Vuitton, usually for people with money, and that's fine". He added that he isn't against veganism but sees a moralistic streak in it (the subject of L8).

The argument mixes three things under one name:

| What gets called "veganism" | What it actually is | Does the "business" criticism reach it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ethical position | Refusing, as far as possible and practicable, the exploitation of animals | No |
| The everyday diet | Grains, pulses, vegetables, fruit, nuts | No: these are ordinary, cheap foods |
| The plant-based market | Processed plant-based meats, cheeses and milks | Yes, in part |

The criticism applies to the third row and is presented as if it brought down the first.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's a genetic fallacy, with guilt by association.** Judging an idea by who profits from it swaps examining the idea for examining its company. There are businesses around vaccines, schoolbooks and solar panels, and none of that shows that vaccinating, studying or using clean energy is a mistake.

**2. Veganism isn't a catalog.** The Vegan Society, the British organization that coined the term, defines veganism as a philosophy and way of living that seeks to exclude, as far as is possible and practicable, the exploitation of animals (L24). Someone who eats rice, beans, potatoes and greens and has never bought a plant-based burger is as vegan as anyone else. A 2021 study of 150 countries found that vegetarian and vegan diets based on ordinary foods were among the cheapest in high-income and upper-middle-income countries (J1; on access, see J2).

**3. The business of exploiting animals is far bigger.** According to the Good Food Institute, using data from the market research firm Euromonitor, worldwide retail sales of plant-based meat, seafood, milk, yogurt, ice cream and cheese totaled 28.6 billion US dollars in 2024, of which 6.1 billion was plant-based meat and seafood. In the same year JBS alone, the world's largest meat company, reported net revenue of 77.2 billion dollars. Beyond Meat, one of the best-known vegan brands, took in 326.5 million. The world produces more than 360 million tonnes of meat a year, and the sector receives far more public money and does far more lobbying than the alternatives (J10).

**4. Meat companies selling plant-based products shows something else.** JBS owns the Dutch company Vivera, which in 2025 bought The Vegetarian Butcher brand from Unilever. That shows that companies follow demand, not that the demand is fake. Anyone who dislikes funding those companies has a simple way out: eat food that has no brand.

**5. The vegan label has limits, and vegans know it.** A product can contain no animal ingredients and still have labor or environmental problems in its supply chain. That calls for more attention, not for a return to animal products, which have the same problems plus the killing of the animals (K5).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the market hollows out the cause

The most serious version doesn't say veganism is false. It says that, by becoming a marketable lifestyle, it loses force: a person buys a plant-based burger, feels they've done their part and stops there. The cause becomes a consumer identity for those who can pay, and the animals drop out of the picture.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a criticism of a deviation, not of the principle.** Many vegans make the same criticism. It calls for a veganism centered more on animals and less on products.
2. **Products also reduce harm.** Someone who swaps meat for a plant-based substitute, for whatever reason, stops paying for slaughter. The effect on the animal doesn't depend on the purity of the intention (L17).
3. **Every cause that grows attracts commerce.** It happened with environmentalism, with healthy eating and with rights movements. In no case did the cause become wrong because of it.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Suppose the entire vegan market is opportunistic. Animals still feel pain and fear, and are still killed by the billions. The question about them isn't answered by any company's balance sheet. Anyone who distrusts the market can ignore it completely and still stop eating animals. What justifies killing them is a question that stands with or without a shelf of vegan products.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J15 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges the idea by who profits from it |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Links veganism to luxury brands and meat companies |
| Logical | Equivocation | Calls the plant-based product market "veganism" |
| Epistemological | Cherry picking | Looks at the vegan sector's profit and ignores the meat sector's |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the rule to what they consume.** Meat is a business too, with much bigger marketing, brands and lobbying.
2. **Says "and that's fine".** If there's no problem with its being a business, the fact can't serve as an objection.
3. **Criticizes the price of vegan products.** But doesn't count the rice, beans and vegetables already on their plate.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there's a profitable market around an idea, the idea doesn't deserve to be taken seriously".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar energy | Caring about the climate would be just marketing | No one concludes this |
| The gym and supplement industry | Exercise wouldn't be good for you | The business doesn't change the fact |
| Recycling | Sorting waste would be pointless, because companies profit from scrap | No one concludes this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that there is a market, that it advertises, that many products are expensive and that part of vegan communication looks like a shop window for people with money. It's right that a vegan label doesn't guarantee a clean supply chain and that there is opportunism from companies that go on profiting from slaughter. It's a criticism the movement would do well to hear.

But it reaches the market, not the principle. Ceasing to exploit animals doesn't require buying anything special, and what happens to animals doesn't change according to who profits. If the existence of a business disqualified a practice, the first to fall would be animal farming.
