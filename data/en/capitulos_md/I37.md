# I37. "Science shows animal testing works, so it's ethical"

**Thesis:** these are two different questions. Whether an animal test produces useful knowledge is a scientific question, and the honest answer is mixed: a 2024 review, pooling 122 systematic reviews, found that 86% of positive results in animals were confirmed in human studies, but that only 5% of therapies tested in animals went on to be approved for human use. Whether it's right to carry out the test is another question, which no data answers on its own: from the fact that something works it doesn't follow that it ought to be done. The ethics committees for animal research exist precisely because the question is moral, and regulation is changing: since 2022 US law no longer requires animal testing for new drugs, and in 2025 the FDA announced a plan to replace it with other methods.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The people who understand this are the scientists, and they say testing works. Being against it is being against science."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Research on animals has produced, and continues to produce, knowledge and treatments that benefit humans and animals.
2. **P2.** The scientific community and regulators, who know the subject best, consider testing necessary and submit it to ethics committees.
3. **P3.** A practice that brings great benefits and is approved by experts and by ethics committees is ethically justified.
4. **C.** So animal testing is ethical.

P1 is partly true: some tests have brought benefits (I1), but how well they translate to humans is debated. P2 describes the institutions well, but the committees exist precisely because the question isn't only technical. The central problem is P3: it's a moral premise, not a scientific finding. It's the premise that needs defending, and whoever defends it is doing ethics, not science.

The argument is **valid** with P3. Without P3, which usually stays implicit, it isn't: from facts about benefits and approvals, no permission follows on its own. Its **soundness** depends on P3, which proves too much: it would also justify experiments on humans who don't consent, if they brought great benefits and were approved by experts.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone discovers that, by copying a classmate's test, they get a better mark. That "works": the mark goes up. But working doesn't mean it's right. To know whether it's right, you need to ask another question: is it fair to the classmate? It's the same with animals. Scientists can tell us whether a test works, but the question "is it fair to the animal?" belongs to all of us.

## 4. Short answer

Whether a test works and whether it's right are different questions. Science answers the first, and the answer is mixed: a 2024 review showed that positive results in animals are usually repeated in the first human studies, but that only 5% of therapies tested in animals end up approved. No data answers the second question on its own. Experiments on people without consent would also "work", and we ban them for ethical reasons, not scientific ones. So much so that Brazilian law puts a representative of animal protection societies on the committees that approve animal research.

**Question to ask back:** "If an experiment on people who hadn't consented gave excellent results, would that make it ethical?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument lumps together questions of different kinds:

| Question | Kind | Who can answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does this test produce information that applies to humans? | Empirical | Science, with data |
| Are the expected benefits large? | Empirical, with estimates | Science, with uncertainty |
| Is it permissible to impose this harm on this animal to obtain these benefits? | Moral | Anyone who reasons with arguments; science informs but doesn't decide |

The philosopher Nathan Nobis has a section titled "Science does not answer moral questions": a benefit only justifies a practice together with a moral principle, and writing the argument out validly exposes the leap from fact to permission (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 61).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. "It works" doesn't yield "it ought".** David Hume observed, in the *Treatise of Human Nature*, that many authors move from claims about what is to claims about what ought to be without explaining how. This is known as [Hume's law](../falacias.html#hume-s-law-from-is-to-ought). To go from "the test brings benefits" to "the test is permissible", a moral premise is needed, and once it is written down it can be examined like any other.

**2. The hidden premise proves too much.** "Whatever brings great benefits and is approved by experts is ethical" would also approve experiments on humans who don't consent. The Tuskegee study was run by the United States Public Health Service from 1932 to 1972: hundreds of Black men with syphilis were followed without informed consent and without treatment, even after penicillin became available. The ethical rules for research on humans, such as the Belmont Report (1979), arose partly in reaction to cases like this, and they don't only ask whether the research works.

**3. The scientific answer is mixed, and should be presented fairly.** A review published in 2024 in *PLOS Biology* pooled 122 systematic reviews covering 54 human diseases and 367 therapies. Of the therapies tested in animals, 50% reached some human study, 40% reached randomized controlled trials and 5% reached approval. The concordance between positive results in animals and in clinical studies was 86%. The authors conclude that translation may be higher than is often claimed, but that the low approval rate suggests problems in the design of both animal studies and early clinical trials. Another analysis, from 2022, estimates that about 90% of drug candidates entering clinical trials fail (I1). Defenders of testing tend to cite the 86%, and critics the 5%. Neither number decides the moral question.

**4. The ethics committees show that the question is moral.** In Brazil, the Arouca Law (Law No. 11,794/2008) requires the Animal Use Ethics Committees to include, besides vets, biologists and researchers, a representative of animal protection societies. The 3Rs principle, of replacing, reducing and refining the use of animals, proposed by William Russell and Rex Burch in 1959, assumes that the animal's suffering counts. These committees weigh harms and benefits, which is a moral judgment, but they were created to decide how and under what conditions research may be done, not to reopen the question of whether using animals is permissible in principle.

**5. "Necessary" changed by decision, not by discovery.** The FDA Modernization Act 2.0, of December 2022, removed the legal requirement for animal testing of new drugs in the United States. In April 2025, the FDA announced a plan to replace animal testing in the development of monoclonal antibodies and other drugs with computational models, organoids and organs-on-chips. In Brazil, Law No. 15,183/2025 added to the Arouca Law a ban on using live vertebrates in tests of cosmetics, perfumes and personal hygiene products. What was "required by science" depended largely on regulatory choices (see I1, point 9).

**6. Being against testing isn't being against science.** The question "is it permissible?" denies no data. Many critics are scientists who develop methods without animals. Confusing a moral position with a denial of facts is the same mistake as in E5, only in reverse.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: society has delegated this decision to those who understand it

The most serious version says: there's no leap from fact to value, because scientists and committees apply an ethics, that of welfare and the 3Rs, and society has approved it in law. It would be worse if every layperson judged technical experiments they don't understand: experts know better than anyone how to assess the pain of a procedure, the need for a test and the available alternatives. Delegating to those who know is a reasonable division of moral labour.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Trusting experts about facts is reasonable; about the moral premise, it isn't.** They know better how much a procedure hurts and what alternatives exist. But whether a benefit justifies harm to someone who hasn't consented isn't a technical competence. The Arouca Law itself recognizes this by including a representative of animal protection societies on the committees.
2. **The ethics of the 3Rs already assumes the answer.** It asks how to reduce and refine use, not whether use is permissible. That is a moral position, welfarism, which may be right or wrong, but needs to be defended like any other (B18).
3. **Those who decide usually gain from the answer.** This isn't an accusation of bad faith, but a reminder of why independent review exists. The same goes for other technical authorities, such as animal scientists (B9).
4. **Approved law can change, and does.** In 2022 and 2025, the rules changed in the United States and Brazil after public debate. If legal approval settled the question, these changes wouldn't have happened.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Science can and should tell us how much animal testing helps and how much it fails. The question that remains is another one: is it fair to make a being who feels fall ill, suffer and die, without consent, for the benefit of others? That question belongs to no profession. It's a moral question, and everyone can and should think about it with arguments.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept that "it works" justifies experiments on humans without consent.** And accepts it for animals.
2. **Says the decision belongs to scientists.** But doesn't give the same weight to scientists who develop and advocate methods without animals.
3. **Treats the legal requirement as proof of necessity.** But the requirement changed in the United States in 2022 by political decision, not because of a discovery about animals.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a practice works and is approved by experts, it's ethical".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Experiments on humans without consent | They'd be ethical if they gave good results and were approved | The Tuskegee study was run by a public health agency for 40 years |
| Torture in interrogations | It would be permissible if it produced information | The ban is moral, not technical |
| Child labour in an efficient factory | It would be ethical because it works | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that science matters: some animal tests have brought benefits, and critics shouldn't say they never worked (I1). It's also right that scientists know more about the facts, and that ethics committees and the 3Rs have reduced suffering.

But knowing whether something works is different from knowing whether it's right. The first question belongs to science; the second belongs to everyone, and it remains open as long as the harm to those who didn't consent isn't justified.
