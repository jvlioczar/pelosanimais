# E11. "I love animals, but..."

**Thesis:** the "but" reveals the contradiction. Loving some animals while paying for others, just as able to suffer, to be killed is what psychology calls the meat paradox. The sentence isn't an argument: it's the description of a conflict between what the person values and what they do. And that conflict is actually the best starting point for change.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I have three dogs. I'm an animal lover, but I won't give up meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** I love and care for several animals.
2. **P2.** Eating meat is a separate practice, tied to food rather than affection.
3. **P3.** I can love animals and eat meat without contradiction, as many people do.
4. **C.** So eating meat doesn't contradict my love of animals.

P1 is sincere. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the split between animals "for affection" and animals "for food" doesn't correspond to any difference in the animals themselves.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which treats a category created by habit as a real difference.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone who says: "I really love my classmates, but I push some of them." Strange, right? If we love animals, it makes sense to think about whether the pig and the chicken also deserve that love. They feel fear and pain just like the dog at home.

## 4. Short answer

Loving animals is real and a great start. But pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like the dogs and cats you love. The difference between them isn't in the animals, but in the place habit has given each one. Psychology calls this the meat paradox. If the love of animals is sincere, it's the best reason to extend that care to those on the plate.

**Question to ask back:** "What does a pig have that's different from your dog that justifies eating one and loving the other?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The sentence puts together a value and a practice that pull in opposite directions:

| What the person values | What the person does |
| --- | --- |
| That animals don't suffer | Pays for practices that make animals suffer |
| Caring for the animals at home | Funds the slaughter of others with similar capacities |
| Being outraged by cruelty | Accepts practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The difference is in the habit, not the animal.** Pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like dogs and cats. Comparisons of intelligence between species are imprecise, and not even needed: what matters is the capacity to suffer. The split between "pet" and "food" is cultural: in other countries, different animals sit on each side.

**2. It's the meat paradox.** Psychology has studied this conflict for over a decade. Research shows people attribute less mind and less moral status to the animals they eat, and do so more when reminded of the link between meat and suffering.

**3. The category changes perception.** In one experiment, classifying an animal as food was enough to lower the moral status attributed to it. The animal doesn't change; the way we see it does.

**4. Love is a starting point.** The sentence isn't a defense; it's the recognition of a conflict. Those who love animals already accept the most important premise: that they matter.

**5. The "but" usually hides another argument.** After the "but" usually comes taste (J5), custom (D1) or priority (A12). Those are the arguments that need examining.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) | Eating meat reduces the moral status and mind attributed to animals | A classic study on the meat paradox |
| [Bastian et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21980158/) | Being reminded of the link between meat and suffering increases the denial of mind to animals | Shows the defense mechanism |
| [Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569805/) | Classifying an animal as food lowers the moral status attributed to it | The category changes perception |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: love is always partial

The most serious version says all love is partial: we love our family more than strangers, and no one finds that contradictory. Loving one's own animals more than others would be equally natural.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Loving more isn't licensing harm.** We love our family more than strangers, but we don't pay for strangers to suffer (A12).
2. **Partiality explains care, not exploitation.** Not caring for everyone is different from funding the suffering of some.
3. **The counter-reply concedes the central point.** If the issue is just how intensely one loves, the other animals still deserve, at least, not to be harmed.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Arbitrary criterion | The difference between "pet" and "food" isn't in the animals |
| Epistemological | Compartmentalization | Keeps affection and consumption in separate boxes |
| Moral | Contradiction between value and practice | Values animal welfare and funds the opposite |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Would be horrified if someone ate their dog.** But accepts eating animals with similar capacities.
2. **Condemns cruelty to dogs.** And funds practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs.
3. **Says they love "animals", not "some animals".** The sentence already contains the contradiction.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains E11 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat paradox | The conflict is reduced by denying minds to food animals | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| Functional categorization | "Food" and "companion" seem like different kinds of animal | [Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569805/) |
| Mind denial | Being reminded of suffering leads to attributing less mind | [Bastian et al., 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21980158/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "I can love one category of beings and fund the suffering of another similar one".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dogs and cats in other countries | Eating them would be acceptable for someone who has other dogs | Those who say this usually condemn it |
| People | Loving one's own family would license exploiting strangers | No one accepts this |
| Pet pigs | They'd be loved at home and eaten on the farm | Same animal, two categories |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Loving animals is sincere and valuable. People who care for dogs and cats, rescue animals or are outraged by cruelty already show that animals matter to them. That affection is a real force, not hypocrisy.

What the sentence reveals isn't an excuse but a conflict. And conflicts like this are often the beginning of change: all it takes is extending the same love to the animals still on the other side of the line.
