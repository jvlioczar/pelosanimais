# E11. "I love animals, but..."

**Thesis:** the "but" reveals the contradiction. Loving some animals while paying for others, just as able to suffer, to be killed is what psychology calls the meat paradox. Pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like dogs and cats; there is good evidence that fish feel pain, strong evidence for octopuses, crabs and lobsters, and growing but still uncertain evidence for insects. The sentence isn't an argument: it's the description of a conflict between what the person values and what they do. And that conflict is actually the best starting point for change: the love of animals opens the door, but the reason to extend care to those on the plate is that they feel in the same way, not the affection they inspire.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I have three dogs. I'm an animal lover, but I won't give up meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** I love and care for several animals.
2. **P2.** Eating meat is a separate practice, tied to food rather than affection.
3. **P3.** I can love animals and eat meat without contradiction, as many people do.
4. **C.** So eating meat doesn't contradict my love of animals.

P1 is sincere. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the split between animals "for affection" and animals "for food" doesn't correspond to any difference in the animals themselves.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which treats a category created by habit as a real difference.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone who says: "I really love my classmates, but I push some of them." Strange, right? We don't let anyone hurt the dog at home because it feels fear and pain. The pig and the chicken feel fear and pain in the same way, so they also deserve not to be hurt.

## 4. Short answer

Your love of animals is real and a great start. But pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like the dogs and cats you love. There is good evidence that fish feel pain, and many invertebrates, such as octopuses and crabs, may also have negative experiences. The difference between them isn't in the animals, but in the place habit has given each one. Psychology calls this the meat paradox. What matters isn't affection but the fact that all of them feel: that's what calls for extending that care to those on the plate, not justifying a poor, short life for them.

**Question to ask back:** "Your dog feels fear and pain, and so does the pig. What changes, to eat one and protect the other?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The sentence puts together a value and a practice that pull in opposite directions:

| What the person values | What the person does |
| --- | --- |
| That animals don't suffer | Pays for practices that make animals suffer |
| Caring for the animals at home | Funds the slaughter of others with similar capacities |
| Being outraged by cruelty | Accepts practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The difference is in the habit, not the animal.** Pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like dogs and cats. Comparisons of intelligence between species are imprecise, and not even needed: what matters is the capacity to suffer. The split between "pet" and "food" is cultural: in other countries, different animals sit on each side.

**2. The circle is wider than it seems.** The capacity to suffer doesn't end with mammals and birds. There is good evidence of pain in fish, though some researchers still dispute it. A review of over 300 studies carried out for the UK government in 2021 found strong evidence of sentience in octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters. For insects the evidence is growing, but it is more uncertain and varies widely between groups. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) sums up this picture: consciousness is a "realistic possibility" in all vertebrates and in many invertebrates, and ignoring that possibility in decisions affecting these animals would be irresponsible.

**3. It's the meat paradox.** Psychology has studied this conflict for over a decade. Research shows people attribute less mind and less moral status to the animals they eat, and do so more when reminded of the link between meat and suffering.

**4. The category changes perception.** In one experiment, classifying an animal as food was enough to lower the moral status attributed to it. The animal doesn't change; the way we see it does.

**5. Love is a starting point.** The sentence isn't a defense; it's the recognition of a conflict. Those who love animals already accept the most important premise: that they matter.

**6. The "but" usually hides another argument.** After the "but" usually comes taste (J6), custom (D1) or priority (A12). Those are the arguments that need examining.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: love is always partial

The most serious version says all love is partial: we love our family more than strangers, and no one finds that contradictory. Loving one's own animals more than others would be equally natural.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Loving more isn't licensing harm.** We love our family more than strangers, but we don't pay for strangers to suffer (A12).
2. **Partiality explains care, not exploitation.** Not caring for everyone is different from funding the suffering of some.
3. **The counter-reply concedes the central point.** If the issue is just how intensely one loves, the other animals still deserve, at least, not to be harmed.

| Level | Error | How it appears in E11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Arbitrary criterion (speciesism) | The difference between "pet" and "food" isn't in the animals |
| Epistemological | Compartmentalization | Keeps affection and consumption in separate boxes |
| Moral | Contradiction between value and practice | Values animal welfare and funds the opposite |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Would be horrified if someone ate their dog.** But accepts eating animals with similar capacities.
2. **Condemns cruelty to dogs.** And funds practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs.
3. **Says they love "animals", not "some animals".** The sentence already contains the contradiction.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains E11 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "I can love one category of beings and fund the suffering of another similar one".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dogs and cats in other countries | Eating them would be acceptable for someone who has other dogs | Those who say this usually condemn it |
| People | Loving one's own family would license exploiting strangers | No one accepts this |
| Pet pigs | They'd be loved at home and eaten on the farm | Same animal, two categories |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Loving animals is sincere and valuable. People who care for dogs and cats, rescue animals or are outraged by cruelty already show that animals matter to them. That affection is a real force, not hypocrisy.

What the sentence reveals isn't an excuse but a conflict. And conflicts like this are often the beginning of change: all it takes is extending the same care to the animals still on the other side of the line, because they feel in the same way.
