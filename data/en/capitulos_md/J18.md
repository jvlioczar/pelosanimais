# J18. "Veganism is a political ideology or a globalist agenda"

**Thesis:** the argument assesses no reason at all; it just associates veganism with something the person rejects. It's guilt by association. Veganism has supporters on the left, on the right and outside politics, and it rests on a simple question: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? Conspiracy theories about "making us eat bugs" don't answer that question.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's a leftist thing. They want us to eat bugs. They want to control what you eat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some international organizations and governments propose reducing meat consumption.
2. **P2.** These proposals are part of broader political agendas I disagree with.
3. **P3.** Those who promote veganism are, knowingly or not, serving these agendas.
4. **C.** So veganism should be rejected as a political instrument.

P1 is true in some cases. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: an idea's origin or allies say nothing about whether it's correct.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, and the argument is a genetic fallacy and guilt by association.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes, when we don't like an idea, we say it's "something people we don't like believe". But that doesn't show whether the idea is right or wrong. Veganism's question is simple: is it fair to hurt animals when we don't need to? People of every kind can answer that question.

## 4. Short answer

Saying an idea is "leftist" or "globalist" doesn't show it's wrong; it's guilt by association. Veganism has supporters on the left, on the right and outside politics. Its question is simple: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? No one is proposing to force anyone to eat insects. Disagreeing with governments or organizations doesn't answer the question about the animals.

**Question to ask back:** "If someone you like defended veganism, would the answer to the question about the animals change?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument swaps assessing an idea for assessing its supposed allies:

| What the argument does | What it should do |
| --- | --- |
| Associates veganism with rejected groups | Assess veganism's reasons |
| Attributes hidden intentions | Examine the facts about the animals |
| Rejects by origin | Judge by content |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's guilt by association.** An idea doesn't become wrong because people we dislike defend it.

**2. It's the genetic fallacy.** An idea's origin doesn't determine its truth.

**3. Veganism cuts across parties.** There are vegans of many political positions, and concern for animals appears in very different religious, philosophical and cultural traditions.

**4. The conspiracy theories have no basis.** There's no proposal to force populations to eat insects. There is research on alternative protein sources, which is something else.

**5. There are right-wing vegans, and animal protection laws passed by conservative governments.** On average, people further to the right eat more meat and give up vegetarianism more often, but the same research shows there are conservative vegetarians and vegans. In the United Kingdom, the law recognizing animals as sentient beings was passed in 2022 by a Conservative government. The [Political objections](../objecoes-politicas.html) page shows that neither liberalism, nor libertarianism, nor left-wing theories offer, on their own terms, a reason to exclude animals.

**6. There's no imposition under way.** No government is forcing anyone to be vegan. Arguing that exploiting animals is wrong isn't the same as imposing a law, and the debate about what the state should do is a different one (E2).

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Hodson and Earle, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2017.08.027) | People further to the right eat more meat and are more likely to lapse from vegetarianism, but the sample includes conservative vegetarians and vegans | *Appetite*, 120 |
| [United Kingdom, Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2022/22/contents) | Recognizes vertebrate animals, octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters as sentient beings | Passed under a Conservative government |
| [Pew Research Center, diet in India (2021)](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/07/08/eight-in-ten-indians-limit-meat-in-their-diets-and-four-in-ten-consider-themselves-vegetarian/) | 39% of Indian adults describe themselves as vegetarian, largely for religious reasons | Shows that refusing meat doesn't belong to one political camp |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: there are real public policies against meat

The most serious version points out that governments and organizations discuss targets for reducing meat consumption, and that being wary of those policies is legitimate.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Being wary of policies is different from rejecting the moral question.** One can disagree with a policy and still stop eating animals.
2. **Individual choice doesn't depend on policy.** No one needs a government to change their own plate.
3. **The question about the animals remains.** No political wariness answers it.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument shifts the focus from the animals to politics. But the pig, the hen and the cow belong to no party. The moral question is the same for anyone, of any persuasion: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J18 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Guilt by association | Rejects the idea because of its supposed allies |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges the idea by its origin |
| Epistemological | Conspiratorial thinking | Attributes hidden intentions without evidence |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Knows vegans of different political positions.** Or could.
2. **Doesn't reject other ideas because of who defends them.** When they agree with them.
3. **Values freedom of choice.** And veganism is a choice.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an idea is defended by groups I dislike, it's wrong".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protecting dogs | It would be wrong if defended by political opponents | No one concludes this |
| Fighting corruption | It would be wrong if defended by the other side | No one concludes this |
| Veganism | It would be wrong by association | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's legitimate to be wary of policies imposed from above, and some proposals for dietary change can be poorly communicated or authoritarian. It's also true that the subject has been politicized, and that vegans sometimes reinforce that polarization.

But the question about animals comes before party politics. Anyone can examine it, and its answer doesn't depend on who asks it.
