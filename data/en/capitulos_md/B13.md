# B13. "A lobster boiled alive feels nothing and tastes fresher"

**Thesis:** there is serious evidence that decapod crustaceans, such as lobsters and crabs, feel pain. A review of more than 300 studies led the United Kingdom to recognize them as sentient in 2022, and Switzerland has required since 2018 that they be stunned before being killed and banned transporting them alive on ice. Boiling the animal alive is choosing the slowest way to kill it for the sake of freshness. Stunning reduces suffering at the moment of death, but it doesn't solve the main problem: killing an animal who can feel, without need, is still unjust.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Lobster has to be boiled alive, it's fresher. And it doesn't feel anything, it's basically a sea insect."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Crustaceans have a much simpler nervous system than mammals.
2. **P2.** Without a complex nervous system, there is no pain.
3. **P3.** Boiling alive guarantees freshness and food safety.
4. **C.** So boiling lobsters alive is acceptable.

P1 is true. P2 is the premise in dispute, and the evidence points against it. P3 has some truth about freshness, but it doesn't require boiling a conscious animal.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2.

## 3. Answer for children

Lobsters and crabs move and try to escape when they're put into hot water. Scientists studied them and found that they probably feel pain. That's why some countries require them to be knocked out first, instead of being boiled alive. That makes it hurt less, but the animal still loses its life.

## 4. Short answer

The evidence indicates that lobsters and crabs feel pain: a review of more than 300 studies led the United Kingdom to recognize them as sentient in 2022. Switzerland has required since 2018 that they be stunned before being killed and banned transporting them alive on ice. Boiling the animal alive is choosing the slowest way to kill it, and freshness doesn't depend on it. And stunning first reduces suffering, but it doesn't make killing it just.

**Question to ask back:** "If the lobster feels nothing, why does it try to escape the pot?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument assumes | What the evidence shows |
| --- | --- |
| No cortex, no pain | Animals with other nervous structures show signs of pain |
| Movement in the pot is a reflex | Crustaceans learn to avoid harmful stimuli and trade off benefits to avoid them |
| Boiling alive is necessary | There are electrical or mechanical stunning methods before cooking |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The evidence of pain is serious.** The review commissioned by the UK government from the London School of Economics assessed more than 300 studies and concluded there is strong evidence of sentience in decapod crustaceans and cephalopods. On that basis, the UK's 2022 animal sentience law included these animals (A2).

**2. Countries have already changed the rules.** Switzerland, since March 2018, requires lobsters to be stunned before being killed, by electric shock or mechanical destruction of the brain, and banned transporting them on ice. It's progress, because it reduces suffering at the moment of death. But the rule still treats the essential point as normal: killing, without need, an animal for whom there is serious evidence of feeling. A less painful death is still an imposed death, and stunning answers the how, not the whether (B1).

**3. Freshness doesn't require cruelty.** Stunning immediately before cooking keeps the freshness. The tradition of boiling alive is habit, not necessity.

**4. Doubt calls for care.** Even for those who still have doubts, the cost of being wrong is high: if the animal feels, it goes through a slow and painful death.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: science isn't certain yet

The most serious version points out that some researchers, such as those at the University of Maine's Lobster Institute, doubt that lobsters process pain.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Uncertainty doesn't license the worst method.** Faced with doubt, choosing the slowest way to kill is betting against the animal.
2. **The weight of evidence has shifted.** Governments that reviewed the evidence began protecting these animals.
3. **The underlying question remains.** Even stunned, the animal is killed for a dish no one needs to eat.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Requiring a specific structure for pain | Only the mammalian cortex would count |
| Epistemological | Ignoring the accumulated evidence | Treats as settled a conclusion science has revised |
| Moral | Freshness above suffering | A matter of taste justifies the slowest death |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the lobster doesn't feel.** And explains away the noise in the pot to feel better.
2. **Wants the animal fresh.** And rejects the stunning that would keep it fresh.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same with a cat.** Boiling an animal alive would be obvious cruelty.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an animal has a simple nervous system, any way of killing it is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Octopuses, which also lack a cortex | They could be boiled alive | They're recognized as sentient in the same review |
| Fish | They could die by slow asphyxia | See I25 |
| The Swiss stunning requirement | It would be unnecessary | It was based on evidence of pain, though it still allows killing |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that crustaceans are very different from us, that there is still scientific debate about their experience, and that freshness matters for food safety.

But the latest evidence led governments to protect them, and freshness doesn't require boiling the animal alive. When in doubt, care is the reasonable choice, and the next question is whether it needs to be killed at all.
