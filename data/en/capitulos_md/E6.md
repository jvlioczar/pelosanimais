# E6. "Even philosophers don't agree"

**Thesis:** disagreement isn't a tie. Philosophers disagree about almost everything, including how to ground human rights, and no one concludes that anything goes. On the question of animals there is strong convergence: the main ethical theories condemn unnecessary suffering, and it's hard to find a philosopher who defends factory farming as it exists. Among specialists in ethics, most consider eating mammal meat morally bad.

**Family:** E. Relativism, freedom and evasions · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "It's an open debate. Some philosophers defend eating meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Competent philosophers disagree about the moral status of animals.
2. **P2.** When experts disagree, laypeople can't be required to adopt a position.
3. **C.** So everyone can carry on with their practice, including eating meat.

P1 is true: there is disagreement, especially about details. The problem lies in P2: disagreement among experts doesn't make all positions equally reasonable, and the points of convergence are already enough to condemn most of what happens.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, and the argument ignores what philosophers have in common.

## 3. Answer for children

Doctors sometimes disagree about which medicine is best. But they all agree you shouldn't give a patient poison. It's similar with philosophers: they disagree about many details, but almost all agree that making animals suffer without need is wrong.

## 4. Short answer

Philosophers disagree about almost everything, and no one concludes from that that anything goes. On the question of animals there's a strong point of convergence: the main ethical theories condemn unnecessary suffering, and it's hard to find anyone who defends factory farming as it exists. In a survey of ethics professors in the United States, about 60% rated regularly eating mammal meat as morally bad.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you know any philosopher who defends factory farming as it is today?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats every disagreement as a tie. But there are degrees of agreement:

| Question | Is there convergence? |
| --- | --- |
| Is causing animals unnecessary suffering wrong? | Yes, broad |
| Does factory farming cause unnecessary suffering on a large scale? | Yes, broad |
| Is killing an animal painlessly wrong? | More disagreement |
| Do animals have rights in the same sense as humans? | Theoretical disagreement |

Disagreement on the lower questions doesn't affect the conclusion of the upper ones, which is already enough to condemn almost all the meat that is consumed.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Disagreement isn't a tie.** Every field of knowledge has disagreements, and that doesn't make all positions equivalent. Philosophers also disagree about how to ground human rights.

**2. The main theories converge.** Utilitarianism condemns unnecessary suffering; rights theories condemn using sentient beings as means; virtue ethics condemns cruelty; contractualism, in its recent versions, has been extended to animals. By different routes, they reach similar conclusions.

**3. Ethics specialists lean one way.** In a survey of US professors, about 60% of ethics specialists rated regularly eating mammal meat as morally bad, compared with about 20% of professors in other fields.

**4. Defenses of factory farming are rare.** Even philosophers who defend eating meat usually defend only forms very different from current production, such as animals raised with a good life and killed painlessly (B3).

**5. The argument usually serves to end the conversation.** But when you look at what philosophers say about animals, the conclusion is clearer than the phrase suggests.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Schwitzgebel, *The Moral Lives of Ethicists*](https://faculty.ucr.edu/~eschwitz/SchwitzPapers/EthicalEthicists-150715.pdf) | About 60% of ethics specialists rated regularly eating mammal meat as morally bad | A survey done with Joshua Rust; the same study shows their behavior didn't change to the same extent |
| [PhilPapers Survey 2020](https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/all) | About 26% of philosophers accept or lean toward vegetarianism as an ethical position, and 18% toward veganism | The question is about what is permissible to eat; together the two positions reach about 44% |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: even the experts eat meat

The most serious version points out that, in the same study, ethics specialists ate meat almost as much as other professors. If not even those who study the subject change, the moral conclusion can't be that clear.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Behavior doesn't measure truth.** The study shows that knowing what's right isn't enough to change, not that the conclusion is wrong (E15).
2. **The counter-reply confirms the convergence.** The specialists think it's wrong, even if not all of them change.
3. **The difficulty of changing is a practical problem.** It's a reason to create conditions for change, not to deny the problem.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in E6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False balance | Treats any disagreement as a tie |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the debate | Ignores the points of convergence |
| Epistemological | Disagreement as absence of an answer | Concludes there's no answer because there's discussion |
| Moral | Using disagreement as an excuse | Doubt becomes permission |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't use disagreement on other subjects.** Philosophers disagree about how to ground human rights, and no one concludes that anything goes.
2. **Wouldn't use the argument for cruelty to dogs.** Even though some philosophers deny we have direct duties to animals.
3. **Hasn't read the philosophers they cite.** The argument usually comes without any author.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains E6 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | Disagreement is remembered only where it excuses change | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Status quo bias | When in doubt, the current practice is kept | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if experts disagree, I can do as I please".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Climate change | One dissenting scientist would be enough to ignore the problem | No sensible person accepts this |
| Human rights | Philosophical disagreement would be enough to disregard them | No one accepts this |
| Medicine | One dissenting doctor would be enough to refuse any treatment | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that there is philosophical disagreement about animals, and that it's serious: there are legitimate discussions about the value of animal life, the strength of duties and borderline cases. Recognizing this is a sign of intellectual honesty.

But the disagreement is in the details, not at the center. Almost everyone agrees that unnecessary suffering is wrong, and that's already enough to condemn most of what happens to animals today.
