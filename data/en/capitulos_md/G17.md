# G17. "The WHO exaggerates; red meat isn't bad for you"

**Thesis:** the argument picks the convenient studies and ignores the whole. The WHO cancer agency's classification was based on more than 800 studies: processed meat is carcinogenic to humans, and red meat is probably carcinogenic. The WHO also recommends limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of calories. Nutrition studies have limits, but the answer to those limits is to weigh all the evidence, not to discard what's inconvenient.

**Family:** G. Health and nutrition · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Nutrition studies are all observational. Saturated fat has been cleared. Meat doesn't cause cancer."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Many nutrition studies are observational and don't prove causation.
2. **P2.** Some recent reviews have questioned the role of saturated fat and red meat.
3. **P3.** The increase in individual risk attributed to meat is small.
4. **C.** So the recommendations against red meat are exaggerated.

P1 is true. So is P2: there are reviews that dispute it. P3 is true in absolute terms. The problem lies in the conclusion: the existence of uncertainty doesn't cancel the weight of the body of evidence, and small risks per person add up to a lot across a population.

The argument is **valid** as a criticism of exaggeration. It fails when it concludes that red and processed meat carry no risk.

## 3. Answer for children

Scientists from around the world looked at lots of research on meat and health, more than 800 studies. They saw that eating a lot of processed meat, like sausages and ham, increases the chance of getting a type of cancer. That doesn't mean everyone will get sick, but that the risk goes up.

## 4. Short answer

The WHO's cancer agency reviewed more than 800 studies before classifying processed meat as carcinogenic and red meat as probably carcinogenic. Every 50 grams of processed meat a day was linked to an 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer. The WHO also recommends limiting saturated fat. Nutrition studies have limits, but the answer is to weigh the whole body of evidence, not to pick only the studies that confirm what you want to eat.

**Question to ask back:** "If you distrust observational studies, which studies do you rely on to say meat isn't bad for you?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument selects criticisms and ignores the whole:

| What the argument highlights | What it leaves out |
| --- | --- |
| Observational studies don't prove causation | Several lines of evidence converge: cohorts, mechanisms, trials |
| Some reviews dispute it | Health organizations maintain their recommendations |
| The individual risk is small | Small risks add up to millions of cases across a population |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The classification is based on many studies.** The WHO cancer agency's working group, with 22 experts from 10 countries, reviewed more than 800 studies before reaching its conclusion.

**2. The classification measures the strength of evidence, not the size of the risk.** Being in the same group as tobacco doesn't mean being as dangerous as tobacco, and the WHO itself clarified this. It means the evidence that it causes cancer is strong.

**3. The saturated fat recommendation stands.** In 2023, the WHO published a guideline recommending that saturated fat be limited to less than 10% of calories.

**4. Small risks add up.** An 18% increase in the relative risk of colorectal cancer may be small for one person, but it represents many cases across a whole population.

**5. Picking only the criticisms is cherry picking.** Every field has dissenting studies. Using them to discard the whole is the same method used to deny consensus in other fields.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [IARC, press release no. 240 (2015)](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr240_E.pdf) | Processed meat is carcinogenic (group 1) and red meat is probably carcinogenic (group 2A); every 50 g of processed meat a day linked to 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer | Based on more than 800 studies |
| [WHO, guideline on saturated and trans fat (2023)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073630) | Recommends limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of calories | Official guideline |
| [GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators, *The Lancet* (2019)](https://healthdata.org/research-analysis/library/health-effects-dietary-risks-195-countries-1990-2017-systematic-analysis) | Dietary risks linked to about 11 million deaths in 2017 | Most came from too much salt and too little whole grain and fruit; red and processed meat weigh less. It shows that diet matters, not that meat is the main risk |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some reviews say the evidence is weak

The most serious version cites reviews that rated the evidence against red meat as low certainty, and that concluded most people can keep eating meat.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Low certainty isn't absence of risk.** The same reviews found associations in the same direction.
2. **Those reviews were heavily criticized.** Health organizations kept their recommendations.
3. **The moral question doesn't depend on this.** Even if meat were harmless to whoever eats it, it isn't harmless to the animal.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The debate about the risk meat poses to those who eat it is important, but it's about the eater's health. The animals die either way. Even if meat did no harm at all, the question would remain: is it fair to kill them when we don't need to?

| Epistemological | Selective skepticism | Demands definitive proof only against meat |
| Logical | Confusing strength of evidence with size of risk | Ridicules the comparison with tobacco |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts observational studies when they favor meat.** But rejects them when they don't.
2. **Trusts the WHO on other subjects.** Such as vaccines and tobacco.
3. **Doesn't demand decades-long clinical trials for other risks.** No one expects a randomized trial on smoking.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains G17 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation bias | Accepts only the studies that confirm the habit | General literature on confirmation bias |
| Motivated reasoning | A taste for meat makes doubt more attractive | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Overconfidence (Dunning-Kruger) | The opinion of someone who has read little challenges systematic reviews | [Kruger and Dunning, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1121) |
| Belief perseverance | The belief that meat carries no risk holds even in the face of the body of evidence | [Ross, Lepper and Hubbard, 1975](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.32.5.880) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if there are dissenting studies, the evidence can be discarded".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tobacco | For decades there were dissenting studies, funded by the industry | Today no one denies the risk |
| Climate change | One dissenting study would be enough to ignore it | No sensible person accepts this |
| Sugar | The industry also funded studies that played down the risk | The pattern repeats |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that nutrition studies have limitations, that headlines exaggerate, and that the individual risk of meat is smaller than that of tobacco. Skepticism toward alarmism is healthy, and there are legitimate debates about the size of the effects.

But the body of evidence points to real risks from processed and red meat, and the main health organizations maintain their recommendations. And even if meat did no harm to whoever eats it, the harm to the animal would remain.
