# N5. "Who are we to interfere with nature?"

**Thesis:** we are the same people who interfere with nature all the time, with cities, farming, medicine and environmental programmes. Helping an injured or sick animal isn't arrogance, just as helping a person isn't. And the "freedom" being protected is often just the freedom to die young of hunger or disease.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Wild animals must be free. Who are we to decide what's good for them?"

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Wild animals have a way of life of their own, independent of humans.
2. **P2.** Respecting a being includes respecting its autonomy and way of life.
3. **P3.** Intervening to help them replaces their way of life with our values, creates dependence and is paternalistic.
4. **C.** So respecting wild animals means not interfering.

P1 and P2 contain some truth. The problem lies in P3: not being hungry, cold and sick isn't a human value imposed on animals; it's what any animal seeks.

The argument is **valid**, but P3 is false in many cases.

## 3. Answer for children

If a little bird falls out of its nest and breaks a wing, is taking it to a vet being bossy? Of course not. It wants to fly again, and we're just helping. Respecting animals isn't leaving them to suffer. It's helping in the way they'd want, if they could ask.

## 4. Short answer

We interfere with nature all the time, for ourselves. Helping a sick animal isn't arrogance, just as helping an injured person isn't. No animal wants to die of hunger or disease; helping it with that doesn't impose human values, it respects the animal's own interests.

**Question to ask back:** "Is helping an unconscious person, who can't consent, arrogance or respect?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument gathers several objections with the same background: the idea that helping is disrespecting.

| Objection | What it says | Short reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrogance | We have no right to decide for them | We already decide, including when we do nothing |
| Freedom | Helping takes away their freedom | Freedom from what? From hunger and disease? |
| Humanization | Helping imposes human values | Not suffering isn't a human value; it's what every animal seeks |
| Dependence | Helping creates dependence | Dependence is better than death; and much help, like vaccination, creates no dependence |
| Paternalism | We don't know what's good for them | We know no animal prefers to starve |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. We already interfere all the time.** Farming, cities, roads, dams, reforestation, reintroduction and elimination of species. We rarely call this arrogance. The charge appears almost only when the intervention aims at the good of the animals themselves.

**2. Not deciding is also deciding.** Faced with a suffering animal we could help, omission also determines its fate. There is no neutral position.

**3. The interests are the animals', not ours.** Avoiding hunger, cold, pain and fear is what animals themselves seek. Helping them with that is taking their point of view seriously, not ours.

**4. The human test.** We help unconscious people, babies and people who can't consent without calling it paternalism, because we know they don't want to suffer. No one says vaccinating children "takes away their freedom". Denying help to animals with these arguments is applying a rule we don't apply to humans.

**5. Respecting a way of life doesn't require accepting suffering.** It's possible to respect how an animal lives and, at the same time, spare it disease, parasites and hunger. No one thinks treating a sick dog disrespects its dog nature.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the sovereignty of wild communities

The most serious version comes from Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka: wild animal communities should be treated as sovereign, like peoples with a right to follow their way of life without domination. Constant interventions would make them dependent, which would be a form of colonization.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The authors themselves accept help.** Donaldson and Kymlicka accept interventions in disasters and specific cases, as we would with sovereign countries. Sovereignty limits how to help; it doesn't forbid helping.
2. **Human sovereignty doesn't prevent aid.** Sovereign countries receive humanitarian aid in disasters and epidemics without losing their sovereignty.
3. **The analogy has limits.** Human peoples can ask for or refuse help. Young animals starving in their first hours have no way of exercising any sovereignty.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | Uses "freedom" as if it meant "wellbeing" |
| Logical | False neutrality | Treats omission as if it weren't a decision |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts helping humans without consent and denies it to animals |
| Moral | Selectivity | Calls only interventions for the animals' good arrogant |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Supports environmental interventions.** Reforestation and species control are interference too.
2. **Takes their own animal to the vet.** Without thinking it humanizes or disrespects it.
3. **Supports rescuing animals in disasters.** Which is exactly interference to help.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "respecting someone means not interfering in their life, even when they suffer".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unconscious people | Helping them would be disrespect | No one accepts this |
| Children | Vaccinating them would be undue paternalism | Rejected |
| Countries in disaster | Humanitarian aid would violate sovereignty | Aid is accepted and requested |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that respecting wild animals includes respecting their way of life, that interventions can be invasive and that creating dependence can be a problem. It's also right that humans have already done much harm to nature through careless interventions.

But respecting isn't abandoning. When helping is possible and safe, refusing help in the name of freedom or humility is letting others suffer for a principle we'd never apply to ourselves.
