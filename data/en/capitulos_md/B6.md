# B6. "Without farming, these animals wouldn't even exist"

**Thesis:** bringing someone into existence gives no right to kill them. Parents give life to their children and gain no right over their lives because of it. Henry Salt called this argument, in 1914, "the logic of the larder": the idea that whoever loves animals most would be whoever breeds the most of them to eat. And it protects no animal that already exists; it only justifies breeding others to kill.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If no one ate meat, there'd be no cows. We're giving them life."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Existing with a life worth living is better, for a being, than not existing.
2. **P2.** Farmed animals only exist because they are bred for consumption.
3. **P3.** If consumption ended, these animals would not exist.
4. **C.** So consumption benefits the animals by bringing them into existence.

The strong version has a well-known philosophical formulation, the replaceability argument: if each animal killed is replaced by another with an equally good life, the total amount of well-being in the world does not decrease.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1, which treats non-existence as a loss for someone who doesn't exist, and in the conclusion, which moves from "brought into life" to "may kill".

## 3. Answer for children

Your parents gave you life. Does that mean they can do whatever they want with you, even hurt you? Of course not! Giving someone life doesn't give the right to take it. It's the same with animals: making a calf be born doesn't give anyone the right to kill it later.

## 4. Short answer

Giving someone life doesn't give the right to take it: parents give life to their children and gain no right over their lives. Someone who doesn't exist loses nothing by not existing; someone who exists loses everything by being killed. And the sentence protects no cow that exists today: it only justifies breeding others to kill. No one would say breeding dogs for meat is a favor to dogs.

**Question to ask back:** "If someone had a child only to sell it later, would having given it life justify the sale?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats existence as a gift that gives rights to the giver:

| Step | What the argument assumes | The problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Not existing would be bad for the animal | Someone who doesn't exist can be harmed | There is no one to be harmed by non-existence |
| Giving life is a benefit | The benefit creates a credit | Benefits create no right over the life of whoever received them |
| So killing is allowed | The credit licenses the harm | No human relationship works like that |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Bringing into existence gives no right to kill.** Parents, dog breeders and sanctuaries bring animals and people into existence, and no one considers that this licenses them to kill.

**2. Someone who doesn't exist isn't harmed.** There is no cow who fails to exist and misses life. Whoever loses something is the cow who exists and is killed. The argument compares a real being with an imaginary one.

**3. Henry Salt answered this in 1914.** In the essay *The Logic of the Larder*, Salt observed that, by this reasoning, the true lover of animals would be the one whose larder was fullest of them. He called the idea the very negation of reverence for life.

**4. The life given is rarely good.** About three quarters of farmed animals live in intensive systems (B2). For them, "we gave them life" means "we gave them a life of confinement".

**5. Extinction is not a harm to the animals.** The end of farming would reduce the number of animals born to die; it wouldn't kill the ones that exist. Domesticated species could go on existing in smaller numbers, and many were shaped by breeding to serve production, with health problems they wouldn't have on their own.

**6. Killing someone who exists isn't the same as not breeding someone.** When an animal is killed, there is an individual who loses their life. When an animal is not bred, no one loses anything: there are only possibilities. That's why one death is not "cancelled out" by another birth. If it were, parents could kill a child as long as they had another in its place.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: replaceability

The most serious version says that, for beings without a sense of themselves over time, what matters is the total amount of good experiences in the world. If an animal with a good life is killed and replaced by another with an equally good life, the total doesn't change. Peter Singer discussed this idea, though with many caveats.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It proves too much.** Applied to beings without self-awareness, it would also hold for human newborns. Almost no one accepts this (A12, A16).
2. **It requires good lives.** Replaceability only works if each animal has a life worth living, which doesn't describe most animal farming.
3. **It treats individuals as containers.** Each animal stops being someone and becomes a place where well-being is stored. That is exactly what an ethics of individuals rejects.
4. **Singer himself doesn't use it to defend today's consumption.** He considers it inapplicable to factory farming.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Non sequitur | From "brought into life" it concludes "may kill" |
| Logical | Comparison with someone who doesn't exist | Treats non-existence as a harm to someone |
| Epistemological | Idealizing the life given | Assumes the life created is good |
| Moral | A credit over another's life | Treats existence as a debt to be paid with death |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the reasoning to children.** Giving life has never licensed anyone to take it.
2. **Wouldn't accept breeding dogs for meat.** Even if it "gave life" to more dogs.
3. **Isn't worried about the extinction of wild species.** The concern for existence appears only for farmed species.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever brings a being into existence may kill it later".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Children | Parents could dispose of their children's lives | No one accepts this |
| Bred dogs | Breeders could slaughter them for meat | Almost no one accepts this |
| Beings created in the lab | They could be killed by whoever created them | Contradicts all research ethics |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There's a real philosophical question here: the ethics of bringing beings into existence is hard, and whether it's good for there to be more good lives in the world divides serious philosophers. It's also true that the end of animal farming would change the relationship between humans and domesticated species, and that this calls for careful thought.

But none of this turns birth into permission to kill. If the animal exists, it is already someone, and what matters is what is done to it, not the fact that it was brought into the world for that purpose.
