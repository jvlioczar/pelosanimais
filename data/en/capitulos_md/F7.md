# F7. "Oysters and mussels don't feel"

**Thesis:** the argument is partly plausible, and that is exactly why it confirms the criterion. Oysters and mussels have very simple nervous systems, with no centralized brain, and some philosophers who defend animals admit they may not feel. But by saying "they don't feel, so it's fine", the person accepts that what matters is sentience. And that criterion condemns eating every animal who does feel, including fish, octopuses and crabs.

**Family:** F. Edge cases and hypotheticals · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Oysters have no central nervous system. Bivalves are almost plants."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Oysters and mussels have no centralized brain and have very simple nervous systems.
2. **P2.** Without that kind of system, they're unlikely to be sentient.
3. **P3.** If they aren't sentient, eating them causes no suffering.
4. **C.** So eating oysters and mussels is acceptable.

P1 is true. P2 is plausible, but uncertain. P3 is true if P2 is. The most important point is that the argument accepts sentience as the criterion.

The argument is **valid** and perhaps **sound** for bivalves. But it can't be used to defend eating animals who feel, and the uncertainty about P2 is a reason for caution.

## 3. Answer for children

Some animals, like oysters and mussels, are very simple and may not feel pain. Scientists are still studying this. But if we decide that only those who feel matter, then we need to look after everyone who does feel, like fish, octopuses, chickens and pigs.

## 4. Short answer

You may be right about oysters and mussels: they have very simple nervous systems, and even some animal advocates admit they may not feel. But by saying that, you accept that what matters is whether the animal feels. And that criterion condemns eating fish, octopuses, crabs, chickens and pigs, who do feel. The exception, if it exists, proves the rule.

**Question to ask back:** "If what matters is whether the animal feels, why eat the ones who do?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument accepts the sentience criterion, and that has consequences:

| Animal | Evidence of sentience | What the criterion implies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mammals and birds | Strong | Don't eat |
| Fish | Strong and growing | Don't eat |
| Octopuses and crabs | Strong, recognized even in UK law | Don't eat |
| Oysters and mussels | Weak and uncertain | Perhaps acceptable; caution advised |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It confirms the criterion.** If the problem is the lack of sentience, sentience is what matters. That's exactly the criterion of veganism and animal ethics.

**2. The exception doesn't extend.** Octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters show strong evidence of sentience. In 2021, a London School of Economics report recommended treating them as sentient, and the United Kingdom included them in its animal sentience law in 2022.

**3. Uncertainty calls for caution.** Bivalves have nerve ganglia and react to stimuli. The absence of strong evidence isn't strong evidence of absence. When in doubt, the cost of not eating them is very small.

**4. The exception doesn't cover the rest of the plate.** Some people eat only bivalves for this reason, but most of the time the argument comes from people who eat many other animals, for whom it doesn't hold.

**5. Harvesting and farming bivalves also have effects.** Collection and production can affect other animals and ecosystems, which should be taken into account.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: veganism should then accept bivalves

The most serious version says that if bivalves probably don't feel, veganism should accept them, and that refusing them shows the movement is dogmatic.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Some animal advocates do accept them.** Some philosophers and activists consider eating bivalves acceptable. The debate is honest and open.
2. **Caution isn't dogma.** Faced with uncertainty, preferring not to take the risk is a reasonable position.
3. **Nothing changes for the other animals.** Accepting bivalves or not doesn't affect the conclusion about animals who feel.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in F7 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Fallacy of accident (converse) | Uses the case of bivalves for other animals |
| Epistemological | Absence of evidence taken as evidence of absence | Uncertainty becomes certainty |
| Moral | Criterion used only where convenient | Accepts sentience as the criterion and ignores what it implies |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Accepts sentience as the criterion.** And eats clearly sentient animals.
2. **Doesn't eat only bivalves.** The argument doesn't describe their own consumption.
3. **Would ask for caution in other cases of uncertainty.** In medicine, safety, the environment.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if an animal doesn't feel, eating it isn't wrong".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oysters and mussels | Perhaps acceptable | With caution because of the uncertainty |
| Octopuses and crabs | Not acceptable, because they feel | Recognized in UK law |
| Fish, birds and mammals | Not acceptable, because they feel | The principle condemns almost all consumption |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The argument is largely right. Bivalves are genuinely hard cases, the science about them is uncertain, and some animal advocates admit they may not feel. Recognizing this is a sign of honesty and shows that veganism rests on criteria, not dogma.

And it's precisely that criterion that settles the rest. Whoever accepts that what matters is feeling already has the answer for every other animal on the plate.
