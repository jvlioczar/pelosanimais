# I13. "Without zoos, several species would already be extinct"

**Thesis:** the argument has real cases on its side. The Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx disappeared, or nearly disappeared, from the wild and only came back because there were individuals in captivity. But captive breeding was decisive for a small share of recovered species: according to a survey published in *Science* and later revised, it was a major action for 13 of the 68 vertebrate species that moved out of threatened categories. Most conservation comes from protecting habitats. And reintroduction programs involve a small fraction of the animals kept in zoos, nearly all of whom are displayed to the public and never return to the wild.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If it weren't for zoos, lots of animals would have vanished already. Many species only exist today because someone kept a few in captivity and released them later."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Several species became extinct in the wild and survived only in captivity.
2. **P2.** Captive breeding made it possible to reintroduce some of these species.
3. **P3.** Without institutions that keep animals in captivity, these species would have disappeared.
4. **C.** So zoos are necessary, and keeping animals in them is justified.

P1, P2 and P3 are true for some cases. The conclusion overgeneralizes: it justifies breeding for conservation where it's needed, not the display of thousands of animals from species that aren't threatened.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails in the step from "some programs saved species" to "zoos in general are justified".

## 3. Answer for children

Some species almost disappeared, and were only saved because people looked after a few animals and then released them into the wild. That's wonderful. But most animals in zoos aren't threatened and will never go back to the wild. And the best way to save animals is to look after the forests and grasslands where they live.

## 4. Short answer

There are real cases: the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx were saved thanks to individuals in captivity. But they aren't the majority: captive breeding was a major action for 13 of the 68 vertebrate species that moved out of threatened categories, and most conservation comes from protecting habitats. Reintroduction programs involve a small fraction of zoo animals. Breeding a threatened species to return it to the wild is different from keeping thousands of animals on display.

**Question to ask back:** "How many of the animals in this zoo will ever go back to the wild?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoos saved species from extinction | Some programs were decisive | They're few, and almost always come with habitat protection |
| Most recoveries came from captivity | Captive breeding helped in some cases | It was a major action for 13 of 68 recovered species |
| So zoos are justified | Breeding for conservation can be necessary | Displaying non-threatened animals isn't justified by that |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The success stories are real.** The Arabian oryx became extinct in the wild in 1972 and was reintroduced in Oman from 1982. Every remaining wild California condor was captured in 1987, and the species returned to the wild in 1992. The scimitar-horned oryx, declared extinct in the wild in 2000, was reintroduced in Chad from 2016 and reclassified as "Endangered" in 2023.

**2. But they aren't the majority.** A survey published in *Science* in 2011 identified captive breeding as a major action in the recovery of 13 of the 68 vertebrate species whose extinction risk fell, a figure revised by the authors themselves after it was challenged. Most recoveries came from protecting habitats, fighting poaching and controlling invasive species.

**3. Most zoo animals aren't involved.** The same study shows that zoos keep a share of threatened species, but most animals on display belong to species that aren't threatened and will never be reintroduced (see I12).

**4. Breeding for conservation doesn't require display.** Programs that work usually involve specialized breeding centers, protected areas and planned releases. This can be done without turning the animals into an attraction.

**5. The species isn't the individual.** Conservation programs look after the species, and not always after each animal's welfare: individuals deemed surplus have been killed in European zoos, such as the giraffe Marius in Copenhagen in 2014. For the guide, conservation matters as a means of protecting the lives of real sentient beings (see A10).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: without captivity, these species wouldn't exist

The most serious version says that for species that only survived in captivity there was no alternative, and that rejecting zoos would mean accepting extinction.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It justifies breeding for conservation, not the zoo as an attraction.** The cases cited depend on specific programs, not on displaying animals to the public.
2. **The most effective response is still habitat.** Without protected areas there is nowhere to reintroduce to, and the species stays stuck in captivity.
3. **It's a controversial question among animal advocates.** Some accept breeding programs aimed at reintroduction and at each individual's welfare, and some reject all captivity. What doesn't hold up is using a few success stories to justify zoos in general.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether saving a species justifies keeping animals confined for display. When captive breeding is the only way to prevent an extinction, and is done with the aim of returning animals to the wild and with each one's welfare in mind, the discussion is legitimate. But that describes a small part of what zoos do.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I13 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Generalizing from success stories | A few programs would justify all zoos |
| Empirical | Overstating the contribution | "Most recovered species came from captivity" |
| Moral | Confusing species with individual | Saving the species is treated as good for each confined animal |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Cites species that were saved.** And defends keeping thousands of animals from species that aren't threatened.
2. **Talks about returning animals to the wild.** And most animals never leave the zoo.
3. **Says it protects animals.** And accepts killing individuals deemed surplus.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I13 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survivorship bias | The success stories are visible; the animals who never go back are not | General literature on reasoning |
| Conservation halo | Some programs' work lends legitimacy to the whole institution | General literature on judgment |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if some institutions helped save species, keeping animals in captivity is justified".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoos without a conservation program | They'd be justified | The argument doesn't cover them |
| Animals from non-threatened species | Their confinement would be justified | There's no species to save |
| Breeding centers aimed at reintroduction | They're what the argument really defends | This is the legitimate discussion |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that species like the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx only exist in the wild today because there were individuals in captivity, and that captive breeding can be the last barrier against an extinction.

But these cases are few, almost always depend on habitat protection, and don't justify keeping animals on display in zoos in general.
