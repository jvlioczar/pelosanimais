# I13. "Carmine is a natural dye, better than artificial ones"

**Thesis:** carmine, or cochineal dye, is made from insects: female cochineals, which live on cacti, are dropped into hot water, dried and ground, and about 70,000 are needed to make a pound of dye, or about 150,000 per kilogram. Like every insect, the cochineal has a nervous system, and there is evidence of pain-like states in several insect orders; their sentience is uncertain, but they may have negative experiences. Being natural says nothing about who was killed to make it. And there are natural red dyes from plants, such as those from beetroot, paprika, tomato and purple fruits. Carmine shows up in yogurts, drinks, sweets, processed meats, lipsticks and eyeshadows, sometimes under unclear names such as E120 or CI 75470.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Carmine is a natural dye. Much better than those artificial colorings full of chemicals. And it's just an insect."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some artificial red dyes have been linked to health risks.
2. **P2.** Carmine is a natural dye, used for centuries.
3. **P3.** The insects used probably feel little or nothing.
4. **C.** So using carmine is the healthiest choice and causes no relevant harm.

P1 is true for some dyes. P2 is true. P3 is uncertain: there is growing evidence of experiences in insects, and cochineals have been little studied. And the conclusion ignores that there are natural plant dyes, which solve P1 without P3.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3 and in the false choice between carmine and artificial dye.

## 3. Answer for children

Some yogurts, candies and lipsticks are red because of a dye made from little bugs called cochineals. To make a little dye, thousands of them are killed. They have a nervous system, and scientists think it's possible that some insects feel something like pain. You can make things red with beetroot, strawberry or paprika, without hurting any creature.

## 4. Short answer

Natural, yes, but do you know where it comes from? Carmine is made from cochineals: about 70,000 females, killed in hot water, are needed for a pound of dye, about 150,000 per kilogram. They have a nervous system and, although insect sentience is uncertain, there is evidence of pain-like states in several insect orders. Being natural doesn't change that. And the choice isn't between carmine and artificial dye: there are natural reds from beetroot, paprika, tomato and purple fruits. If you want to avoid it, look on the label for "carmine", "cochineal", "E120", "INS 120" or, in cosmetics, "CI 75470". When it's uncertain whether these insects feel, is a colour worth killing so many?

**Question to ask back:** "If you can get the same red from beetroot, why kill thousands of insects?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Option | Where it comes from | Does it kill animals? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carmine (E120, INS 120, CI 75470) | Female cochineals, *Dactylopius coccus* | Yes, about 70,000 per pound (about 150,000 per kilogram) |
| Beetroot red (E162) | Beetroot | No |
| Anthocyanins (E163) | Grapes, red cabbage and other fruits and vegetables | No |
| Paprika and lycopene (E160c and E160d) | Peppers and tomatoes | No |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Natural doesn't mean without victims.** Carmine is natural because it comes from animals. Cochineals are removed from the cacti, dropped into hot water, dried and ground.

**2. The scale is enormous.** According to McGill University's Office for Science and Society, about 70,000 females are needed to make a pound of dye, which comes to about 150,000 per kilogram. Estimates vary depending on whether one counts dried insects or refined pigment, but all fall in the range of tens to hundreds of thousands of insects per kilogram.

**3. Insect sentience is a serious possibility.** Like all insects, cochineals have a nervous system, with cerebral ganglia and a nerve cord running along the body, and they react to harmful stimuli. That alone doesn't prove they feel anything. But a 2022 review, which assessed insects against eight criteria for sentience, found strong evidence of pain-like states in adult flies and cockroaches, substantial evidence in bees, wasps, ants, crickets, grasshoppers, butterflies and moths, and no clear case of an insect failing a criterion. The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness recognizes a realistic possibility of consciousness in insects. Cochineals belong to an order (Hemiptera) that the review couldn't assess for lack of studies, and adult females lead an almost motionless life, attached to the cactus, which makes the question even more uncertain. Even so, they may have negative experiences, including when dropped into hot water, and doubt, in the face of such large numbers, is a reason for caution.

**4. There are natural plant alternatives.** Beetroot, anthocyanins, paprika and lycopene give red and pink tones without killing animals. The comparison with artificial dyes is a false choice.

**5. Carmine also causes allergies.** Since 2011, the US Food and Drug Administration has required carmine to be declared by name on food and cosmetic labels, because of reports of severe allergic reactions.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: plant dyes are less stable

The most serious version says carmine holds its color under heat, light and acidity better than many plant dyes, and that industry needs that stability.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The technical difference is real, but small next to the cost.** Many products already use plant dyes with good results, and stabilization technology keeps improving.
2. **A slightly less intense color doesn't justify thousands of deaths.** The choice is between an aesthetic detail and the lives of many animals.
3. **Consumers can choose.** Reading labels and preferring brands without carmine changes demand.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's worth killing thousands of animals who may feel, to make a product red, when there are other ways to do it. The word "natural" doesn't answer that.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Prefers natural for health.** And ignores that carmine also causes allergies.
2. **Says it's just an insect.** And wouldn't crush thousands of them for a lipstick with their own hands.
3. **Avoids artificial dyes.** And doesn't look for plant ones.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if the dye is natural, killing the animals who produce it is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tyrian purple, made from thousands of sea snails | It would be acceptable | It was abandoned for dyes without animals |
| Plant-based red dyes | Natural and without deaths | This is the alternative |
| Shellac, made from insect secretions | It would be acceptable | There are alternatives too |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that some artificial red dyes raise health concerns, that carmine is natural and has been used for centuries, and that it is stable.

But it costs the lives of thousands of insects per pound, and there are natural plant reds. Natural and victim-free aren't the same thing.
