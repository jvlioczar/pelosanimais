# I7. "Leather is just a byproduct of meat"

**Thesis:** profitable byproducts help pay for slaughter. In the United States, hides traditionally made up about 6% to 8% of the total value of a steer, and were the most valuable byproduct; with the recent fall in prices, the share reached about 1% in 2020. Even small, it's revenue: buying leather helps pay for slaughter and sustains the system. Besides, some leather comes from animals raised partly for their skin, and from highly polluting tanning processes. And there is a viable option that requires killing no animal: synthetic and plant-based leathers, already used for shoes, bags, clothing and upholstery.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The leather would be thrown away. Better to use the whole animal. Synthetic leather is plastic."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Cattle are slaughtered mainly for meat.
2. **P2.** If the hide weren't used, it would be thrown away.
3. **P3.** Using what would be thrown away is better than wasting it.
4. **C.** So using leather causes no additional harm and is even a form of respect.

P1 is true for most cattle. P2 is partly true: there are times when hides are worth little. The problem lies in the conclusion: leather has economic value, and that value helps fund slaughter.

The argument is **valid** if leather had no value. Its **soundness** fails because it does.

## 3. Answer for children

Leather is a cow's skin. People who sell meat also sell the skin, and make money from it. That money helps pay for raising and killing more cows. So buying leather also helps keep going the system that kills the animals. But there are shoes, bags and jackets made with materials that look like leather, and no animal has to die for them.

## 4. Short answer

Leather isn't waste: it's a product that gets sold. In the United States, it traditionally made up about 6% to 8% of the total value of a steer, and was the most valuable byproduct; with falling prices, it reached about 1% in 2020. Even so, it's revenue that helps pay for slaughter. Buying leather funds part of livestock farming. And tanning is one of the most polluting industrial processes, using chromium and other chemicals. Synthetic and plant-based leathers are a viable, cruelty-free option. Many still contain plastic, but in several life-cycle studies they have a smaller carbon footprint than cowhide.

**Question to ask back:** "If leather had no value, why would slaughterhouses sell it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats as disposable something that has a price:

| Source of livestock revenue | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Meat | Most of it |
| Leather | Traditionally the most valuable byproduct; about 6% to 8% of a steer's value in the US, with large price swings |
| Other byproducts (fat, bones, offal) | The rest |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Leather has economic value.** It's sold to tanneries. When the price of hides falls, the price paid for cattle falls too, which shows it's part of the calculation.

**2. Byproducts subsidize the main product.** Byproduct revenue lowers the cost of meat and makes livestock farming more profitable. Buying leather funds the system.

**3. Not all leather comes from beef cattle.** Some leathers come from animals raised partly or mainly for their skin, such as crocodiles, ostriches and some goats.

**4. Tanning pollutes heavily.** Chrome tanning produces toxic waste that pollutes rivers and affects workers in many countries.

**5. There are viable, cruelty-free alternatives.** Synthetic and plant-based leathers are already used in shoes, bags, clothing and upholstery, and no animal has to die to make them. It's worth being precise about what they are. Most synthetic leathers are a polyester fabric coated with polyurethane (PU) or PVC, that is, fossil-based plastic. Many materials sold as "plant-based", made with cactus, apple, grape or pineapple fibre, mix these agricultural residues with a layer of polyurethane or another polymer. There are also fungal mycelium materials with very little plastic. A 2021 study comparing nine alternatives found that none reproduces every property of leather, such as resistance to tearing and flexing, although some do well on specific properties; lower durability can mean replacing the product sooner.

**6. The environmental balance has costs on both sides.** Plastic doesn't break down and leaves waste at the end of its life. But cowhide carries livestock farming and tanning with it, and several life-cycle assessments find a much smaller carbon footprint for the alternatives; one for a mycelium leather, for example, estimated about 8% of the footprint of the cowhide used as a benchmark. The figures vary widely with the method and with who funds the study, and the leather industry disputes some of them. Even so, the environmental cost of the alternatives can be reduced by choosing the material better, while the cost to the animal is built into leather.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: leather is worth little today

The most serious version points out that in some recent years the value of hides fell so much that many were discarded, and that under those conditions using leather funds nothing.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The fall proves leather counts.** When hides are worth less, cattle are worth less: the revenue is part of the calculation.
2. **Buying leather sustains demand.** It's precisely demand that keeps its value up.
3. **The waste argument applies to meat too.** And it doesn't justify raising and killing more animals.

### (f) Back to the moral question

Leather comes from the skin of an animal who was killed, and selling it helps pay for that death. If there are alternatives for clothing and shoes, the moral question remains: what justifies funding the slaughter?

2. **Pays more for leather.** Which shows it has value.
3. **Uses synthetic materials in other things.** Without worrying about plastic.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if it's a byproduct, buying it doesn't fund the main activity".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ivory from slaughtered elephants | Buying it wouldn't fund poaching | That's why the trade was banned |
| Wild animal furs | They'd be acceptable if the animal died for some other reason | The market encourages hunting |
| Leather | It wouldn't fund slaughter | The revenue exists |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that most cattle are slaughtered for meat, and that the value of hides is sometimes low. It's also true that some synthetic materials have an environmental impact, and that choosing among alternatives deserves attention.

But leather is part of livestock revenue, and buying it helps pay for slaughter. There are ways to dress and wear shoes without funding the death of animals.
