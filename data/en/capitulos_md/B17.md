# B17. "Animals don't mind, they even like it and, in a way, they consent"

**Thesis:** an animal seeming calm doesn't mean they don't mind, and liking affection or food doesn't mean liking being confined, separated from their young or killed. Animals have preferences, and animal welfare science measures how hard they work to get or avoid something. Consent requires understanding and the ability to refuse; animals don't understand what will be done to them, and when they show refusal, by fleeing, resisting or calling for their young, the refusal is ignored. And even an animal who noticed nothing would lose the rest of their life.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The cow doesn't mind being milked; she even likes it. The hen lays the egg and forgets about it. If they didn't want it, they'd run away."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** In the routine of farms, many animals show no obvious signs of suffering.
2. **P2.** Some seem to enjoy contact with people and approach of their own accord.
3. **P3.** What doesn't bother an animal doesn't harm them, and what they accept without resisting is, in a way, consented to.
4. **C.** So using these animals doesn't harm them.

P1 is only partly true, and it picks the calm moments. P2 is true of certain kinds of contact, such as food and affection, but not of everything that happens to the animal. P3 is false: there are harms the animal doesn't notice at the time, such as death, and accepting without resisting isn't consenting when one doesn't understand what will happen and can't refuse.

The argument is **valid**, but not **sound**: P3 confuses not reacting with not being harmed, and calm with consent.

## 3. Answer for children

A dog can like getting a treat and still not like being locked up. It's the same with farm animals: the cow may like grass and being petted, but she calls for her calf when he is taken away. Liking one thing doesn't mean liking everything.

## 4. Short answer

Seeming calm isn't the same as not minding, and liking food or affection isn't liking being confined, separated from one's young or killed. Cows separated from their calves call for them and become agitated, and hens work to get a nest to lay in. Consent requires understanding what will happen and being able to refuse; when an animal refuses, by fleeing or resisting, no one respects it. And even if they noticed nothing, they would lose the rest of their life.

**Question to ask back:** "If the animal could refuse, do you think they would agree to go to the slaughterhouse?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument observes | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The animal seems calm | Many moments of the routine are quiet | Separation, confinement, mutilations, transport and slaughter |
| The animal approaches people | Food and contact can be good for them | That this says nothing about what comes next |
| The animal doesn't resist | Many were bred to be docile | That docility isn't consent, and that resistance, when it happens, is restrained |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Animals have preferences, and they can be measured.** Animal welfare science has developed tests, such as those of the zoologist Marian Dawkins, that measure how hard an animal will work to get access to something or to avoid it. They show that hens, pigs and cows have clear preferences about space, nests, companionship and environment. "Doesn't mind" is an assumption, not an observation.

**2. There are clear signs that they care.** In a study by Daniel Weary and Belinda Chua, published in 2000, cows and calves separated after birth vocalized and became agitated, and the reaction was stronger the longer they had been together. Prey species, such as cattle and sheep, also tend to hide signs of pain, which makes suffering seem smaller than it is.

**3. Liking a part isn't liking the whole.** A pig who runs to whoever brings food likes the food, not the truck that will come later. The argument uses the pleasant moments to answer for the others.

**4. Consent requires understanding and being able to refuse.** No animal understands that they will be slaughtered, and none can effectively refuse. In animal research, some argue for respecting at least the animal's refusal, their dissent, as is done with young children. In farming, when an animal resists, the refusal is restrained with fences, chutes and electric prods.

**5. Not noticing isn't not being harmed.** Even if an animal never suffered at any moment, death would take the rest of their life from them. It is a harm of deprivation, which doesn't depend on their noticing it (see B2).

| Source | What it says | Note |

| [Kantin and Wendler, 2015, *Cambridge Quarterly of Healthcare Ethics*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0963180115000110) | Discuss whether animal research should respect animals' dissent, as is done with those who can't consent | Shows the question of consent is taken seriously in bioethics |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: domesticated animals were bred to be docile, so they suffer little

The most serious version says: thousands of years of domestication produced calm animals, less stressed by human presence. If they really are less bothered, the argument would have a grain of truth.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Docility was chosen by us and for us.** Breeding animals who resist less doesn't show that they agree; it shows we made using them easier.
2. **Docile doesn't mean without preferences.** The same docile animals seek their young, avoid pain and work for space and comfort.
3. **And death is still a harm.** A calm animal loses the rest of their life as much as an agitated one.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B17 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | The calm moments answer for all the others |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Accepting" used in the sense of consenting |
| Epistemological | Argument from ignorance | Not seeing suffering is taken as proof there is none |
| Moral | Harm reduced to what is noticed | Ignores the harm of death |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says the animal likes it.** And doesn't ask what the animal does when they can choose.
2. **Says they would accept it.** And uses fences, chutes and prods to move them.
3. **Recognizes that their own dog misses the people they love.** And assumes the cow doesn't miss her calf.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B17 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat paradox | Assuming the animal doesn't mind reduces the discomfort of eating them | [Bastian and Loughnan, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868316647562) |
| Mind denial | We attribute less mind to the animals we eat | [Bastian and Loughnan, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868316647562) |
| Confirmation bias | The calm moments are noticed and the others forgotten | General literature on selective perception |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if someone doesn't resist or seems calm, they aren't harmed, and in a way they consent".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A baby who smiles at a stranger | The baby would consent to anything the stranger did | No one accepts this |
| A sedated person | There would be no harm in doing anything to them | No one accepts this |
| One's own dog wagging their tail | They would accept being sold for slaughter | Almost no one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that many animals have quiet moments, that some enjoy human contact and that domestication produced more docile animals. It's also right to give weight to what the animal feels: that's the right criterion.

But, applied carefully, that criterion turns against the argument: animals have preferences, show when they don't want something and lose their lives at slaughter, whether they notice or not.
