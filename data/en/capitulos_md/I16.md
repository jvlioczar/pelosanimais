# I16. "Safaris and animal tours are harmless tourism"

**Thesis:** "animal tourism" lumps very different things together. Watching free animals from a distance, in their habitat, is one thing; riding elephants, posing with lion cubs or swimming with captive dolphins is another. A University of Oxford study assessed 24 types of wildlife tourist attraction and found negative welfare impacts in 18 of them, while the vast majority of tourists rated even the worst attractions positively.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Riding an elephant in Thailand was the best part of the trip. The animals looked happy."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Animal tourism brings income to the places that keep the animals.
2. **P2.** That income can fund conservation and the animals' care.
3. **P3.** The animals look fine during the activities.
4. **C.** So taking part in these tours is harmless, or even good for the animals.

P1 is true. P2 holds for some activities, above all watching free animals. P3 confuses appearance with welfare.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3 and in stretching P2 to every kind of tour.

## 3. Answer for children

Seeing an elephant walking free on the savanna, from far away, is very different from climbing on the back of an elephant trained for it. For the elephant to obey, someone had to teach it with fear and pain. Free animals don't need us close by; the captive ones didn't choose to be there.

## 4. Short answer

It depends on the tour. Watching free animals from a distance, in their habitat, can even help protect natural areas. But riding elephants, posing with lion cubs or swimming with captive dolphins depends on capture, breeding and training that make the animals suffer. An Oxford study assessed 24 types of wildlife attraction and found welfare harm in 18, yet most tourists still rated them positively.

**Question to ask back:** "If the elephant were free to choose, would it carry tourists all day?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument puts very different activities in the same sentence:

| Activity | What the animal lives through |
| --- | --- |
| Watching free animals from a distance | A free life; the impact depends on distance, number of vehicles and rules |
| Riding elephants | Training to obey, chains, repetitive work |
| Posing with lion or tiger cubs | Cubs taken from their mothers and handled all day, often feeding captive breeding |
| Swimming with dolphins in tanks | Captivity in a tiny space for animals that travel long distances |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Most of the attractions assessed cause harm.** Moorhouse and colleagues, from the University of Oxford's conservation research unit, assessed 24 types of wildlife tourist attraction. In 18, the welfare impact was negative, affecting between 230,000 and 550,000 animals; in 14, the conservation impact was negative too.

**2. Tourists don't notice.** In the same study, at least 80% of tourists typically left positive TripAdvisor reviews, even for the attractions with the worst welfare standards. The impression that "the animals looked happy" is not a good measure.

**3. A calm animal isn't always a well animal.** A cub lying still in someone's lap may be exhausted, scared or trained not to react. Elephants that carry tourists were conditioned to obey.

**4. The money feeds the supply.** Paying for direct contact keeps up the demand that leads to capturing and breeding more animals for the same use.

**5. Watching from a distance is something else.** Seeing free animals, without touching or feeding them, can fund protected areas. The objection mixes what can help with what clearly harms.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: tourism funds conservation

The most serious version points out that, in many countries, tourism income is what pays for national parks, rangers and the communities that live alongside wild animals. Without it, habitats could become pasture or farmland.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That holds for watching, not for contact.** Funding for protected areas comes mostly from seeing free animals, not from riding or holding them.
2. **Conserving the species doesn't license exploiting the individual.** The trained elephant gets nothing from being the attraction (A10).
3. **There is tourism that helps without harming.** Just choose activities with no contact, with distance rules and with no captive animals.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The underlying question is the same as for any use of animals: do their interests count, or only the experience of whoever pays? Watching without interfering respects the animals; turning them into a holiday toy doesn't.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in I16 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | What holds for watching is extended to contact |
| Epistemological | Appearance as proof | "It looked happy" is taken as welfare |
| Moral | The tourist's pleasure above the animal's interest | The photo outweighs the animal's life |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says they love animals.** And pays for them to be kept captive and trained.
2. **Prefers free animals in documentaries.** And chooses to see them chained on holiday.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same with dogs.** A ride on dogs trained by force would be cruelty.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains I16 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | Wanting the experience hides the cost to the animal | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Social proof | If millions do it and rate it well, it must be fine | [Moorhouse et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0138939) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if tourism brings income and the animal looks calm, the activity is harmless".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dancing bears | They would be harmless tourism | They have been banned in several countries for cruelty |
| Photos with sedated animals | They would be acceptable | A calm animal may be drugged |
| Whale watching from a distance | It would be low-impact tourism | Here the principle works better, with rules |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that tourism can fund habitat protection, and that watching free animals, with distance rules, can be a respectful way to get to know them. It's also true that many tourists go to these attractions because they like animals.

But that same liking calls for choosing the tours where the animal doesn't pay the price: no contact, no captivity, no animals trained to entertain.
