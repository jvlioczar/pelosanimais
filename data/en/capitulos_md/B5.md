# B5. "Milk and eggs don't kill anyone"

**Thesis:** milk and egg production kills too, just out of sight. In egg production, male chicks are culled at hatching: an estimated 7.83 billion in 2024. In dairy, the cow has to be made pregnant to produce milk, the male calves have little value and the cows themselves are slaughtered when production drops. The egg and the milk don't kill directly; the system that produces them does. And it isn't only death: in most systems, and above all in industrial ones, the whole production cycle imposes on the animals confinement, repeated pregnancies, separation from their young and health problems linked to extreme output.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I only eat cheese and eggs. The cow doesn't die to give milk, and an egg isn't a chicken."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** What makes eating animals wrong is killing them.
2. **P2.** Milking a cow or collecting a hen's eggs doesn't require killing the animal.
3. **C.** So consuming milk and eggs is not wrong.

P2 is true in the narrowest sense: the act of milking or picking up an egg kills no one. The problem is what commercial production requires for that act to happen.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, understood as a description of the supply chain, and at P1, which reduces harm to death and ignores the suffering of production.

## 3. Answer for children

To have eggs to sell, farms hatch lots of chicks. Half of them are boys, who don't lay eggs, and they are killed right after hatching. And for a cow to have milk, she has to have a calf, which is taken away from her. So even if no one kills the hen or the cow right then, many animals pay the price of eggs and milk.

## 4. Short answer

When you buy eggs or milk, you pay for a production that kills, just out of sight. The male chicks of laying breeds are useless to the industry and are culled at hatching: almost 8 billion a year by one estimate. A cow only gives milk after giving birth, the calves are taken from her, and she herself is slaughtered when production drops. And before that, on most commercial farms, hens and cows spend their lives confined and with health problems caused by production itself. Being vegetarian is a step, but it doesn't take you out of that chain.

**Question to ask back:** "What do you think happens to the male chicks of laying hens?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument looks only at the last link in the chain:

| Product | What the consumer sees | What production requires |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eggs | An egg in a carton | Hatcheries, culling of males at hatching, slaughter of hens when laying drops |
| Milk | Milk and cheese | Repeated pregnancies, separation of the calf, slaughter of the males or rearing them for beef, slaughter of the cow when production drops |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Male chicks are culled.** Laying hens are breeds selected to lay eggs, and the males of these breeds don't grow enough to be profitable as meat. So most are killed at hatching. Faunalytics estimated 7.83 billion male chicks culled worldwide in 2024. Some countries, such as Germany and France, now require sexing in the egg, which avoids part of this culling, but they are the exception.

**2. The hens are slaughtered too.** When laying drops, hens stop being profitable and are killed, long before the natural end of their lives. In commercial production this usually happens at about a year and a half of age: a Scottish Government report considers keeping them beyond 76 to 80 weeks commercially unviable.

**3. There's no milk without pregnancy.** Cows, like all mammals, only produce milk after giving birth. To keep production going, they are made pregnant again and again, and the calves are separated from them so the milk can be sold. Separating the calf from its mother within the first hours or days of life is common practice on dairy farms; in 2023 the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommended that the calf stay with its mother for at least one day, and that this contact be extended progressively.

**4. Calves and cows go to slaughter.** Male calves of dairy breeds have little value and are usually slaughtered young or raised for beef, and cows are slaughtered when their yield declines. In a study of Dutch herds, the average age of culled cows was about 5.9 years, while the natural lifespan of dairy cattle is around 20 years. The dairy chain is also a meat chain.

**5. The whole cycle causes suffering, not only the deaths.** Even without counting the deaths, egg and milk production, in most systems and above all in industrial ones, involves confinement, breeding for extreme output and the health problems that come with it. In 2023, EFSA listed bone lesions, such as keel bone fractures, and restriction of movement among the most relevant welfare consequences for laying hens, and recommended moving away from cages, which are still common in many countries. For dairy cows, the corresponding opinion dealt with lameness, mastitis, restriction of movement and metabolic disorders, and concluded that cows permanently tied in stalls have impaired welfare. This varies from system to system: on small farms with pasture access, some of these problems are reduced. But repeated pregnancy, separation from the young, culling of males and slaughter of those who stop producing are part of almost all commercial production (B2 deals with death; B10 with the rules).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the problem is the industry, not the product

The most serious version admits the culling and slaughter but says they are consequences of industrial production, and that it's possible to produce eggs and milk without killing: raising the males, keeping the cows until the end of their lives and leaving the calf with its mother.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That system barely exists at scale.** Keeping the males and the unproductive cows until the end of their lives would make eggs and milk many times more expensive. It isn't what people buy.
2. **Even there, the animal is still a means.** Repeated pregnancies and separating the calf are still needed for there to be milk to sell.
3. **The rare case exists, and is dealt with separately.** The egg from a rescued hen, which feeds no industry, is discussed in chapter F9 precisely because it is an exception.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Cropping to the last link | Judges the act of milking and ignores the chain that makes it possible |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the process | Ignores the culling of males and the slaughter of females |
| Moral | Reducing harm to direct killing | Ignores the suffering of production and the indirect deaths |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Would be shocked to see chicks being culled.** But pays for it when buying eggs.
2. **Thinks young animals should stay with their mothers in other contexts.** Separating the calf is the basis of dairy production.
3. **Gave up meat for the animals.** And still funds the death of other animals through milk and eggs.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if obtaining the product doesn't require killing at that moment, it involves no killing".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leather | It would be innocent, because the skin is taken from an animal already dead | Leather helps fund slaughter (I8) |
| Products of slave labor | Buying the final product would be innocent | No one accepts this |
| Male chicks | They wouldn't count, because they're not in the egg | They are part of the cost of every egg |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There's a real difference between eating an animal and consuming something it produces. Being vegetarian greatly reduces the number of animals killed directly, and it's an important step that deserves recognition. There are also rare cases, like rescued hens, where an egg involves no industry at all.

But in commercial production, eggs and milk don't come from animals who simply give something without losing anything. They come from a system that needs births, culling and slaughter to work. Anyone who doesn't want to kill animals finds, in milk and eggs, the same animals by another route.
