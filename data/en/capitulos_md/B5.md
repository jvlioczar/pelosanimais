# B5. "They had a good life and a quick death"

**Thesis:** the argument combines two promises that are rarely kept and, even when they are, don't justify the conclusion. A good life gives no one the right to take it, and a quick death is still a death. No one would accept the sentence as a justification for killing a dog or a person.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "The animals had a good life and died without noticing. Better than dying of old age in the wild."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** What matters to an animal is the quality of its life and the absence of suffering.
2. **P2.** An animal raised with good welfare and slaughtered without pain had a life worth living and a death without suffering.
3. **P3.** In the wild, many animals die of hunger, disease or predation, with great suffering.
4. **C.** So raising and slaughtering with good welfare does not harm the animal, and may even be better for it.

P3 is true: life in the wild involves a great deal of suffering (see Beyond veganism). The problem lies in P1, which leaves out the loss of life, and in P2, which describes a rare ideal.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P1 (A16) and P2 (B1 and B2). The comparison in P3 is a **false dichotomy**: the alternative to the slaughterhouse isn't the wild, it's not breeding animals to kill them.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "They had a good life and a quick death." |
| Everyday conversation | "They lived well and died without noticing." |
| Everyday conversation | "Better than dying in the wild." |
| Social media | "A grass-fed steer has a better life than a lot of people." |
| Rhetorical question | "Would you rather it got eaten by a wolf?" |
| Academic version | "A life worth living followed by a painless death is a net positive for the animal." |
| Disguised version | "Without farming, these animals wouldn't even exist." (which is B6) |

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone says: "Your kitten has lived very well, so now he can be killed." Would you agree? No, because he could still live many happy years. Having a good life is no reason to take it away. It's precisely because it's good that it's worth going on living.

## 4. Short answer

A good life doesn't give the right to take it; on the contrary, the better the life, the greater the loss. And a quick death is still the death of a young animal that had many years ahead of it. The comparison with nature is false: the alternative to the slaughterhouse isn't being eaten by a wolf, it's not being bred to die. No one would accept that sentence about a dog.

**Question to ask back:** "Would you accept someone killing your dog by saying he had a good life and a quick death?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument combines two promises and a comparison:

| Part | What it claims | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good life | The animals lived well | About three quarters of farmed animals live in intensive systems (B2) |
| Quick death | They died without noticing | Stunning fails, and some permitted methods cause pain (B1) |
| Comparison | Better than nature | The real alternative is not breeding them, not releasing them into the wild |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. A good life increases the loss.** If the animal's life was good, death takes something valuable from it. The promise of a good life is an argument against killing, not for it (A16).

**2. The animals are killed young.** In commercial production, broiler chickens are slaughtered at a few weeks old and pigs at about five months, long before the natural end of their lives.

**3. The two promises are rarely kept together.** Most animals come from intensive farming, and even on the best farms stunning can fail (B1, B2).

**4. The comparison with nature is a false dichotomy.** Farmed animals wouldn't be released into the wild: they simply wouldn't be brought into existence to die (B6). Wild animal suffering is real, but it isn't the alternative to slaughter.

**5. No one applies the sentence to humans or dogs.** A happy life has never been a justification for cutting it short.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Our World in Data, *How many animals are factory-farmed?*](https://ourworldindata.org/how-many-animals-are-factory-farmed) | About 74% of the world's farmed land animals live in intensive systems | Shows that the "good life" is the exception |
| [Natural life expectancy and age at slaughter](https://swissveg.ch/en/life-expectancy) | Broiler chickens at a few weeks old; pigs at about five months | Table from Swissveg, a Swiss vegan organization: chickens 5 to 6 weeks, pigs 5 months |
| [EFSA, *Welfare of pigs at slaughter* (2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7447914/) | Permitted methods can cause pain, fear and distress before unconsciousness | Also used in B1 |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: a net positive

The most serious version says that, for animals who would not otherwise have existed, a good life followed by a painless death is a net positive: they gain a life worth living. It would be better for them to exist this way than not to exist.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Bringing someone into existence gives no right to kill them.** Parents who give life to a child don't gain the right to take it (B6).
2. **A net positive doesn't require an early death.** If the animal's life is good, the balance would be even greater if it lived longer. Death doesn't improve the sum; it only cuts it short.
3. **The counter-reply only applies to the exception.** For most animals, life is not good, and the balance, even by this criterion, is negative.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Slaughterhouse or the wild, as if there were no other option |
| Logical | Non sequitur | From "it had a good life" it concludes "it can be killed" |
| Epistemological | Generalizing from the ideal case | Takes the exception as the rule |
| Moral | Reducing harm to suffering | Forgets the loss of life |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wouldn't accept the sentence for their own dog.** A pet's good life is a reason to care for it longer.
2. **Mourns the early death of those who lived well.** Among humans, a good life cut short is seen as a tragedy.
3. **Uses nature as a comparison only here.** No one justifies other practices by saying they'd be worse in the wild.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B5 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Optimism bias | Assumes the animals eaten had the life shown in the ads | Linked to B2 |
| Meat paradox | The image of a good life eases the tension between liking animals and eating them | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |
| Willful ignorance | People avoid learning how the animals really lived and died | Linked to E7 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a good life followed by a quick death makes death acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dogs and cats | They could be killed after a few years of good life | Almost no one accepts this |
| Humans | A happy person could be killed painlessly | Absurd |
| Rescued animals | Sanctuaries could slaughter them after a while | Contradicts the very reason sanctuaries exist |

## 11. What the argument gets right

A life with good welfare is much better than a life in confinement, and a painless death is much better than a painful one. It's also true that nature holds enormous suffering, and that idealizing wild life would be a mistake.

But none of this turns death into a favor. If the animal's life is good, the best thing to do for it is to let it live.
