# K12. "The reason doesn't matter, health and the environment convince more people"

**Thesis:** health and environmental arguments can lead people to consume fewer animal products, and that helps animals in the short term. But the reason shapes what happens next. Someone who stops for their health or for the climate has no reason to keep going when a "healthy" or low-impact animal product comes along, may swap beef for chicken and fish, which add up to far more individuals per kilo, and doesn't learn to care about animals outside the plate, such as wild animals and the minds that may arise in the future. And the idea that these arguments convince more people hasn't been shown: the evidence is mixed, and the effects of every kind of appeal are small.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "For the animals the reason doesn't matter; what matters is that people stop consuming. And health and the environment convince far more people."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** What counts for animals is how many of them are exploited and killed.
2. **P2.** A person who stops consuming animal products reduces that exploitation equally, whatever the reason.
3. **P3.** Health and environmental arguments convince more people than arguments centered on animals.
4. **C.** So those who defend animals should prefer health and environmental arguments.

The argument is **valid** in form. P1 has a true part, but it's too narrow: it leaves out animals who aren't exploited, such as wild animals, and those who will exist in the future. P2 is the premise that fails: different reasons produce different behavior when circumstances change. And P3 isn't established: the available studies show no clear advantage for any kind of appeal.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone stops pulling the cat's tail only because their mom said they'd be punished. When mom leaves the room, what happens? Now imagine someone who stops because they understood it hurts the cat. That person will look after the cat even when alone, and the dog, the little bird and animals they've never even met.

## 4. Short answer

In the short term, you may be right: if someone stops eating meat, fewer animals die, whatever the reason. But think about what happens next. Someone who stopped for their health comes back when a "healthy" animal product comes along. Someone who stopped for the climate may swap beef for chicken, and one cow yields as much meat as about 200 chickens. And neither reason makes the person care about the animals who aren't on the plate. As for convincing more people: the studies don't show that clearly, and every kind of appeal has small effects. I use the health and environmental facts when someone says a vegan diet is dangerous or that soy is destroying the Amazon. But the reason that doesn't change with the next nutrition study is that animals feel.

**Question to ask back:** "If tomorrow there were a healthy, low-carbon chicken meat, what would be left of the argument for not eating it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Reason for stopping | What the person learns | What happens when circumstances change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Health | The criterion is my own well-being | Fish, "lean" chicken or a new study in favor of meat bring the person back |
| Environment | The criterion is the impact on the planet | "Regenerative" meat, insects or swapping beef for chicken become acceptable |
| Animals | The criterion is who feels | The reason still stands and extends to other animals |

The objection combines two ideas. The first is that the reason doesn't change the consequences for animals. The second is that indirect arguments, about health and the environment, convince more people because people already accept those values. The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VIII, ch. 4) examines both and concludes that neither holds as a rule.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The reason decides what the person does next.** An indirect argument only covers cases where the animal product harms health or the environment. If a "healthy" fish, a "happy" hen's egg or a pasture-raised meat sold as regenerative comes along (see H9), the argument no longer applies, and the person has no reason to continue. Someone who stopped because animals feel doesn't depend on the next study.

**2. The swap to smaller animals.** A health argument against red meat or a climate argument against beef can lead people to chicken and fish. According to Faunalytics data compiled by Our World in Data, in 2023 one head of cattle yielded on average about 364 kg of meat, a chicken about 1.7 kg and a fish about 4.4 kg. Replacing the meat of one cow with chicken means raising and killing about 200 chickens. The swap can lower emissions per kilo and, at the same time, multiply the number of victims. The meta-analysis by Green, Smith and Mathur (2025) notes that interventions aimed only at red and processed meat have larger effects, but that it's unknown whether they reduce consumption of other animal products, and it considers substitution with poultry and fish likely. This is a hypothesis, not yet measured directly, but it fits what health guidelines themselves recommend. Insect farming, promoted as sustainable protein, takes the same logic even further (H18).

**3. Animals outside the plate are left out.** The argument only counts exploitation. Cunha points out that harms suffered by wild animals from natural processes also matter, as do new forms of suffering that may arise in the future, such as in digital minds (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. IX, ch. 10). An environmentalist argument can even work against these animals, if it teaches that what matters is the ecosystem and not those who live in it. A small 2016 study by Animal Charity Evaluators, with about 600 participants, gave a signal in this direction: people who read an environmental leaflet showed slightly less support for helping wild animals. But the effect was close to the significance threshold, and Faunalytics itself, summarizing it, described the result as somewhat inconclusive.

**4. "Convinces more" hasn't been shown.** The evidence is mixed:
- The meta-analysis by Mathur and colleagues (2021), covering 100 studies, concluded that animal welfare appeals reduce meat consumption intentions and consumption, at least in the short term. Most studies measured intentions or self-reported consumption.
- The meta-analysis by Green, Smith and Mathur (2025), restricted to randomized trials with a control group that measured consumption at least one day after the intervention, found small effects for every kind of appeal. The estimate was slightly larger for health and the environment than for animal welfare, but in none of the three cases was the difference from zero clear, and the authors didn't formally test the difference between them. The conclusion is that meaningfully reducing consumption of animal products remains an unsolved problem.
- Hopwood and colleagues (2020) found health to be the reason most often given by people considering going vegetarian. But in the same study, environmental and animal rights motives predicted reactions to flyers on those themes, while the health motive didn't predict reactions to health flyers. Being the most common reason isn't the same as being the argument that changes behavior most.

**5. People who stop for animals tend to last longer, with caveats.** In a survey of vegetarians, Hoffman and colleagues (2013) found that ethical vegetarians had more conviction, more restrictions and longer adherence than health vegetarians. In the 2014 Faunalytics study of current and former vegetarians and vegans, health was the only motivation cited by a majority of those who quit (58%) (M2). These are correlational studies: they don't prove that the reason causes the duration. And the same Faunalytics study shows that, among those who quit, those motivated by animals went on to eat much like the others. The ethical reason is no guarantee, but it's the only one that doesn't lose its point when the facts about health or impact change.

**6. The reason is what gets passed on.** Cunha observes that a person's influence through their own consumption lasts a lifetime, while the values they pass on affect how other people decide, for generations. A successful indirect argument changes a menu; a direct argument also teaches that animals matter. That's why this guide asks for [restraint with indirect arguments](../alem/argumentos-diretos.html): use them to correct facts, not as the foundation.

**7. Nobody knows for sure which strategy is best.** Cunha himself acknowledges that there's still no data to compare the long-term effectiveness of strategies, and calls for an open mind to change position in the face of new evidence. The honest answer isn't "direct arguments always convince more", but rather: there's no evidence that indirect ones convince more, and there are good reasons to think that, on their own, they leave many animals out.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the indirect argument is a gateway

The most serious version says that many people defend speciesism not out of conviction but because they like the taste of meat. If a person stops consuming, for whatever reason, they lose that interest and become more open to accepting that animals matter. Many come for health and stay for the animals.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It calls for combining, not replacing.** If the indirect argument only opens the door, the direct argument is still needed for the person to walk through. Cunha notes that, in that case, it's probably more efficient to spread consideration for animals directly.
2. **The premise is uncertain.** Someone who only gives up meat for health or the environment, and not because of the enormous suffering of animals, shows they give strong weight to those other values. There's no guarantee they will come to consider animals later.
3. **No one has to pick just one.** Restraint isn't prohibition. People who came for health are welcome; the problem is letting the indirect argument take the place of the direct one.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hidden premise | Assumes every reason leads to the same behavior, in any circumstance |
| Epistemological | [Cherry picking](../falacias.html#cherry-picking) | Cites the popularity of the health motive and leaves out how long the change lasts and the substitutions |
| Epistemological | Confusing popularity with effectiveness | Takes the most cited motive to be the argument that changes behavior most |
| Moral | Counting only today's consumption | Leaves out wild animals and the sentient beings of the future |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says what matters is the animals.** But chooses arguments in which the animals don't appear.
2. **Says the goal is to reduce exploitation.** But accepts arguments that can increase the number of animals killed, through swapping beef for chicken and fish.
3. **Says only the result matters.** But doesn't measure the long-term result, only the immediate change.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains K12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present bias | Today's reduction weighs more than the values that will guide future decisions | General literature on temporal discounting |
| Scope insensitivity | Counts kilos of meat, not the number of individuals killed | [Our World in Data, meat per animal](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/kilograms-meat-per-animal) |
| Sizeism | Chickens and fish seem to weigh less in the account because they are small | Linked to H18 |
| Anthropocentric thinking | Assumes only arguments about human interests can convince | [*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VIII, ch. 4](https://questaodeetica.biblioverse.org/en/v8/04-the-use-of-anthropocentric-and-environmentalist-arguments.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "the reason for a change doesn't matter, only the immediate effect".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workplace discrimination | Convincing a company not to discriminate only because it loses money would be enough | When discrimination becomes profitable, the argument no longer applies |
| Traffic | Teaching people to respect the speed limit only because of fines would be enough | Without speed cameras, behavior changes |
| Other movements | Defending a human group's rights only because discrimination harms the health of those who discriminate would be the best strategy | No human rights movement accepted that as its foundation |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that, for a particular animal, it makes no difference whether the person who stopped buying them acted for health, for the climate or for them. It's right that many people come to veganism for health or environmental reasons, and that the facts about nutrition and environmental impact are real and useful for answering objections. And it's right that effectiveness matters: no one knows for sure which strategy convinces more.

The mistake is treating the reason as neutral. The reason decides what the person does when circumstances change, which animals they swap for which, and whether they will care about those who aren't on the plate. Only the argument centered on animals covers all these cases.
