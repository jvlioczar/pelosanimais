# I3. "Cosmetics tested on animals are safer"

**Thesis:** cosmetics aren't medicine: there's no disease to cure that would justify the testing. And safety doesn't depend on it. The European Union has banned the sale of cosmetics tested on animals since 2013, and Brazil banned in 2023 the use of vertebrates in testing cosmetics with ingredients of known effect. Companies use already-assessed ingredients and animal-free methods, such as skin models reconstructed in the lab.

**Family:** I. Other uses: testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companionship · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I prefer cosmetics tested on animals. At least I know they won't give me an allergy."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Cosmetics can cause irritation and allergies.
2. **P2.** Animal tests identify these risks before sale.
3. **P3.** Consumer safety is worth more than the use of some animals.
4. **C.** So testing cosmetics on animals is justifiable.

P1 is true. P2 is partial: animal models predict many human reactions poorly, and there are validated animal-free methods. P3 ignores that there are ways of ensuring safety without tests.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and P3.

## 3. Answer for children

In the past, to find out whether a shampoo stung the eyes, people put shampoo in rabbits' eyes. Today scientists can test on little pieces of skin made in the lab. And many countries have banned testing makeup and shampoo on animals.

## 4. Short answer

Cosmetics aren't medicine: there's no disease to cure that would justify the testing. And safety doesn't depend on it: the European Union has banned the sale of cosmetics tested on animals since 2013, and Brazil banned in 2023 the use of vertebrates in testing cosmetics with ingredients of known effect. Companies use already-assessed ingredients and animal-free methods, such as lab-reconstructed skin.

**Question to ask back:** "If cosmetics sold in Europe are safe without animal testing, what does the test add?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| Question | Medicines (I1) | Cosmetics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there a medical need? | Yes, in many cases | No |
| Are there animal-free methods? | Partly | Yes, for most assessments |
| What does the law require? | Still requires testing in many cases | EU bans; Brazil restricts |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. There's no medical need.** The medicines argument, debatable as it is, appeals to lives saved (I1). With cosmetics, the benefit is aesthetic.

**2. Safety is ensured in other ways.** The European Union has banned animal testing of cosmetics since 2009 and the sale of cosmetics tested on animals since 2013, under Regulation 1223/2009. Products sold there are still assessed, using ingredients of known safety and alternative methods.

**3. Brazil has changed too.** Normative Resolution 58 of Brazil's National Council for the Control of Animal Experimentation (CONCEA), in force since March 2023, bans the use of vertebrates in research and quality control for cosmetics, personal hygiene products and perfumes with ingredients of known effect. Animal protection organizations point out that the resolution doesn't ban selling products tested abroad.

**4. Animals are poor models for many human reactions.** Differences between species mean that animal tests predict some reactions in people poorly, which drove the development of methods using human cells and tissues.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: new ingredients still need to be assessed

The most serious version points out that new ingredients, with no history of use, need safety assessment, and that not all effects can be predicted without animals.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **No one needs a new cosmetic ingredient.** There are thousands of ingredients already assessed.
2. **Alternative methods keep advancing.** The European rule has worked for more than a decade without animal testing.
3. **The benefit doesn't justify the harm.** Even with some uncertainty, an aesthetic advantage doesn't outweigh animals' suffering.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The question is whether it's fair to make animals suffer for a beauty product. When not even safety depends on it, only habit is left.

| Logical | False equivalence | Cosmetics are treated as if they were medicine |

| Moral | Aesthetic benefit above suffering | Avoiding a possible allergy justifies others' pain |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Buys European cosmetics.** Which can't be tested on animals.
2. **Loves their own pet.** And accepts that rabbits and guinea pigs suffer for a lipstick.
3. **Doubts safety without testing.** Without knowing that most ingredients are already known.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "any safety gain justifies animal testing".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testing perfumes on dogs | It would be acceptable | Almost no one accepts that |
| The 2013 European ban | It would have made cosmetics dangerous | There's no evidence of that |
| Medicines for serious diseases | The debate is different | See I1 |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that cosmetics can cause reactions and that consumer safety matters. It's also true that genuinely new ingredients require careful assessment.

But European experience and the Brazilian rule show that safety can be ensured without animals. For a product with no medical need, the harm isn't justified.
