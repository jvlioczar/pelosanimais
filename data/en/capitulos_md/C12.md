# C12. "If we don't eat them, animals will take over the planet"

**Thesis:** the argument gets the cause backwards. Farmed animals exist in enormous numbers because they are bred: inseminated, hatched and fed for that purpose. If demand fell, fewer animals would be bred, and there would be no explosion of cows and chickens. Today, farmed animals already make up about 60% of the mass of all mammals on the planet precisely because we eat them.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "If no one ate meat, cows would take over. There'd be chickens everywhere."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Farmed animals reproduce.
2. **P2.** Without slaughter, they would keep reproducing out of control.
3. **C.** So eating animals is necessary to control their populations.

P1 is true in a general sense. P2 is false: the reproduction of farmed animals is controlled by the farmers themselves.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which reverses the relationship between breeding and population.

## 3. Answer for children

Farms make lots of animals be born on purpose, because they want to sell the meat. If people bought less meat, farms would have fewer animals born. It's like a bakery: if no one buys bread, it bakes less bread, and it doesn't end up full of leftover bread forever.

## 4. Short answer

It's the opposite: farmed animals are numerous because they're bred to be eaten. They're born through artificial insemination and in hatcheries, in the numbers the market asks for. If demand fell, fewer animals would be bred, and there'd be no planet full of cows. Today farmed animals make up about 60% of the mass of all the world's mammals, precisely because of consumption.

**Question to ask back:** "If people ate less chicken, would hatcheries keep incubating the same billions of eggs?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument pictures farmed animals as a wild population that reproduces on its own. In reality:

| How the argument pictures it | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| The animals are born and need to be controlled | The animals are born because their births were scheduled |
| Consumption reduces the population | Consumption is the cause of the population |
| Without consumption, there'd be overpopulation | Without consumption, there'd be far fewer animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Reproduction is controlled.** Chickens hatch in hatcheries; pigs and cows are artificially inseminated on a large scale. The population exists because it is produced.

**2. Demand creates the population.** Farmed animals make up about 60% of the planet's mammal biomass, and farmed birds about 70% of bird biomass. That figure is the result of production for consumption.

**3. The transition would be gradual.** No one stops eating meat all at once worldwide. With demand falling gradually, farmers would simply breed fewer animals (J19).

**4. Many farmed animals wouldn't even survive on their own.** Breeds selected to grow fast or produce a lot of milk depend on human management. Some species, like pigs, can form wild populations when they escape, as feral pigs have in the United States, but that is a local management problem created by farming itself, not a takeover of the planet (H16).

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |

| [Zuidhof et al., 2014](https://www.thepoultrysite.com/articles/growth-efficiency-and-yield-of-commercial-broilers-from-1957-1978-and-2005) | Broiler chickens were bred to grow about four times faster | Shows their dependence on human management |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: what about the animals that already exist?

The most serious version admits the population is bred, but asks what would happen to the animals that already exist if consumption fell suddenly.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The change wouldn't be sudden.** Consumption changes gradually, and production adjusts.
2. **The existing animals could live out their lives.** Either in sanctuaries, or with a gradual reduction in births (J19).
3. **The question doesn't justify continuing to breed.** Even if dealing with existing animals were hard, that's no reason to bring billions more into the world to die.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Reversed causation | Treats a consequence of consumption as a reason to consume |
| Epistemological | Not knowing how production works | Ignores that reproduction is controlled |
| Epistemological | Unrealistic scenario | Imagines consumption ending abruptly |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Knows production follows demand.** For any other product, they understand that fewer sales mean less production.
2. **Doesn't fear overpopulation of other domesticated animals.** Species bred in smaller numbers don't "take over" anything.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains C12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated reasoning | An imaginary scenario serves to excuse change | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Status quo bias | The current number of animals seems natural | [Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/jrisku/v1y1988i1p7-59.html) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if we stop consuming something, it piles up out of control".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bread | If no one bought it, bakeries would be full forever | Bakeries would bake less |
| Cars | If no one bought them, factories would keep producing | Production would fall |
| Purebred dogs | Without buyers, there'd be an explosion of puppies | Breeders would stop breeding |

## 11. What the argument gets right

The question of what would happen to farmed animals in a world with less consumption is legitimate and deserves careful answers, involving a gradual transition, sanctuaries and public policy.

But the fear of overpopulation reverses reality. Farmed animals aren't a pest to control; they are individuals brought into the world to be eaten. Eating less doesn't multiply them: it means fewer of them are born to die.
