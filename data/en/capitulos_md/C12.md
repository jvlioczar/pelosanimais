# C12. "We are animals and part of the ecosystem; no one lives without impact"

**Thesis:** it's true that we are animals, that we depend on ecosystems and that no life is free of impact. Veganism never claimed otherwise: its definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". What doesn't follow is the rest: from "harm can't be brought to zero" you can't conclude "it makes no difference how much harm I cause", and being part of an ecosystem gives no permission to kill those we don't need to kill. "Taking part consciously" describes the state of mind of the one who kills, not what happens to the one who dies.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "We're animals too, we're part of the ecosystem. Thinking you can live outside the cycle, with no impact at all, is an illusion. What matters is taking part consciously."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Humans are animals and depend on ecosystems in which every living being affects others.
2. **P2.** No way of eating is free of impact: crops also take up land and kill animals.
3. **P3.** If no option is free of impact, what separates the options is the awareness with which we take part in the cycle, not whether they include killing animals.
4. **C.** So eating animals consciously is as legitimate as not eating them.

This argument is common among advocates of regenerative agriculture, and P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: it jumps from "every option has some impact" to "the size and kind of impact don't separate the options". Between causing some unavoidable harm and deliberately killing someone who didn't need to be killed there is a difference the word "conscious" doesn't erase.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3.

## 3. Answer for children

It's true: we're animals too, and everything we do changes nature a little. Even walking on grass flattens it. But stepping on an ant by accident is very different from hurting a little animal on purpose. We can't live without bothering anyone, but we can choose to bother others as little as possible.

## 4. Short answer

I agree with the first part: we are animals and no one lives without impact. Veganism never said anything else, and its definition says "as far as is possible and practicable". But if harm can't be brought to zero, the question becomes how much harm I cause, and to whom. Being part of the ecosystem doesn't explain why I should kill those I don't need to kill. And "conscious" is a word about me. For the animal, being killed by someone mindful or by someone distracted comes to the same thing.

**Question to ask back:** "If we can't live without causing any harm, does that mean it makes no difference whether we cause a little or a lot?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument joins true statements to a conclusion that doesn't come out of them:

| What the argument says | Is it right? | What actually follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| We are animals | Yes | Nothing about what we ought to do: it describes what we are |
| We depend on ecosystems | Yes | A reason to look after them, not a license to kill |
| No one lives without impact | Yes | That we should reduce impact, since we can't eliminate it |
| What matters is the awareness of the one taking part | No | The killer's awareness doesn't change what the victim loses |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It answers something veganism doesn't say.** The definition from the Vegan Society, the British organization that coined the word "vegan", speaks of excluding exploitation and cruelty "as far as is possible and practicable". No one there promises a life outside the cycle or without impact. The "illusion" being criticized is a straw man (L5).

**2. It's the nirvana fallacy.** That's the name for rejecting a better option because it isn't perfect. No hospital cures every patient, and no one concludes that it makes no difference whether we treat people or not. If harm can't be brought to zero, that's a reason to reduce it, not to stop measuring it.

**3. It's an appeal to nature.** From "we are part of an ecosystem in which some eat others" you can't conclude "we ought to eat others". The Scottish philosopher David Hume noted in the eighteenth century that you can't move from a description of how things are to a rule about how they ought to be without an extra premise (the so-called is-ought gap). Disease, infanticide and starvation are also part of nature, and we draw no permission from them (C1, C2).

**4. "Conscious" describes the one who kills, not the one who dies.** Attention, gratitude and respect are states of the eater. The animal loses its life all the same. Judging an act only by the inner disposition of the person doing it, without looking at who the act is done to, forgets precisely the interested party (C7).

**5. The impacts aren't equal.** The argument suggests that, because crops kill too, everything evens out. But raising animals takes more crops, not fewer: according to Our World in Data, if the world adopted a plant-based diet, agricultural land would fall by about 75%, and cropland would shrink too. Anyone who takes "all food has an impact" seriously has one more reason to eat plants (H2).

**6. The human test.** Humans are animals too, and are also part of ecosystems. No one accepts that someone may kill a person and explain that "no one lives without impact" and that they acted "consciously". If the reasoning doesn't justify harm to humans, it isn't what justifies harm to other animals: some other premise is doing the work, and that premise is the one that needs defending (A10).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: joining the cycle is more honest than outsourcing death

The most serious version says: those who raise and kill their own food, in a system that looks after the soil, face death directly and take responsibility. Those who eat plants from monocultures kill without seeing it (rodents, insects, birds) and think themselves innocent. It would be more honest to take part in the cycle with open eyes.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Seeing death doesn't make it necessary.** Facing what you do is better than hiding it, but the question remains: did anyone need to be killed?
2. **Killing on purpose and causing accidental deaths aren't the same thing.** Everywhere else in life we distinguish the driver who hits someone by accident from the one who does it on purpose, and we ask both to reduce the risk.
3. **The tally of accidental deaths favors plants.** Because farmed animals eat crops, eating animals adds the deaths of the harvest to the deaths of the slaughterhouse (H2).
4. **You can take part consciously without killing.** Caring for the soil, supporting agroecology and cutting waste are ways of taking part in the cycle that require no victims (H15, H16).

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Nirvana fallacy | Because harm can't reach zero, it treats reducing and not reducing as equivalent |
| Logical | Appeal to nature (is-ought gap) | From "we are part of the ecosystem" it concludes "we may kill" |
| Logical | Straw man | Attributes to veganism the promise of a life without impact |
| Epistemological | False equivalence | Treats impacts of very different sizes as if they were equal |
| Moral | Agent-centered focus | Judges the act by the awareness of the one who kills, not by what happens to the one who dies |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't apply the reasoning to humans.** No one accepts "no one lives without impact" as a defense for hurting a person.
2. **Measures impact in everything else.** Advocates of regenerative agriculture compare soils, emissions and systems with care. Only for the death of animals is the comparison waived.
3. **Invokes the ecosystem while living outside it.** Medicine, houses and supermarkets show that we choose all the time which parts of nature to take part in.
4. **Says everything has an impact, but picks the larger one.** If impact matters, the option that uses less land and kills fewer should count in its favor.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "since no one lives without causing harm, what matters is the awareness with which it is caused, not how much harm is caused".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pollution | Since everyone pollutes a little, consciously dumping sewage in the river would be acceptable | No one accepts this |
| Traffic | Since every driver poses some risk, driving drunk while mindful of it would be acceptable | The size of the risk matters |
| Harm to people | Someone who hurt a person could claim to be part of the ecosystem and to have acted consciously | No one accepts this |
| Hunting for fun | It would be legitimate as long as the hunter felt grateful | Gratitude changes nothing for the animal |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It gets important things right. We are animals, and the idea that humans stand above or outside nature has done a great deal of damage. No diet is free of impact: crops take up land, harvests kill animals, and some vegans do talk as if their own plate were innocent. Remembering this is a good antidote to perfectionism and moral arrogance. It's also right that attention to the soil and to how food is produced matters.

But the concession stops here: recognizing that zero harm is impossible is the start of the conversation about how to reduce it, not the end. Being part of the ecosystem explains why we cause some impact; it doesn't justify killing those we don't need to kill. The awareness that counts is the kind that changes what happens to others.
