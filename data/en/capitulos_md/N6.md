# N6. "We didn't cause it, so we have no duty to help"

**Thesis:** the difference between causing harm and failing to prevent it may carry some moral weight, but it doesn't erase the duty to help when helping is possible and costs little. No one would accept this reasoning about a drowning child or flood victims. The feeling that omission is innocent is a known bias, omission bias, and it weighs more heavily when the victims are animals.

**Family:** N. Wild animals, new minds and the future · **Part:** DIR

**Note:** this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I didn't do anything to these animals. If they suffer in nature, it's not my problem."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** We have a strong duty not to cause harm to others.
2. **P2.** The duty to help those who suffer from causes we didn't create is, at most, weak and optional.
3. **P3.** The suffering of wild animals wasn't caused by us.
4. **C.** So we have no duty to help them.

P1 and P3 are acceptable. P2 has serious defenders in philosophy, under the name of the doing/allowing distinction. The problem is drawing from it the conclusion that there's no duty at all.

The argument is **valid**, but P2, in its strong form, doesn't survive the human cases we all accept.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday conversation | "Nature's problems aren't my problem." |
| Everyday conversation | "We have a duty not to harm, not to help." |
| Activism | "Veganism is not exploiting; the rest isn't our business." (which leads to N14) |
| Academic version | "Negative duties are strict; positive duties are imperfect." |
| Disguised version | "I don't kill; I just eat." (which is K3) |

## 3. Answer for children

If you saw a kitten stuck in a drain, you weren't the one who put it there. Even so, you'd want to call someone to get it out, wouldn't you? Not having caused the problem doesn't mean we can't help. Sometimes helping is the most important thing.

## 4. Short answer

No one threw the child into the pond, and yet you probably think whoever can save it without great risk should save it. Not having caused suffering doesn't release us from helping when we can. And for the victim, it makes no difference whether the harm came from someone or from nature.

**Question to ask back:** "If you saw a child drowning and could save them without risk, would you say it isn't your duty because you weren't the one who pushed them in?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument depends on turning a difference of degree into an all-or-nothing difference:

| Position | What it says | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Doing and allowing weigh the same | Failing to save is as serious as killing | Enormous duties to help |
| Doing weighs more than allowing | Causing harm is worse than failing to prevent it, but failing still counts | Real duties to help, proportional to cost and benefit |
| Allowing weighs nothing | There's only a duty not to cause | No one would need to save the child in the pond |

The argument needs the third position, which almost no one accepts when the victim is human.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The child in the pond.** Peter Singer proposed in 1972: if you can save a child drowning in a shallow pond at the cost of muddying your clothes, you should save it. Almost everyone agrees, and no one caused the drowning.

**2. Omission seems lighter than it is.** Psychology experiments show that people judge harm caused by omission as less serious than the same harm caused by action, even when outcome and intention are equal. This is omission bias. It explains the feeling of innocence, but doesn't justify it.

**3. The human test.** Faced with a flood, an earthquake or an epidemic, we accept that governments, organizations and individuals should help, even though they didn't cause the disaster. No one says helping victims is optional because the rain is to blame. If we deny this reasoning to animals, the victims' species is doing the deciding.

**4. Even defenders of the distinction accept helping.** Those who hold that duties not to harm are stronger usually accept there are reasons to help when the cost is small and the benefit large. The scale of suffering in nature makes those reasons strong.

**5. Helping doesn't require heroism.** The duty to help can start with the simplest things: not opposing those who help, supporting research, and arguing that the wellbeing of wild animals be considered in decisions we already make.

**6. The requirement of having caused it doesn't hold up in any major theory.** Let's call "the origin requirement" the idea that we are only answerable for harms that arose from our own practices. Those who defend it in the debate on wild animals include Bovenkerk and colleagues (2003), the Brazilian philosopher Sônia T. Felipe (2015), Clare Palmer (2015) and Paul Taylor (1986). But consequentialism openly rejects it. Virtue ethics would hardly applaud someone who stays indifferent to immense suffering just because they didn't cause it. A contract made without knowing which species each party would belong to would approve a duty to help, whatever the origin of the harm. And deontology, even when it gives more weight to not harming, only needs to limit the *means* of helping, not deny the duty (Cunha, 2022, pp. 192–193). Many deontologists, such as Philippa Foot and Frances Kamm, hold that doing weighs more than allowing; but that's the second row of the table above, not the third.

**7. The rock and the doctor.** A rock rolls downhill toward someone with their back turned, and you can warn them with a shout. Saying "I didn't make it roll" doesn't excuse you from shouting. A doctor who refused to treat a cancer because "I didn't put it in you" would be reported, not praised (Cunha, 2022, p. 193).

**8. By the proximate-cause criterion, no one causes anything.** It's said that when we fail to help, the harm is caused by the disease or the hunger, not by us. By the same criterion, whoever shoots kills no one, the bullet does; and whoever buys meat causes no death, the slaughterhouse worker or their tool does (K3). The criterion would also eliminate the duties not to harm that its defenders accept (Cunha, 2022, p. 195).

**9. "It would have happened anyway" doesn't separate acts from omissions either.** It's said that natural harms would occur even without us, while the harms of our acts depend on us. But there are acts whose result would occur anyway, because someone else would have done the same, and omissions where, without us, someone else would have helped. What matters isn't what the world would be like without us, but how it can turn out depending on what we decide (Cunha, 2022, pp. 195–197).

**10. Intention assesses character, not duty.** Someone who lets a child drown because they don't want to leave the TV has no intention of killing the child, and still failed a duty. Intention helps measure someone's guilt, but doesn't decide whether there was something to do (Cunha, 2022, pp. 198–199).

**11. We thank people for what is hard, not for omissions.** It's said that we thank those who help us, but not those who refrain from attacking us, so helping would be beyond duty. But we also thank the driver who swerves into a wall so as not to run us over, and that is refraining from causing harm. What makes gratitude fitting is effort or cost, not the difference between acting and omitting (Cunha, 2022, pp. 199–200).

**12. The uncertainty of helping doesn't outweigh the certainty of omission.** It's said that we predict the effects of what we do better than the effects of what we fail to do. But the harm of letting nature take its course is certain and enormous, while the risk of a well-planned intervention is uncertain and usually small. Comparing outcomes by severity multiplied by probability, the expected impact, doesn't favor omission (Cunha, 2022, pp. 200–201).

**13. Faceless victims are still victims.** A bomb dropped from afar kills people the attacker will never know, and that doesn't make it less serious than a punch. That nature's victims are anonymous explains why they're easy to forget, but doesn't lessen what they lose (Cunha, 2022, p. 202).

**14. "I only have to avoid making anyone worse off" isn't enough either.** Some say we only have a duty not to leave anyone below a minimum, or not to reduce anyone's wellbeing. But omission also leaves people below the minimum: those already starving keep starving, and untreated disease progresses. And the typical life of a wild animal is already below any reasonable minimum. The worse off someone is, the stronger the reason to help (Cunha, 2022, pp. 205–208).

**15. Proximity and reciprocity prove too much.** Some say we should only help those close to us or those who can return the favor. Imagine three buttons: A protects all human beings from cancer, B only the people close to us, C does nothing. No one would find it acceptable to press B when A is within reach (Faria, 2015). And whoever uses the lack of a relationship against wild animals loses it against exploitation: we have no relationship with farmed animals either (Cunha, 2022, pp. 209–213).

**16. A debt to domesticated animals gives priority, not exclusivity.** Aaron Simmons (2009) and Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka (2011) argue that we owe domesticated animals, because of domestication, what we don't owe wild ones. Even granting the debt, it would at most give priority to domesticated animals, not a reason to ignore the others (Cunha, 2022, pp. 123–124; N7).

**17. Part of the duty is simply to stop doing things.** Many human practices don't cause harm directly, but create the conditions for it to happen. For Cunha (2022, pp. 197–198, 208, 220), restoring or expanding natural areas increases the number of animals born exposed to hunger, predation and disease; so part of the duty toward wild animals would be negative: not creating new victims. This thesis, which he shares with Oscar Horta and Brian Tomasik, is highly contested, especially by conservationists and climate policy (N16). But the logical point holds even for those who reject the thesis: "we didn't cause it" doesn't describe well everything we do.

**18. Not even Singer says that failing to act is always as serious as causing.** In *Practical Ethics* (ch. 8), he denies that there is an *intrinsic* difference between killing and letting die, but he recognizes five differences that usually come with the two cases: motivation, how hard the duty is to fulfil, the certainty of the outcome, the identifiable victim and causal responsibility, which is precisely the "we didn't cause it". For him, motivation and certainty of outcome carry moral weight, so failing to help shouldn't be condemned as killing; it is closer to reckless driving, which is also serious. The idea that we only answer for what we cause would, in his view, rest on a questionable notion of responsibility, perhaps backed by a theory of rights like Locke's or Nozick's. He also separates assessing the act from blaming the person. Singer is discussing human poverty, not wild animals; the transfer is this guide's. But the lesson holds here: granting that an omission may weigh less isn't the same as saying it weighs nothing.

**19. Intention weighs on judgment, not on pain.** Brian Tomasik notes that intention matters for judging the one who acts, but doesn't change how much a harm hurts the one who suffers it. Because we react more strongly to intentional harm, we tend to underestimate the immense unintentional suffering in nature: the young buffalo eaten alive feels the same as it would if someone had hurt it on purpose.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the duty to help has no limit

The most serious version says: if failing to help counts, we'd be guilty of all the suffering we could have prevented in the world, human and animal. Such a morality would be impossible to fulfil. The doing/allowing distinction exists precisely to give our obligations a reasonable limit.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **A limit isn't zero.** It's reasonable for the duty to help to have cost limits. That doesn't lead to the conclusion that there's no duty at all.
2. **We apply reasonable limits to humans.** We don't require each person to give everything they have, but we expect them to help when it's easy. The same standard, applied to animals, already asks far more than we do today.
3. **The first step costs almost nothing.** Taking the problem seriously, supporting research and not obstructing those who help are well within any reasonable limit.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in N6 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | False dichotomy | Either the duty to help equals the duty not to harm, or it doesn't exist |
| Epistemological | Omission bias | Treats omission as innocent because it seems less serious |
| Moral | Double standard | Accepts the duty of rescue for humans and denies it for animals |
| Moral | Focus on the agent | Asks only "who caused it?" and forgets "who suffers?" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Thinks the child in the pond should be saved.** Without having caused the drowning.
2. **Gives to or supports disaster relief.** Without having caused the disaster.
3. **Approves of rescuing animals in floods and fires.** Which already admits a duty to help animals one didn't harm.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "we only have obligations regarding suffering we caused".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A drowning child | There'd be no duty to save them | Almost no one accepts this |
| Disaster victims | Humanitarian aid would be merely optional charity | Many countries treat it as a duty |
| A person collapsed in the street | Walking past would be morally neutral | In several countries, failing to assist is even a crime |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that causing harm is usually worse than failing to prevent it, and that our duties to help need reasonable limits. It's also right that, for exploited animals, the first duty is to stop harming them.

But "worse" doesn't mean "the only one". When we can relieve enormous suffering at no great cost, not having been the cause isn't an answer, for humans or for animals.
