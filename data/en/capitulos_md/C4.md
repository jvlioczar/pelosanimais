# C4. "Eating meat made us evolve"

**Thesis:** even if meat had been decisive in human evolution, an adaptive past doesn't dictate present morality: eating meat today doesn't make our brains keep growing, and no gain would by itself justify the harm done to animals. And the claim itself is disputed: a 2022 study showed that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating after the emergence of *Homo erectus* disappears once research effort is accounted for. Cooking, not just meat, stands out as a strong explanation.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Without meat, we'd still be in the trees. Our brains grew thanks to meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Eating meat provided nutrients and energy that allowed the human brain to grow.
2. **P2.** We owe our intelligence and our civilization to that past.
3. **C.** So eating meat is a legitimate part of what made us human, and there's no reason to give it up.

The strong version rests on the hypothesis known as "meat made us human", which has been very influential in anthropology. It deserves respect as a scientific hypothesis.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because P1 is disputed and because, even if true, it doesn't lead to the conclusion: a fact about the past isn't a norm for the present.

## 3. Answer for children

Long ago, our ancestors did many things to survive: they slept in caves, had no medicine and hunted. That helped our species get here. But today we live differently: we have houses, medicine and food at the store. What was needed in the past doesn't have to be repeated forever. And eating meat today doesn't make anyone's brain keep growing: evolution takes a very, very long time and doesn't depend on what each of us eats for lunch.

## 4. Short answer

Even if meat helped our ancestors, that doesn't tell you what to do today. Eating meat now doesn't make your brain keep growing, because evolution doesn't work through each person's diet. And even if it did, would a gain for you by itself justify the harm to those who die? Our ancestors also lived without medicine and anesthesia, and no one wants to go back to that. And the claim itself is debated: a 2022 study showed that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating in the time of *Homo erectus* disappears once research effort is accounted for. Cooking food, including plants, is another strong explanation.

**Question to ask back:** "If what helped us evolve is mandatory, should we go back to living in caves?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument moves from a hypothesis about the past to a rule about the present:

| Claim | Type | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat contributed to the evolution of the brain | Scientific hypothesis | Disputed |
| So eating meat today is legitimate | Moral conclusion | Doesn't follow from the hypothesis |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The past doesn't dictate the present.** Many things that helped our ancestors survive aren't obligations today, and some would be unacceptable. Moving from "it was useful" to "it should continue" is Hume's law (C1).

**2. The hypothesis is disputed.** In 2022, Barr and colleagues analyzed 59 site levels in eastern Africa, between 2.6 and 1.2 million years ago. They concluded that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating after the emergence of *Homo erectus* disappears once sampling effort is accounted for: the most studied sites show more evidence because they were studied more.

**3. There are alternative explanations.** The anthropologist Richard Wrangham argues that cooking food, including tubers and other plants, was decisive in freeing up energy for the brain. Several factors probably contributed.

**4. Even if true, the hypothesis says nothing about the present.** What our ancestors needed to survive in environments without agriculture tells us nothing about what we need with supermarkets and alternatives.

**5. Eating meat today doesn't make the brain keep growing.** Even if meat helped our ancestors, evolution doesn't work through each person's diet. Natural selection acts on heritable differences over many generations; what someone eats during their life doesn't accumulate into ever-larger brains in their descendants. Nor is there any sign that the human brain has kept growing over the last few thousand years. Some researchers, such as DeSilva and colleagues, even estimate a small recent reduction; others, such as Villmoare and Grabowski, dispute that analysis and conclude that size has stayed stable since our species emerged. In neither scenario is meat making our brains grow.

**6. Even a gain wouldn't justify the harm.** Suppose eating meat still brought some gain in ability. It would remain to explain why that gain authorizes taking the lives of beings who can suffer. A benefit to the one who acts isn't enough to justify the harm to the one who pays for it: both sides have to be weighed, and on the animal's side is their whole life.

**7. The ancestral fantasy.** The argument usually comes with an image: the prehistoric hunter, strong and resourceful, who depended on hunting to survive. That image works as a symbol, picked to lend dignity to a very different habit. Studies of present-day hunter-gatherers, used cautiously as a reference for the past, show very varied diets: in warm climates the average is close to half plants and half animal foods, but the proportions vary widely with the environment. And no ancestor bought meat from animals raised in sheds and slaughtered by the billions. Using the figure of the ancestor to justify supermarket meat is taking an idealized version of the past as if it were a portrait of the people eating today. Pointing this out isn't mocking anyone: it's separating real history from a comforting image.

**8. We also owe a debt to cooperation and empathy.** If the argument held, we should equally value the capacities that, according to anthropology, were decisive for our species, such as cooperating and caring for the vulnerable.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: meat is still the ideal food

The most serious version says that, if meat was important in evolution, our bodies are still adapted to it, and removing it from the diet may carry risks.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It becomes a health argument.** And current evidence shows that well-planned vegan diets are adequate (family G).
2. **Adaptation isn't necessity.** The human body adapts to varied diets; that's what being an omnivore means (C2).
3. **The moral question remains.** Even if meat were ideal, that wouldn't answer whether it's just to get it at others' expense.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hume's law (from is to ought) | From a role in the past it concludes a duty in the present |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges a current practice by its origin |
| Epistemological | Hypothesis treated as fact | Presents a disputed thesis as settled |
| Epistemological | Misunderstood evolution | Assumes that eating meat today keeps making the brain grow |
| Epistemological | Ancestral fantasy | Uses an idealized image of the prehistoric hunter to legitimize an industrial habit |
| Moral | Anachronism | Applies ancient survival conditions to present choices |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't live like their ancestors in anything else.** Housing, medicine and work have changed completely.
2. **Eats meat from animals their ancestors never knew.** Industrially bred chickens and pigs didn't exist.
3. **Values reason, which also evolved.** The capacity to question what we do is part of the same history.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what helped the species evolve should continue".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violence between groups | It should continue, if it helped groups survive | No one accepts this |
| Nomadic life | We should go back to it | Absurd |
| Cooking plants | It would also be mandatory, if the cooking hypothesis is right | The argument doesn't favor only meat |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Human evolution is fascinating, and there are good reasons to think animal foods played some role in it, alongside cooking and other changes. Understanding our history matters, and the subject deserves to be studied without prejudice.

But the past explains where we came from, not where we should go. If evolution gave us the capacity to think about what we do, using that capacity is as human as any ancestral habit.
