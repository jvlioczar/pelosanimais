# C4. "Eating meat made us evolve"

**Thesis:** even if meat had been decisive in human evolution, an adaptive past doesn't dictate present morality. And the claim itself is disputed: a 2022 study showed that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating after the emergence of *Homo erectus* disappears once research effort is accounted for. Cooking, not just meat, stands out as a strong explanation.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Without meat, we'd still be in the trees. Our brains grew thanks to meat."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Eating meat provided nutrients and energy that allowed the human brain to grow.
2. **P2.** We owe our intelligence and our civilization to that past.
3. **C.** So eating meat is a legitimate part of what made us human, and there's no reason to give it up.

The strong version rests on the hypothesis known as "meat made us human", which has been very influential in anthropology. It deserves respect as a scientific hypothesis.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because P1 is disputed and because, even if true, it doesn't lead to the conclusion: a fact about the past isn't a norm for the present.

## 3. Answer for children

Long ago, our ancestors did many things to survive: they slept in caves, had no medicine and hunted. That helped our species get here. But today we live differently: we have houses, medicine and food at the store. What was needed in the past doesn't have to be repeated forever.

## 4. Short answer

Even if meat helped our ancestors, that doesn't tell us what to do today. Our ancestors also lived without medicine and anesthesia, and no one wants to go back to that. And the claim itself is debated: a 2022 study showed that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating in the time of *Homo erectus* disappears once research effort is accounted for. Cooking food, including plants, is another strong explanation.

**Question to ask back:** "If what helped us evolve is mandatory, should we go back to living in caves?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument moves from a hypothesis about the past to a rule about the present:

| Claim | Type | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meat contributed to the evolution of the brain | Scientific hypothesis | Disputed |
| So eating meat today is legitimate | Moral conclusion | Doesn't follow from the hypothesis |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The past doesn't dictate the present.** Many things that helped our ancestors survive aren't obligations today, and some would be unacceptable. Moving from "it was useful" to "it should continue" is Hume's law (C1).

**2. The hypothesis is disputed.** In 2022, Barr and colleagues analyzed 59 site levels in eastern Africa, between 2.6 and 1.2 million years ago. They concluded that the apparent increase in evidence of meat-eating after the emergence of *Homo erectus* disappears once sampling effort is accounted for: the most studied sites show more evidence because they were studied more.

**3. There are alternative explanations.** The anthropologist Richard Wrangham argues that cooking food, including tubers and other plants, was decisive in freeing up energy for the brain. Several factors probably contributed.

**4. Even if true, the hypothesis says nothing about the present.** What our ancestors needed to survive in environments without agriculture tells us nothing about what we need with supermarkets and alternatives.

**5. We also owe a debt to cooperation and empathy.** If the argument held, we should equally value the capacities that, according to anthropology, were decisive for our species, such as cooperating and caring for the vulnerable.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: meat is still the ideal food

The most serious version says that, if meat was important in evolution, our bodies are still adapted to it, and removing it from the diet may carry risks.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It becomes a health argument.** And current evidence shows that well-planned vegan diets are adequate (family G).
2. **Adaptation isn't necessity.** The human body adapts to varied diets; that's what being an omnivore means (C2).
3. **The moral question remains.** Even if meat were ideal, that wouldn't answer whether it's just to get it at others' expense.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hume's law (from is to ought) | From a role in the past it concludes a duty in the present |
| Logical | Genetic fallacy | Judges a current practice by its origin |
| Epistemological | Hypothesis treated as fact | Presents a disputed thesis as settled |
| Moral | Anachronism | Applies ancient survival conditions to present choices |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't live like their ancestors in anything else.** Housing, medicine and work have changed completely.
2. **Eats meat from animals their ancestors never knew.** Industrially bred chickens and pigs didn't exist.
3. **Values reason, which also evolved.** The capacity to question what we do is part of the same history.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what helped the species evolve should continue".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Violence between groups | It should continue, if it helped groups survive | No one accepts this |
| Nomadic life | We should go back to it | Absurd |
| Cooking plants | It would also be mandatory, if the cooking hypothesis is right | The argument doesn't favor only meat |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Human evolution is fascinating, and there are good reasons to think animal foods played some role in it, alongside cooking and other changes. Understanding our history matters, and the subject deserves to be studied without prejudice.

But the past explains where we came from, not where we should go. If evolution gave us the capacity to think about what we do, using that capacity is as human as any ancestral habit.
