# C9. "Farm animals exist for this"

**Thesis:** the idea that cattle are "born to become steak" is a purpose assigned by whoever profits from them, not a property of the animal. Farmed animals were shaped by artificial selection to serve production, and that doesn't make them any less able to suffer. Saying they exist for this is describing what we do to them and presenting it as what they are.

**Family:** C. Nature, biology and evolution · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Cattle are born to become steak. Broiler chickens were made for this. It's their purpose."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Farmed animals were developed by human selection to produce meat, milk or eggs.
2. **P2.** Something created for a purpose fulfills that purpose when it is used for it.
3. **C.** So using these animals for production is fulfilling their purpose, not a wrong.

The strong version has a long history. Aristotle stated that plants exist for animals and animals for man. P1 is true: artificial selection transformed farmed animals.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2, which treats sentient beings as manufactured objects, and in the idea that the purpose assigned by the breeder defines what is good for the bred.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine someone said: "This child was born to work in my factory." Would that mean the child was born for that? No! No one decides what another being's life is for. The chicken and the cow weren't born "for" anything: they were born, they feel and they want to live, like anyone.

## 4. Short answer

The purpose "to become steak" was given by whoever breeds and sells the animal, not by the animal. Artificial selection changed farmed animals' bodies, but it didn't take away their capacity to feel. Broiler chickens were bred to grow so fast that they suffer from it, and that isn't their nature: it's what we did to them. Saying they exist for this is calling our use a purpose.

**Question to ask back:** "Who decided that the steer was born to become steak: the steer, or whoever sells the meat?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument confuses two senses of "purpose":

| Sense | What it means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assigned function | The use someone makes of something | A chair is for sitting; a steer is raised for beef |
| One's own good | What is good for the being itself, from its point of view | A steer is doing well when it isn't suffering, is fed and is alive |

Objects only have the first sense. Sentient beings have the second, and it doesn't depend on the use we make of them (A20).

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The purpose is imposed by whoever profits.** The animal didn't choose to be raised for slaughter. The "purpose" describes our use, not a feature of the animal. It's an **imposed teleology**.

**2. Selection didn't remove sentience.** Farmed chickens, pigs and cattle still feel pain, fear and pleasure. Selection changed the body, not the mind.

**3. Selection created suffering.** Broiler chickens were bred to grow about four times faster than in 1957, and the European scientific authority identifies bone lesions and mobility problems as consequences (B10). "Made for this" means, in part, "made to suffer from this".

**4. The same reasoning was once used against humans.** Aristotle, who said animals exist for man, also argued that some humans were slaves by nature. The idea that a being "exists for" serving another has a history we now reject.

**5. Biology has no purposes.** Since Darwin, biology explains organisms' traits through evolution, not purposes. The idea that a species exists "for" something predates modern science.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: domestication was a partnership

The most serious version says domestication was a mutually beneficial relationship: the animals gained protection, food and reproductive success, and humans gained food. In that sense, domesticated species "accepted" this role over the course of evolution.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Species don't make agreements.** Each individual is the one who suffers and dies, and none of them agreed to anything (A18).
2. **Today's "benefit" is doubtful.** In intensive farming, the animals' lives aren't a benefit to them (B2).
3. **Reproductive success isn't welfare.** A numerous species can be made up of individuals who suffer.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in C9 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Begging the question | Defines the animal by the use one wants to justify |
| Logical | Equivocation | Confuses assigned function with one's own good |
| Epistemological | Pre-scientific teleology | Attributes purposes to species |
| Moral | Sentient beings treated as objects | Applies the logic of tools to someone who feels |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Doesn't accept that anyone is "born to serve" among humans.**
2. **Treats their own dog as someone, not as a function.** Dogs were also shaped by artificial selection.
3. **Recognizes that chickens suffer from fast growth.** Which contradicts the idea that it's their "nature".

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "a being exists for the purpose we assign it".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enslaved people | "Born to serve" would be a justification | That was exactly the argument used |
| Dogs | Guard or fighting breeds would "exist for that" | Dogfighting is a crime |
| Children from poor families | "Born to work" | No one accepts this |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that farmed animals have been deeply transformed by human selection, and that domestication is one of the oldest relationships in human history. Understanding that history matters, and there are serious debates about what we owe the species we have shaped.

But having been shaped by us isn't having been born for us. If we shaped these animals, we bear even more responsibility for what they feel, not less.
