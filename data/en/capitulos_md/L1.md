# L1. "Vegans are annoying and think they are better"

**Thesis:** it's an attack on the person, not the argument. Even if some vegans are annoying, that doesn't change the validity of what they say about animals. Psychology even has a name for the reaction: do-gooder derogation. In 2012, researchers showed that meat eaters tend to rate vegetarians worse precisely when they imagine being judged by them. The irritation often comes from discomfort, not from the vegan's behavior.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "How do you know someone's vegan? They'll tell you. You think you're better than everyone."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some vegans are pushy, moralistic or arrogant.
2. **P2.** That behavior is unpleasant and harms dialogue.
3. **P3.** A movement that behaves like that doesn't deserve to be taken seriously.
4. **C.** So veganism can be ignored.

P1 and P2 may be true in some cases. The problem lies in P3: the behavior of some people says nothing about whether the idea they defend is true.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which is an ad hominem.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes someone speaks in an annoying way, even when they're right. Imagine a classmate tells you, in an irritating way, that you're standing on someone's foot. The way they said it might be annoying, but it's still good to move your foot. What matters is whether the person is right, not whether they're nice.

## 4. Short answer

Some vegans may be annoying, like people in any group. But that changes nothing about the animals. Psychology shows that part of the irritation comes from feeling judged: in a 2012 study, meat eaters rated vegetarians worse precisely after imagining that vegetarians judged them. Often, a vegan only has to say they don't eat meat to be seen as "superior". The question remains: do the animals deserve to be spared?

**Question to ask back:** "If the nicest vegan in the world made the same argument, would they be wrong?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument swaps assessing the idea for assessing the people:

| What the argument assesses | What it should assess |
| --- | --- |
| The tone of some vegans | Whether animals feel and whether it's right to kill them |
| The supposed superiority | The reasons given |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's ad hominem.** The behavior of those defending an idea doesn't make it true or false.

**2. The irritation often comes from discomfort.** Minson and Monin showed in 2012 that meat eaters tend to disparage vegetarians, especially when they imagine being morally judged by them. The authors called this do-gooder derogation.

**3. Merely mentioning it already bothers people.** Often, a vegan is seen as annoying just for turning down a dish or answering a question.

**4. Stereotypes generalize.** Vegans are diverse, and many avoid talking about the subject precisely so as not to be seen this way.

**5. We wouldn't accept the argument for other causes.** That some supporters of a cause are inconvenient is no reason to dismiss it; the question about the cause remains.

### (c) Data and sources

| Source | What it says | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Minson and Monin, 2012, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/12/Minson---Monin,-2011.pdf) | Meat eaters disparage vegetarians, especially when they anticipate being morally judged by them | A preprint of the article |

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: moralism hurts the cause

The most serious version says some vegans' moralistic behavior harms their own cause, and that they should change how they communicate.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a legitimate criticism of form.** And animal advocates should take it seriously.
2. **It doesn't change the content.** The message is still the same.
3. **It's no excuse for the listener.** The question about the animals is for each person.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument is about vegans, not animals. But the animals didn't choose their advocates. The moral question doesn't depend on anyone's charm: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Listens to annoying people on other subjects.** When they agree with them.
2. **Talks about food all the time.** But finds it annoying when a vegan does.
3. **Also thinks they're right in their convictions.** Like anyone.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L1 | Evidence |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if whoever defends an idea is unpleasant, the idea can be ignored".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anti-smoking activists | The warning about cigarettes could be ignored | No sensible person concludes this |
| Persistent abolitionists | Slavery wouldn't need questioning | Absurd |
| Vegans | The question of animals could be ignored | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There are vegans who communicate badly, judge others and push people away, and the movement should listen to that criticism. No one likes to feel judged, and dialogue works better with respect.

But the quality of an idea doesn't depend on the charm of whoever defends it. The question about the animals is still waiting for an answer, whoever asks it.
