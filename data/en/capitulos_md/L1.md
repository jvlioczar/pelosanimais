# L1. "Vegans are annoying and think they are better"

**Thesis:** it's an attack on the person, not the argument. Even if some vegans are annoying, that doesn't change the validity of what they say about animals. Psychology even has a name for the reaction: do-gooder derogation. In 2012, researchers showed that meat eaters tend to rate vegetarians worse precisely when they imagine being judged by them. The irritation often comes from discomfort, not from the vegan's behavior. And there is one point where the difference is real: those who stop buying animal products cause less harm to animals in that respect. That isn't a matter of opinion or of personal worth, and it doesn't make anyone a better person overall; it's a matter of consequences for animals, and it mostly bothers those unwilling to change.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "How do you know someone's vegan? They'll tell you. You think you're better than everyone."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some vegans are pushy, moralistic or arrogant.
2. **P2.** That behavior is unpleasant and harms dialogue.
3. **P3.** A movement that behaves like that doesn't deserve to be taken seriously.
4. **C.** So veganism can be ignored.

P1 and P2 may be true in some cases. The problem lies in P3: the behavior of some people says nothing about whether the idea they defend is true.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, which is an ad hominem.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes someone speaks in an annoying way, even when they're right. Imagine a classmate tells you, in an irritating way, that you're standing on someone's foot. The way they said it might be annoying, but it's still good to move your foot. What matters is whether the person is right, not whether they're nice.

## 4. Short answer

Maybe some vegans really are annoying, like people in any group. But what does that change for the animals? Notice, too, where part of the irritation comes from: in a 2012 study, meat eaters rated vegetarians worse precisely after imagining that vegetarians judged them. Often, a vegan only has to say they don't eat meat to be seen as "superior". And the reverse happens all the time: vegans hear jokes, get attacked and find barbecue photos in their comments just for saying they're vegan, and nobody treats that as proof that eating meat is wrong. On one specific point, there is a difference: those who don't buy animal products cause less harm to animals in that respect. That isn't opinion, and it doesn't make anyone a better person overall either. So, leaving the annoying vegan aside: do the animals deserve to be spared?

**Question to ask back:** "If the nicest vegan in the world made the same argument, would they be wrong?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument swaps assessing the idea for assessing the people:

| What the argument assesses | What it should assess |
| --- | --- |
| The tone of some vegans | Whether animals feel and whether it's right to kill them |
| The supposed superiority | The reasons given |
| Who "thinks they're better" | Which choices cause less harm to animals |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It's ad hominem.** The behavior of those defending an idea doesn't make it true or false.

**2. The irritation often comes from discomfort.** Minson and Monin showed in 2012 that meat eaters tend to disparage vegetarians, especially when they imagine being morally judged by them. The authors called this do-gooder derogation.

**3. Merely mentioning it already bothers people.** Often, a vegan is seen as annoying just for turning down a dish or answering a question.

**4. Stereotypes generalize.** Vegans are diverse, and many avoid talking about the subject precisely so as not to be seen this way.

**5. In one respect, the difference is real, and it isn't a matter of opinion.** Those who stop buying meat, milk, eggs and other animal products stop paying for part of the breeding and killing of animals. When it comes to who causes less suffering to animals, vegans are ahead, and that is a matter of consequences, not of personal worth. Causing less harm in this respect doesn't make anyone a better person overall: a vegan can be dishonest, rude or unfair in other areas of life. But the comparison on this specific point mostly bothers those unwilling to change, and the usual reaction is to attack whoever points out the difference.

**6. We wouldn't accept the argument for other causes.** That some supporters of a cause are inconvenient is no reason to dismiss it; the question about the cause remains.

**7. Judging people settles nothing about actions.** The philosopher Nathan Nobis points out that criticism of activists, fair or not, never shows that a use of animals is permissible: if someone said "some anti-abortion activists threaten doctors, so abortion is acceptable", the conclusion wouldn't follow, and the same holds here (*Animals & Ethics 101*, 2016, p. 75). He adds that there are rude people and kind people on both sides, and that smear campaigns tend to rest on false generalizations. Some are organized: in the United States, the Center for Consumer Freedom, founded by the lobbyist Richard Berman and funded largely by restaurant, food and beverage companies, ran the ActivistCash website, with profiles of groups and activists; Nobis lists it among the readings for his chapter on activism (p. 74). The organization is now called the Center for Organizational Research and Education.

**8. The reverse happens too, and often.** There is rudeness on both sides, but hostility toward vegans is common and documented. People who say they are vegan often hear jokes, taunts and attacks on veganism, and posts and comments on the subject frequently draw photos of meat and barbecues posted just to provoke (L34, L36). The studies measure attitudes rather than how often each episode happens, but they point the same way. In 2017, MacInnis and Hodson found that omnivores rated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups that are common targets of prejudice; vegans were rated worse than vegetarians, and vegetarians and, above all, vegans reported experiencing negative reactions because of their diet (L37). In the British press in 2007, Cole and Morgan classified 74% of 397 pieces mentioning vegans or veganism as negative and only 5.5% as positive; the authors called the pattern "vegaphobia" (L8). In a survey of 996 people in Belgium, Vandermoere and colleagues (2019) found negative attitudes toward vegetarians mainly among the most convinced meat eaters, and less among people with a vegetarian at home or among their friends. And an analysis of five years of posts on an anti-vegan Reddit forum (Gregson, Piazza and Boyd, 2022) showed a community organized around rejecting vegans, seen as militant and inflexible, and reacting above all to moral messages.

**9. The stereotype works, in part, as self-defense.** Some of the hostility may be a reaction to vegans who really were rude. But the best-studied explanation is different: the feeling of being morally judged (point 2) and the discomfort of people who love animals and eat meat, the so-called meat paradox. Bastian and Loughnan (2017) describe how this discomfort tends to be relieved by changing beliefs rather than the habit, for example by downplaying animals' minds or treating consumption as normal and necessary. Discrediting whoever brings up the problem serves the same end, and that is what do-gooder derogation describes. On this reading, which is an interpretation supported by these studies and not a measure of each case, the "annoying vegan" is often less a portrait of vegans than a way of not hearing what they say. The same asymmetry shows up elsewhere: hardly anyone asks people devoted to a human cause to justify why they chose that one and not another, but animal advocates are asked this all the time (J20).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: moralism hurts the cause

The most serious version says some vegans' moralistic behavior harms their own cause, and that they should change how they communicate.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a legitimate criticism of form.** And animal advocates should take it seriously.
2. **It doesn't change the content.** The message is still the same.
3. **It's no excuse for the listener.** The question about the animals is for each person.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument is about vegans, not animals. But the animals didn't choose their advocates. The moral question doesn't depend on anyone's charm: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Listens to annoying people on other subjects.** When they agree with them.
2. **Talks about food all the time.** But finds it annoying when a vegan does.
3. **Also thinks they're right in their convictions.** Like anyone.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if whoever defends an idea is unpleasant, the idea can be ignored".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anti-smoking activists | The warning about cigarettes could be ignored | No sensible person concludes this |
| Persistent abolitionists | Slavery wouldn't need questioning | Absurd |
| Vegans | The question of animals could be ignored | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

There are vegans who communicate badly, judge others and push people away, and the movement should listen to that criticism. No one likes to feel judged, and dialogue works better with respect.

But the quality of an idea doesn't depend on the charm of whoever defends it. The question about the animals is still waiting for an answer, whoever asks it.
