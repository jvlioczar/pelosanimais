# K11. "I'll wait for cultivated meat"

**Thesis:** the argument postpones the obligation until it's painless. Cultivated meat may be closer than people imagine: in 2026, a US company announced capacity for more than 1 million pounds a year and began selling cultivated chicken in restaurants. But it still faces challenges of cost and scale, and strong resistance from part of the public and the livestock industry, which has already had it banned in Italy, Hungary and several US states. Those who are betting on it can help by taking a favorable stance. And until it arrives, billions of animals keep being killed: waiting for a future solution doesn't justify causing harm in the present, when plant-based alternatives already exist today.

**Family:** K. Effectiveness and individual responsibility · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "In ten years technology will fix it. When it's cheap, I'll switch."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Cultivated meat can offer meat without raising and killing animals.
2. **P2.** When it's available and cheap, many people will switch effortlessly.
3. **P3.** It's more effective to wait for a technological solution than to demand individual sacrifices.
4. **C.** So I can keep eating meat until then.

P1 is true in principle. P2 is plausible. The problem lies in the conclusion: the date is uncertain, its arrival also depends on political decisions, and the harm continues while we wait.

The argument is **valid** if waiting had no cost. Its **soundness** fails because the cost is the lives of the animals killed in the meantime.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people are making meat from cells, without needing to kill animals, and it has started being sold in a few restaurants. That could be really good. But there's still very little of it, some places have even banned it, and meanwhile many animals keep suffering. People who like the idea can stand up for it. And today there are already lots of tasty foods that don't need animals.

## 4. Short answer

Cultivated meat may be closer than people imagine: a US company already produces on a scale of hundreds of tonnes a year and sells it in restaurants. But that's still a tiny fraction of the meat produced, and it faces strong resistance: Italy, Hungary and several US states have already banned it. If you're betting on it, you can help by standing up for it. But until it arrives, billions of animals keep being killed. So I have to ask: does waiting for a future solution justify causing harm now, when you already have alternatives today: beans, lentils, tofu and many other foods?

**Question to ask back:** "If cultivated meat takes 20 years, how many animals will have died while you waited?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument postpones change to an uncertain future:

| Moment | Situation |
| --- | --- |
| Today | Cheap plant alternatives are available; cultivated meat is starting to be sold on a small scale |
| Uncertain future | Cultivated meat may become affordable, if it overcomes the cost challenges and the bans |
| Meanwhile | Animals keep being raised and killed |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. It may be closer than people imagine, but it's still small.** Cultivated meat was approved for sale in Singapore in 2020 and in the United States in 2023. In November 2026, Upside Foods launched a chicken product that combines cultivated meat with plant ingredients, served in California restaurants and in dining halls such as Stanford University's, and announced production capacity of more than 1 million pounds (about 450 tonnes) a year. That's real progress, but for comparison, the United States produced about 9.4 billion broiler chickens in 2025, equivalent to more than 62 billion pounds live weight. The announced output is still far less than 0.01% of that.

**2. There are challenges of cost and scale.** A 2021 techno-economic analysis by David Humbird concluded that production costs, under the conditions studied, would hardly allow an affordable food. Other analyses, from the industry itself, are more optimistic, and no one knows when, or whether, it will be as cheap as conventional meat.

**3. There's strong resistance from the public and the livestock industry.** Italy banned the production and sale of cultivated meat in 2023, and Hungary's parliament voted for a ban in 2025. In the United States, Florida and Alabama banned it in 2024, and Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, Indiana and Texas followed in 2025, some temporarily. Many of these laws were defended as protecting livestock farming and local traditions. The arrival of cultivated meat depends not only on technology but also on political decisions. Jacy Reese Anthis, of the Sentience Institute, drew lessons for cultured meat from the history of GMOs, in a paper published in the journal *Appetite* (2019): genetic engineering firms saw themselves as humanitarian and environmentally friendly and were unprepared for the public backlash, and highlighting a technology's positive aspects did more for its adoption than responding to negative perceptions.

**4. Those betting on it can help.** If cultivated meat is the solution someone is waiting for, it makes sense to take a favorable stance: support its regulation, oppose bans and try it when it becomes available. Waiting in silence while others ban it is contradictory.

**5. The harm continues while we wait.** Each year of waiting means billions of animals killed.

**6. Alternatives already exist.** No waiting is needed to stop eating animals.

**7. It postpones the obligation until it's painless.** If causing the harm is wrong, future convenience doesn't make it acceptable in the present.

**8. "It isn't natural" isn't a moral objection.** Being natural doesn't tell us whether something is good (C1). What matters is whether cultivated meat harms animals. The real problems, such as the use of fetal bovine serum in early processes, are technical and are being replaced by animal-free growth media. Whether cultivated meat is vegan is an open debate; see [The definition of veganism, debated](../alem/definicao-de-veganismo.html).

**9. Talking only about technology may even backfire.** In a Sentience Institute experiment (Harris, 2020), with 1,502 participants in the United States, those who read about cultured meat or high-tech substitutes came to oppose animal farming less than those who read about simple plant-based foods or an unrelated topic; simple plant-based foods had no such effect. One possible explanation is the one in this very phrase: if technology will solve it, changing now seems less urgent. The study measured attitudes, not behavior, and was run once, online; but it points in the same direction as an earlier study by Macdonald and Vivalt (2017).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: technology is what changes the world

The most serious version says mass change rarely comes from individual moral choices, but from technologies that make the alternative cheaper and more convenient. And the news that production is already scaling up would strengthen that bet.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **That may be true at the level of society.** But it doesn't excuse the individual who can already change.
2. **Demand and political support speed up the technology.** Those who change today help create tomorrow's market, and those who defend cultivated meat against bans help make sure it arrives.
3. **The duty doesn't wait.** The animal who dies today doesn't benefit from future technology.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The argument admits that killing animals is a problem, since it waits for a solution. If it's a problem, the question is why keep causing it while the alternatives already exist.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in K11 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Indefinite postponement | The duty is moved to an uncertain future |
| Epistemological | Technological optimism | Assumes the solution will come soon and cheap, without counting political resistance |
| Moral | Convenience as a condition | Would only change when it's painless |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Admits killing animals is a problem.** By waiting for a solution.
2. **Already has alternatives.** And doesn't use them.
3. **Wouldn't wait for a technology to stop other harms.** If they were serious for them.
4. **Waits for cultivated meat.** But doesn't defend it when it's banned.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains K11 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present bias | The cost of changing today weighs more than the future benefit | General literature on temporal discounting |
| Optimism bias | The technological solution seems near and certain | General literature on optimism bias |
| Pro-innovation bias | Cultivated meat is treated as a guaranteed solution, without counting its limits of cost, scale and timing | [Senciência, 2024 (in Portuguese)](https://senciencia.org/2024/04/22/vieses-que-contribuem-para-a-negligencia-do-longo-prazo-e-dos-riscos-s/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "I can keep causing a harm until a technology makes the alternative painless".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pollution | I can pollute until technology fixes it | No one accepts this |
| Slave labor | I can buy its products until automation fixes it | No one accepts this |
| Meat | I can eat it until cultivated meat arrives | The same error |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Cultivated meat and other technologies could profoundly transform food production, and it's reasonable to bet on them. Production is starting to grow, and for many people change will be easier when there are identical, cheap alternatives. For that very reason, it's worth defending them against those who want to ban them.

But future technology doesn't change what happens today. Until it arrives, plant alternatives already exist, and every animal spared now matters.
