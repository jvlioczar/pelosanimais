# J14. "Billions of people depend on fish for protein"

**Thesis:** the number exists, but it is usually quoted wrongly. What the FAO says is that aquatic foods provide about 3.2 billion people with at least 20% of the animal protein they consume, not 20% of their protein needs. Worldwide, they are 6% of all protein. There are communities that really do depend on fishing, and veganism asks the impossible of no one. But most fish is eaten by people who have alternatives, and depending on something doesn't make it just for those who can choose. A quick death is better than suffocation, and it is still the death of someone who feels.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "A message for oversensitive vegans: 3.2 billion people get at least 20% of their protein needs from fish. Making sure the fish have a quick death is the least we can do." (The message circulated in the promotion of a fish slaughter machine.)

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Billions of people get an important part of their protein from fish.
2. **P2.** A practice that so many people depend on can't simply end.
3. **P3.** If it is going to continue, what we owe fish is a quick death, with stunning.
4. **P4.** Asking for more than that is the sentimentality of people who don't know the real world.
5. **C.** So catching and farming fish is justified, as long as slaughter is quick.

P1 is true when the number is read correctly. P3 describes a real improvement. The problem lies in P2, which moves from what some people need to what everyone may do, and in P4, which attacks the speaker instead of answering what is said.

The argument is **invalid**: from the dependence of some it doesn't follow that the practice is justified for all.

## 2. How it can show up

| Context | Typical form |
| --- | --- |

## 3. Answer for children

In some places, like small islands and very poor fishing villages, there is hardly any other food. No one is picking a fight with those people. But most fish is eaten by people who live near a shop full of beans, rice and vegetables. And fish feel pain and fear. Killing quickly is better than killing slowly, but what the fish really wanted was to keep swimming.

## 4. Short answer

The FAO figure is a different one: fish gives about 3 billion people at least 20% of the animal protein they eat, not 20% of the protein they need. Worldwide, it's 6% of all protein. People who truly depend on fishing to live aren't the target of this conversation. You and I have a choice. And stunning before killing is better than suffocating, but "a quick death" doesn't answer why kill.

**Question to ask back:** "Are you and I among the people who have no other source of protein?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The message carries two arguments and one number. First, the number:

| Edition of the FAO's SOFIA report | What the report says | What the message says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2020 (2017 data) | Fish provided about 3.3 billion people with "almost 20 percent of their average per capita intake of animal protein" | "20% of their protein needs" |
| 2024 (2021 data) | Aquatic foods contributed "at least 20 percent of the per capita protein supply from all animal sources" to 3.2 billion people | "20% of their protein needs" |
| 2026 | At least one-fifth of the animal protein consumption of 3.1 billion people | "20% of their protein needs" |

In every edition, the 20% is a share of animal protein, not of protein needs. The 2024 edition itself states that aquatic foods account for 15% of animal protein and 6% of all protein consumed in the world. People also get protein from grains, beans and other plants, and that part is left out of the sum.

Then, the two arguments: "many people depend on it" and "you're oversensitive".

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The number doesn't say what the message says.** Twenty percent of animal protein is far less than twenty percent of the protein needed. And the figure describes what people eat today, not what they need to eat. Protein doesn't have to come from animals (G2).

**2. Those who truly depend on it are a part.** There are coastal and riverside communities and poor islands where fish is half or more of the animal protein available. The FAO mentions countries such as Bangladesh, Cambodia, Ghana and Sierra Leone. For those people, what the Vegan Society's definition already says applies: veganism seeks to exclude the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable" (D7). No one serious asks them to go hungry.

**3. Most fish doesn't go to those who need it most.** According to the 2026 edition of the FAO report, the availability of aquatic foods is 26.3 kg per person a year in Asia and only 9.1 kg in Africa. The world average was 20.7 kg in 2022. The argument uses the dependence of the poorest to defend the consumption of people who have a whole supermarket at hand.

**4. A large part of the catch doesn't even become food for people.** By weight, the FAO reports that 89% of aquatic animal production goes to direct human consumption, and the rest goes mainly to fishmeal and fish oil. By number of individuals the proportion is much larger, because the fish that are ground up are small: the researchers Alison Mood and Phil Brooke estimate that about half of the wild fish caught are turned into fishmeal and oil, and that 70% to 73% of that fishmeal and oil feeds aquaculture (H14).

**5. Depending isn't the same as justifying.** That many people depend on a practice explains why changing it takes time and alternatives (J13). It doesn't show that the practice is just for those who have a choice. Whole economies have depended on practices that no one defends today.

**6. "The least" is little.** Stunning before killing is better than leaving fish to suffocate, and today most wild fish die without stunning, by gutting or by asphyxiation that can last for hours. But the message admits, without noticing, that fish feel: no one builds a stunning machine for beings who feel nothing (A2). And a quick slaughter answers "how to kill", not "why kill" (B1). Scale matters too: Mood and Brooke estimate that between 1.1 and 2.2 trillion wild fish were caught each year, on average, from 2000 to 2019.

**7. "Oversensitive" is an attack on the person.** Calling someone sensitive doesn't show that they are wrong. Fish's capacity to feel pain is supported by scientific evidence, not by sentimentality (A7).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: fish is irreplaceable for the poorest

The most serious version sets the 20% aside and says: for hundreds of millions of poor people, fish is the most accessible source of good-quality protein, iron, zinc and omega-3. Taking that from them in the name of fish would be swapping one suffering for another. And as long as fishing exists, improving slaughter is concrete help.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It holds for those who are in that situation.** Veganism already recognizes that limit. The counter-reply doesn't reach the consumer in a town with a food shop, who is the one receiving the advertisement for the slaughter machine.
2. **Much of the catch doesn't feed the poor.** About half of the wild fish caught, by number, become fishmeal and oil, mostly for aquaculture. Reducing that consumption takes no food from those who depend on fishing.
3. **Improving slaughter and questioning slaughter aren't mutually exclusive.** Those who defend fish can support stunning today and go on saying that the best thing is not to kill them. Only the second part deals with the fish's interest in living.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The message talks about protein, billions of people and machines. The fish appears only at the end, as someone who deserves to die quickly. But if the fish deserves that, it's because what happens to them matters. And if it matters, the next question is why kill them when there is other food. For those who have no other food, the answer is one thing. For those who read the message phone in hand, with a shop on the corner, it's another.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in J14 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epistemological | Misquoted data | Swaps "animal protein" for "protein needs" |
| Logical | Hasty generalization from an exception | Uses those with no alternative to justify those who have one |
| Logical | Ad hominem and poisoning the well | "Oversensitive" discredits the speaker |
| Moral | Changing the question | Swaps "is it just to kill?" for "how do we kill quickly?" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Builds a machine so the fish won't suffer.** And calls oversensitive anyone who says fish suffer.
2. **Speaks in the name of the poor.** But sells to those who have alternatives.
3. **Says a quick death is the least.** And treats the least as if it were everything.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if many people depend on a practice, it is justified for everyone, and it's enough to make it less painful".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coal | It should be kept forever, because whole regions depend on it | A transition is planned instead |
| Whaling | It would be justified worldwide, because some communities have depended on it | The exception doesn't become the rule |
| Child labor | It would be enough to make it less harsh, since poor families depend on it | A comparison of the argument's structure: the accepted answer is to provide alternatives |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that aquatic foods weigh heavily in the diet of billions of people, that there are communities for whom no other option exists today, and that any serious proposal has to start with them. It's also right that stunning fish before killing them is a concrete improvement in a sector where almost none are stunned, and those who develop that technology reduce real suffering.

But the concession has a clear limit. The dependence of some doesn't justify the consumption of all, and the number used to claim otherwise is misquoted. And "the least we can do" is the least: for those who have a choice, there is more to do, which is to leave the fish alive.
