# B12. "Foie gras is a traditional delicacy, and ducks naturally eat a lot"

**Thesis:** foie gras is the diseased liver of ducks and geese force-fed through a tube pushed down the throat, two or three times a day, in the last weeks of their lives, until the organ grows several times its size. It's true that these birds store fat before migrating, but none of them eats like this of its own accord. Forcing food into someone who doesn't need it and making an animal ill on purpose, for a luxury dish, is a serious harm, and several countries have banned the practice.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Foie gras is a French culinary tradition. And ducks naturally eat a lot; they fatten up on their own before migrating."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Wild ducks and geese store fat in their livers before migrating.
2. **P2.** Foie gras production only enhances this natural capacity.
3. **P3.** Foie gras is an old gastronomic tradition.
4. **C.** So producing and eating foie gras is acceptable.

P1 is true. P2 fails: production doesn't let the bird eat more, it forces food through a tube in amounts the bird wouldn't eat. P3 is true, but tradition doesn't justify harm (D1).

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2.

## 3. Answer for children

To make foie gras, people put a tube down a duck's throat and push in lots of food, several times a day, until its liver becomes huge and sick. The duck doesn't choose to eat that much. It would be like someone stuffing food into you when you're already full.

## 4. Short answer

Foie gras is the diseased liver of force-fed ducks and geese: a tube is pushed down the throat, two or three times a day, in the last weeks of life, until the liver grows several times its size. These birds store fat before migrating, but none eats like this of its own accord. Pushing food into someone who doesn't need it, until the liver is made ill on purpose, is a serious harm, not a detail of the recipe. Several countries have banned it.

**Question to ask back:** "If the duck fattened up on its own, why would a tube down its throat be needed?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What happens in production |
| --- | --- |
| "The duck fattens up on its own" | Food is pushed through a tube, in amounts far above the natural |
| "It just enhances a natural capacity" | The liver grows several times its size and becomes diseased (steatosis) |
| "It's tradition" | The current industrial technique, with individual cages, is recent |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The bird doesn't eat; it's force-fed.** In production, ducks and geese are fed through a tube two or three times a day, for about two weeks in the case of ducks and a little longer for geese.

**2. Making someone ill on purpose is serious.** Force-feeding doesn't answer hunger: it pushes food into an animal that doesn't need it. According to the 1998 European scientific report, the total amount ingested is much greater than the birds would eat of their own accord, and when force-feeding stops, they greatly reduce their food intake for several days. The goal is to produce hepatic steatosis, a build-up of fat in the liver, which grows to 6 to 10 times its normal size. The report notes that no normal animal has steatosis to that degree, that liver function is impaired, and that most of the pathologists consulted considered the condition pathological; some disagree, pointing out that the steatosis is often reversible. But production itself stops in time because, if it continued, mortality would rise sharply, largely from liver failure. Even so, mortality during the force-feeding period is 2% to 4%, against around 0.2% in ducks that aren't force-fed. If someone made a person ill on purpose, it would be seen as serious violence; here, making the animal ill is the production method itself.

**3. European science assessed the problem.** The EU Scientific Committee on Animal Health and Animal Welfare (SCAHAW) concluded in 1998 that force-feeding, as practiced, harms the birds' welfare. The report noted that the birds avoided the person who force-fed them, unlike what happens with ordinary hand-feeding, and that 80% of ducks were kept in individual cages.

**4. Several countries have banned it.** Israel, once a major producer, banned force-feeding in 2005, and the practice is banned in several European countries, though many still import the product.

**5. Tradition doesn't justify harm.** Many old practices were abandoned once the suffering they caused was recognized (D1).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: some producers use neither cages nor tubes

The most serious version points out that some producers make fatty liver by letting birds eat freely before migration, without force-feeding.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **It's a tiny exception.** Almost all foie gras sold comes from force-feeding.
2. **The product is different.** Liver obtained this way is smaller and less fatty, and isn't usually sold as traditional foie gras.
3. **The killing remains.** Even without force-feeding, the bird is killed for a liver no one needs to eat.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Appeal to nature | A natural capacity is used to justify a forced use |
| Epistemological | Not knowing how it's produced | Assumes the bird eats on its own |
| Moral | Gastronomic pleasure above suffering | An occasional delicacy justifies weeks of force-feeding |
| Moral | Deliberate harm treated as technique | Making the liver ill on purpose is described as "fattening" |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says it's natural.** And accepts that a tube is needed to obtain it.
2. **Values tradition.** And accepts the recent industrial technique.
3. **Wouldn't accept the same with a dog.** Force-feeding a dog until its liver became diseased would be cruelty.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B12 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appeal to tradition | The age of the recipe hides the current method | General literature on argumentation |
| Prestige | Its status as a delicacy makes the product hard to question | General literature on luxury consumption |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a bird has a natural capacity, forcing it to the extreme is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forcing a cow to produce milk beyond her limit | It would be acceptable | No one accepts obvious cruelty |
| Force-feeding any animal | It would be acceptable if it naturally fattens | Consent and suffering still matter |
| Bans in several countries | They would be unjustified | They were based on welfare evidence |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that foie gras has a long history and that wild ducks and geese store fat before migrating. It's also true that there are attempts to produce it without force-feeding.

But what's sold as foie gras comes almost entirely from force-feeding, which European science itself assessed as harmful to the birds. Tradition explains the recipe; it doesn't justify the method.
