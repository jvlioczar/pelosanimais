# J9. "I swapped red meat for chicken, which is healthier"

**Thesis:** the swap may make sense for the eater's health, and some health guidelines suggest replacing red meat with poultry or fish. But for animals it usually makes things worse. For the same amount of meat, swapping beef for chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and swapping pork for chicken about 55 times more; swapping for fish tends to raise the count even further (J11). The count measures individuals, not suffering, but the difference per animal would have to be huge to offset that factor. And even if each cow or pig suffered more than each chicken, that wouldn't justify eating only the large animals. The swap that is good for health and neutral for animals is a different one: replacing meat with legumes.

**Family:** J. Practicality, economy and society · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "I've stopped eating red meat. Now it's just chicken and turkey breast, which is lighter and healthier. I'm already doing my part."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There is evidence that eating a lot of red and processed meat raises some health risks (see G17).
2. **P2.** Some health guidelines suggest replacing red meat with poultry or fish.
3. **P3.** Eating less red meat also cuts beef consumption, which has a large environmental impact.
4. **C.** So swapping red meat for chicken is a good, responsible choice.

P1 has support, and P2 describes recommendations that exist. P3 is true for emissions. The problem lies in the conclusion: it looks at the eater's health and at the climate, and leaves out the number of animals killed, which rises sharply with the swap.

The argument is **valid** as a health argument. It fails as a moral justification, because the choice that looks lighter on the plate can be the heaviest for animals.

## 3. Answer for children

If someone stops eating beef and eats only chicken, many more animals die for the same amount of food: more than two hundred chickens in place of each cow. Each chicken is a little animal who feels. Swapping meat for beans, lentils or chickpeas doesn't need any animal at all.

## 4. Short answer

For your health, the swap may make sense. But for animals it usually makes things worse. For the same amount of meat, swapping beef for chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and swapping pork for chicken about 55 times more. And broiler chickens live only about six weeks, often with serious welfare problems. If you want to eat more healthily, you can swap meat for beans, lentils or chickpeas. Looking after your health doesn't have to cost the lives of even more animals.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you know how many chickens die to put on the plate the same amount of meat that comes from a single cow?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

| What the argument says | What's true | What's left out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red meat is worse for your health | There is evidence of risk with high intake of red and processed meat | The eater's health isn't the only thing at stake |
| Chicken is the recommended swap | Some guidelines suggest poultry or fish instead of red meat | They don't take the number of animals into account |
| So the swap is responsible | It may cut emissions | It multiplies the number of animals killed |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The count starts from how much meat comes from each animal killed.** According to Faunalytics data compiled by Our World in Data, and used by the philosopher Daniel Dorado in a paper published in *Food Ethics* in 2026, the average is about 364.4 kg of meat per cow, 96.1 kg per pig and 1.74 kg per chicken.

**2. Kilo for kilo, the swap multiplies the animals.** With these figures, replacing beef with chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and replacing pork with chicken about 55 times more (rounding the chicken to 1.7 kg, as some presentations do, gives about 214 and 57). The ratios assume a complete swap, but even partial swaps add up quickly. Someone who eats 1 kg of beef consumes a small fraction of a cow; 1 kg of chicken is more than half a bird.

**3. Fish would make the count even larger.** Dorado leaves fish out because sizes vary too much between species. Since far less meat comes from most fish eaten than from a cow or a pig, swapping red meat for fish tends to raise the number of animals even further (J11). Feed can also contain fishmeal, but poultry farming, once the largest consumer of fishmeal, now uses little of it, according to IFFO, the industry's international association; this effect weighs far more in fish and shrimp farming than in chicken farming.

**4. The count measures individuals, not suffering.** A chicken and a cow don't go through the same things, and the number of animals doesn't say how much each one suffers. But broiler chickens aren't better off: they are slaughtered at about six weeks old (see B2), and the Welfare Footprint Project, which estimates animals' pain in each production system, documents serious welfare problems in broiler chickens, many linked to fast growth. And even if each cow's or pig's suffering were greater than each chicken's, that wouldn't make eating the large animals acceptable: it would only show that both choices cause harm, and that swapping one for the other doesn't solve it.

**5. The link between health advice and more chicken hasn't been tested.** Dorado argues that messages like "cut down on red meat", without saying what to eat instead, may lead people to chicken, because "healthy" menus and products tend to feature chicken. But he himself says this causal pathway hasn't yet been studied directly. What is certain is the arithmetic: whoever makes the swap raises the number of animals.

**6. There is a swap that is good for health and neutral for animals.** Dorado suggests that instead of recommending poultry in place of red meat, health advice could use neutral wording, such as "replace meat with legumes". Beans, lentils, chickpeas and peas provide protein without choosing which animal dies.

**7. Per kilo, chicken, eggs and farmed fish cause more direct suffering.** The researcher Brian Tomasik estimated the direct suffering, that of the animal itself, caused per kilo of each product: farmed fish, eggs and chicken come well ahead of pork, beef and milk, and in the extreme case farmed catfish causes about 20,000 times more suffering per kilo than milk. The calculation leaves out male chicks, calves and breeding animals, so it underestimates eggs and milk; Tomasik even suggests dairy as a compromise, which this guide does not endorse, because milk requires separating calves and slaughtering cows (B5). But the direction is the same as with the number of animals: switching from beef to chicken increases suffering.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: a chicken doesn't suffer like a cow

The most serious version says counting heads is misleading. A chicken would have a simpler mental life than a cow or a pig, and so would suffer less. If each chicken's suffering weighs much less, the swap may not be worse.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **The difference would have to be enormous.** For the swap from beef to chicken not to be worse, each chicken's suffering would have to weigh about 210 times less than each cow's. There's no evidence of a difference that large, and chickens feel pain and fear (see A5).
2. **Living conditions weigh against the chicken.** Broiler chickens live for weeks, in sheds with thousands of birds, with health problems linked to accelerated growth. That doesn't suggest a better life than a cow's.
3. **Under uncertainty, prudence avoids multiplying victims.** As Dorado argues, anyone who can't reliably compare suffering across species has reason to avoid choices that sharply increase the number of victims.
4. **The choice doesn't have to be between beef and chicken.** Swapping for legumes addresses the health concern without this dilemma.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The swap shows a real concern for one's own health, and that is legitimate. But the moral question is a different one: who pays for the meal? When the answer is "dozens or hundreds more animals", the choice that seemed lighter becomes heavier. If the reason to change is health, there is a change that looks after health without raising the number of deaths.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wants to reduce the harm of meat.** And picks the swap that most increases the number of animals killed.
2. **Says they've already done their part for animals.** And for them, the change was for the worse.
3. **Accepts that food has consequences.** But only counts those that fall on the eater.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if a swap is better for my health, it's a good choice, regardless of the cost to others".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beef for chicken | It would be a good choice | It involves about 210 times more animals per kilo |
| Red meat for fish | It would also be a good choice | It tends to raise the number of animals even further |
| Meat for legumes | It would also be a good choice | Here health and the animals don't conflict |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's true that there is evidence of risk in high intake of red and processed meat, that some health guidelines suggest replacing it with poultry or fish, and that cutting beef reduces emissions. Whoever made the swap acted out of a real concern for health.

But for animals, the swap to chicken almost always multiplies the number of deaths. The swap that serves health without exacting that price is the one to legumes. Genuinely eating less meat, without swapping it for smaller animals, is better than doing nothing, and can be a step toward veganism; but for the animals who are still killed, the harm is still terrible.
