# B10. "Those videos are exceptions or staged"

**Thesis:** much of what appears in footage from farms and slaughterhouses isn't the exception; it's the standard allowed by the rules: gestation stalls, birds shackled while conscious, culling of chicks, castration without anesthesia. When there is abuse beyond the rules, it is exposed precisely by this footage. And part of the industry's response was not to prove the videos lie, but to try to ban them from being made.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "That video is from another country. They filmed the worst case. It's activist propaganda."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Animal advocacy organizations have an interest in showing the worst.
2. **P2.** Videos can be edited, taken out of context or filmed in atypical places.
3. **P3.** Most producers follow the rules and treat their animals well.
4. **C.** So the videos don't represent the reality of production.

P1 and P2 are true: images can mislead, and those who share them have a point of view. The problem lies in P3, which assumes following the rules means treating animals well, and in the conclusion, which dismisses all videos because of the worst ones.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails because much of what the videos show is legal, standard practice (B9), not a violation.

## 3. Answer for children

Sometimes someone shows a photo and says everyone is like that, and it isn't true. But many videos from farms show things that are allowed and happen every day, like mother pigs kept in tight spaces and boy chicks being killed. That isn't an exception: it's the normal way of producing. That's why it's important to look carefully, and not just close your eyes.

## 4. Short answer

Some of what appears in the videos is abuse, and some is exactly what the rules allow: gestation stalls, birds shackled while conscious before stunning, culling of male chicks, castration without anesthesia. That's not the exception; it's the legal standard. And when there is abuse, it was these videos that exposed it. In the United States, several states tried to ban such filming, and courts struck those laws down in whole or in part.

**Question to ask back:** "If the standard is good, why try to ban filming it?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats every video as if it showed a violation. But videos show two kinds of things:

| What the video shows | Is it an exception? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A practice allowed by the rules | No, it's the standard | Gestation stalls early in pregnancy, water baths for birds, culling of chicks (B5, B9) |
| Abuse beyond the rules | Yes, but recurring | Beatings, stunning failures that go uncorrected |

The first kind can't be dismissed as an exception. The second, when it appears again and again in different places, stops being one too.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The legal standard is what shocks.** Many scenes that look like abuse are practices allowed by the rules, as chapter B9 shows. Calling them exceptions is not knowing what the law permits.

**2. Investigations reveal what inspection doesn't see.** Official inspection doesn't follow every animal at every moment. Footage recorded by workers and investigators shows what happens out of sight, and much of it has led to closures and prosecutions.

**3. The industry's reaction is telling.** In the United States, several states passed so-called *ag-gag* laws, which criminalized recording in animal production facilities. Courts struck down laws of this kind, in whole or in part, in Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Utah, Wyoming and North Carolina for violating free speech. Anyone with a good standard to show doesn't need to ban filming it.

**4. The burden is on those who deny.** If the videos are staged, it would be enough to open the farms and slaughterhouses to show otherwise. Ag-gag laws show that, in several places, the opposite response was chosen.

**5. The doubt is usually selective.** Saying the videos are exceptions often comes with refusing to look at how ordinary production of what one eats works (E12).

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: selection bias is real

The most serious version admits the practices shown exist, but says the videos pick the worst moments, that a farm spends most of its time with nothing shocking happening, and that judging a whole sector by a few minutes of footage is unfair.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Standard practices don't depend on the moment.** A sow in a gestation stall spends all her time there, not just the minutes filmed.
2. **Scale makes the rare common.** With billions of animals a year, even a rare practice happens millions of times (B1).
3. **The fix for bias is more transparency.** If the selection of images distorts, the remedy would be to show everything, not to ban recording.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in B10 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Ad hominem | Discredits whoever filmed instead of discussing what was filmed |
| Logical | Reverse generalization | Uses the most extreme videos to dismiss those showing the standard too |
| Epistemological | Not knowing the rules | Assumes what shocks is illegal |
| Moral | Willful ignorance | Prefers to doubt rather than check |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Believes videos that expose other abuses.** The same person accepts hidden-camera footage revealing corruption or the mistreatment of people.
2. **Doesn't check the standard they defend.** Says the videos are exceptions without knowing what the rule is.
3. **Would they accept opening the farms?** If the standard is good, full transparency should be welcome.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains B10 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Willful ignorance | Avoiding information that would create an obligation | Linked to E12 |
| System justification | Official production seems right because it is official | Linked to B9 |
| Confirmation bias | Accepts only information confirming that all is well | General literature on confirmation bias |
| Meat paradox | Discrediting the image eases the tension between liking animals and eating them | [Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488214/) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "images of harm produced by those who expose it don't count".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposés of slave labor | They would be dismissed for coming from inspectors and journalists | No one accepts this |
| Filmed police violence | It would be dismissed as an "isolated case" on principle | It's the kind of argument people criticize in those cases |
| Dog abuse | Recordings would be suspect for coming from activists | They serve as evidence in court |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Skepticism toward images is healthy: videos can be edited, taken out of context or cherry-picked, and those who share them have an aim. It's also true that many producers try hard to treat their animals well and that it's unfair to accuse everyone of the worst.

But skepticism has to apply both ways. What many videos show isn't the illegal exception but the permitted rule. And the best way to prove the images mislead would be to show everything, not to hide it.
