# B10. "The law guarantees animal welfare"

**Thesis:** welfare rules exist, but they allow much of what consumers imagine is banned. In the European Union, sows may be kept in individual stalls for the first four weeks of pregnancy and piglets may be surgically castrated without anesthesia in their first week of life. In the United States, federal law sets almost no rules for how farm animals are housed, and many state cruelty laws exempt "customary" farming practices. Brazil's 2020 pig rule gives deadlines up to 2045. The law follows what the industry can comply with, not what the animals need. And the word "welfare" comes to name the minimum compatible with production: no one would call these same conditions welfare if they were imposed on a person. And tougher laws haven't stopped farming from growing: worldwide, meat supply per person has almost doubled since 1961.

**Family:** B. Harm, death and welfare · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "There are inspections. If it were cruel, it would be banned."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** There are specific animal welfare rules for production and slaughter.
2. **P2.** These rules are drawn up by experts and enforced by the state.
3. **P3.** What these rules allow has been judged acceptable.
4. **C.** So animals in legal production are treated acceptably.

P1 and P2 are true: there are rules, and some have brought real progress. The problem lies in P3, which confuses what is allowed with what is acceptable, and in the gap between what the rules allow and what consumers imagine.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P3, a version of the appeal to the law (D9).

## 3. Answer for children

There are rules for looking after animals on farms, and some of them help. But many rules still allow things that hurt, like keeping a mother pig in a space where she can barely move. A rule saying something is allowed doesn't mean it's good for the animal. It only means it hasn't been banned yet. If this were done to a person, no one would call it "welfare".

## 4. Short answer

The rules exist, but they allow much of what you may imagine is banned. In the EU, sows can be kept in stalls for the first four weeks of pregnancy and piglets can be castrated without anesthesia in their first week. In the US, federal law barely regulates how farm animals are kept, and many state cruelty laws exempt standard farming practices. Slaughter rules allow a gas that pigs avoid and birds shackled while conscious. All of this fits within "welfare standards", and no one would use that word if a person were in the animal's place. Being legal isn't the same as not being cruel.

**Question to ask back:** "Do you know what the law allows to be done to a pregnant sow?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument assumes the rules reflect what the animal needs. In practice, they reflect a balance between welfare and economic viability:

| Rule | What it improves | What it still allows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EU Directive 2008/120/EC, pigs | Group housing for sows during most of pregnancy; anesthesia for castration after the seventh day | Individual stalls in the first four weeks after insemination; farrowing crates; surgical castration without anesthesia up to seven days of age |
| United States, federal and state law | Some states ban gestation crates (for example, California and Massachusetts) | No federal standard for housing on farms; many state cruelty laws exempt customary farming practices |
| Brazil, Normative Instruction 113/2020, pigs | Group gestation, analgesia and anesthesia for surgical castration | Stalls in the first 35 days of gestation; anesthesia mandatory only from 2030; some deadlines up to 2045 |
| Slaughter rules (EU, US, Brazil) | Require stunning for most mammals | High-concentration CO2 for pigs; water baths with shackled birds; religious slaughter without stunning; in the US, no federal rule for poultry (B1) |

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. Allowed isn't acceptable.** Rules are agreements between what is desired and what the industry can comply with. Rules that ban something often do so with long transition periods precisely because the previous standard was much worse.

**2. The rules themselves recognize the problem.** If individual gestation stalls were acceptable, there would be no reason to require group housing for most of pregnancy. The rules admit the stalls were harmful and, even so, allow them for part of the pregnancy.

**3. Painful mutilations remain legal.** Castration, tail docking and beak trimming are routine, and in many places they are allowed without anesthesia when done to very young animals. Brazil's requirement of analgesia and anesthesia for surgical castration only becomes mandatory for everyone in 2030.

**4. Permitted slaughter also hurts.** Slaughter rules don't ban high-concentration carbon dioxide for pigs, which the European scientific authority describes as aversive and impossible to fix within the method (B1).

**5. The rules don't ask whether the animal should die.** All welfare legislation starts from the assumption that the animal will be used and killed. It regulates the how, not the whether.

**6. The word "welfare" changes meaning.** In everyday use, well-being means being well. In the rules and in industry language, the word comes to name the set of minimum conditions production can offer while remaining viable. So a sow in a stall where she can barely turn, a piglet castrated without anesthesia and a bird shackled while conscious can all be "within welfare standards". The term ends up reassuring sellers and buyers more than it describes how the animal is doing. A simple test is to swap the subject: if a person were kept for weeks in a space where they couldn't turn around, or operated on without anesthesia for the convenience of whoever kept them, no one would call it welfare; they would call it abuse. The test doesn't assume animals and humans need the same things; it only assumes that being able to move and not feeling avoidable pain are needs both have, and asks why the word changes meaning when the victim belongs to another species.

**7. Cruelty laws rarely reach standard practices.** Many anti-cruelty laws punish unnecessary or gratuitous cruelty and leave out what the industry considers normal practice, such as castration, beak trimming and confinement. Regulation can reduce some suffering, but it doesn't touch the harm of killing.

**8. Tougher laws coexist with more animals bred and killed.** The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane noted in 2010 that welfare laws were getting tougher at the same time as animals were being bred and killed like never before (Cochrane, 2010, p. 2). Current FAO data, compiled by Our World in Data, show the trend: world meat supply per person went from about 23 kg a year in 1961 to about 39 kg in 2007 and 45 kg in 2023, almost double. Some rich regions have seen a recent fall, such as the European Union (from about 85 kg in 2007 to 79 kg in 2023), but consumption remains high. The United Kingdom is a good example: it had the first modern animal protection law, Martin's Act of 1822, banned veal crates in 1990 and sow stalls in 1999, doesn't allow foie gras production and adopted in 2006 a law imposing a duty of care on owners. Even so, intensive farming continued, and meat supply per person in the country went from about 69 kg in 1961 to 84 kg in 2023 (Cochrane, 2010, p. 75). Better laws reduce some suffering, but they haven't changed the scale of use.

**9. Changing the law doesn't guarantee changing practice.** One lesson Cochrane draws from Marxism, without subscribing to it, is that legal change doesn't equal effective change. The sociologist Ted Benton argues that formal rights count for little against the economic power of agribusiness and the pharmaceutical industry, and Cochrane concludes that even strong laws, or legal rights for animals, only work alongside changes in society's norms, institutions and attitudes (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 112, 140–141). It's one more reason not to take the existence of laws as a guarantee.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: the rules are improving

The most serious version admits that current rules allow bad practices, but says they are advancing, that many countries went from almost nothing to specific rules in a few decades, and that demanding everything at once would make production unviable.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Progressing isn't arriving.** The rules improving confirms that they don't yet guarantee welfare.
2. **The pace is the industry's.** Transition periods of 10 to 25 years mean millions of animals living, in the meantime, at a standard the rules themselves recognize as inadequate.
3. **The improvement doesn't reach the central question.** No welfare rule answers whether it is just to breed and kill animals for a pleasure that can be had another way.

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Wouldn't accept this treatment for their own dog.** Even if a rule allowed it.
2. **Distrusts rules in other sectors.** No one thinks a weak environmental law proves pollution is acceptable.
3. **Thinks castration without anesthesia is already banned.** And is surprised to learn where it is still legal.

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "what the rules allow is acceptable".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Old labor laws | Exhausting hours allowed at the time would have been acceptable | They were corrected precisely because they were unacceptable |
| Weak environmental rules | The pollution allowed would be harmless | No one accepts this |
| Gestation stalls | They would be acceptable until a deadline, and unacceptable the next day | Shows the rule marks an agreement, not a moral fact |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Welfare rules are important and bring real progress. Rules that ban the worst forms of confinement reduce the suffering of millions of animals, and the people who worked for them made a difference. It's also true that inspections exist, and that the regulated sector is usually better than the unregulated one.

But a rule marks the minimum society agreed to demand at a given moment, not what is good for the animal. It regulates how animals are used and killed, and leaves unanswered the question of whether that should happen.
