# L23. "Vegans are against religion"

**Thesis:** it's a false generalization. There are vegans and vegetarians in every religion, and whole religious traditions teach not harming animals. Veganism answers a single question, how to treat animals, and so it is compatible with faith and with the absence of faith. The objection is right on one point: some vegans attack religions, and criticism of halal or kosher slaughter sometimes serves as a pretext for Islamophobia and antisemitism. That is wrong. The consistent criterion criticizes all slaughter, not only other people's.

**Family:** L. Attacks on veganism and vegans · **Part:** IND

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Vegans are all atheists. You don't like Christians or Jesus, and you want to put veganism in the place of religion."

**Strongest version (steelman):**

1. **P1.** Some vegans treat faith with disdain and attack religious practices, such as ritual slaughter and the meals of holy days.
2. **P2.** Veganism proposes rules for living, talks about guilt and conversion, and so competes with religion.
3. **P3.** A movement hostile to my faith doesn't deserve a hearing from believers.
4. **C.** So religious people can reject veganism.

P1 is true of some vegans. P2 confuses a moral position on one issue with a religion (L7). The bigger problem is the move from "some" to "veganism": millions of religious people don't eat animals precisely because of their faith.

The argument is **valid**. Its **soundness** fails at P2 and in the generalization of P1.

## 3. Answer for children

Some people who don't eat animals go to church, to the mosque, to the synagogue or to the temple. And some people who don't eat animals have no religion. Caring for animals goes with any of those choices. In some religions, not hurting animals is even one of the most important teachings.

## 4. Short answer

I have nothing against your faith, and neither does veganism. It only answers one question: how to treat animals. There are Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and Buddhist vegans, and vegans with no religion. In India, 92% of Jains say they are vegetarian, because of a religious principle of nonviolence. If a vegan disrespected your faith, they were wrong, and that's on them.

**Question to ask back:** "Does your religion ask you to be indifferent to the suffering of animals, or does it ask for compassion?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

The argument treats veganism as if it were a competing religious worldview:

| Question | Does a religion answer it? | Does veganism answer it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does God exist? What comes after death? | Yes | No |
| What is the meaning of life? How should we pray? | Yes | No |
| How should we treat animals? | Yes, each in its own way | Yes |

Veganism and religions only meet on the third row. And there, many traditions reach answers close to the vegan one.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. There are vegans and vegetarians in every religion.** A Pew Research Center survey published in 2021 found that 39% of adults in India say they are vegetarian: 92% of Jains, 59% of Sikhs, 44% of Hindus, 25% of Buddhists, 10% of Christians and 8% of Muslims. Outside India, the Seventh-day Adventist Church doesn't require it, but many of its members follow vegetarian or entirely plant-based diets.

**2. There are religious organizations dedicated to animals.** Among Christians, the Christian Vegetarian Association and the UK-based Sarx; among Jews, the Jewish Vegetarian Society; among Muslims, the Vegan Muslim Initiative; among Buddhists, Dharma Voices for Animals. The Animal Interfaith Alliance brings together organizations of several faiths around the same issue. None of them sees a contradiction between believing and not eating animals.

**3. Veganism has no doctrine about God.** The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding exploitation of and cruelty to animals, as far as is possible and practicable. There is nothing in it about religious beliefs. That's why veganism has room for believers and atheists, like any moral cause.

**4. Criticizing a practice isn't being against the faith.** Traditions themselves change and debate their customs: the guide shows, in D10, D11 and D12, that Christianity, Judaism and Islam have teachings against the unnecessary suffering of animals, and that permitting meat is not requiring it.

**5. Selective criticism of religious slaughter is something else, and it's wrong.** Bans on kosher slaughter have been a tool of antisemitic campaigns: Switzerland banned it in 1893, after a referendum preceded by an antisemitic campaign, and Germany banned it in 1933, three months after Hitler came to power. Today, a person who is outraged only by halal or kosher slaughter and eats meat from an ordinary slaughterhouse is using animals as a pretext. The debate over slaughter with or without stunning is legitimate, but the antispeciesist criterion goes beyond it: all slaughter kills an animal who didn't want to die (B1), and the majority's slaughter is what kills almost all of them.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: veganism demands changing practices my faith holds sacred

The most serious version says: this isn't about atheism. In some traditions, sacrifice, the Passover lamb or the Feast of Sacrifice are part of worship. An ethic that condemns those practices condemns, in practice, the religion of those who keep them.

### (e) Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

1. **Disagreeing with a practice isn't rejecting the whole faith.** Believers disagree among themselves about practices of their own tradition all the time, and remain believers. D12 covers this point in more detail.
2. **The disagreement applies to everyone, religious or not.** Veganism asks the same question of the barbecue of someone with no religion. There is no religious target.
3. **Ritual consumption is a small part.** Almost everything of animal origin that people eat comes from daily consumption, which none of the major religions requires.

### (f) Back to the moral question

The faith of the person asking and the lack of faith of the person answering change nothing for the animal. The animal feels pain and fear all the same. The moral question can be asked inside any religion and outside all of them: is it right to make someone who feels suffer and die, when it can be avoided? Many traditions have already answered no.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in L23 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Hasty generalization | Judges all vegans by those who attack religions |
| Logical | False dilemma | Either faith, or compassion for animals |
| Epistemological | Unfamiliarity with the traditions | Ignores whole religious currents that don't eat animals |
| Moral | Changing the subject | The messenger's faith takes the animal's place |

## 8. Contradictions

1. **Says veganism is against religion.** But lives alongside whole religions that teach not harming animals.
2. **Defends compassion as a religious value.** And refuses it when someone applies it to animals.
3. **Doesn't say eating meat is an atheist thing.** Although there are also atheists who eat meat and attack religions.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains L23 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |

| Motivated reasoning | The religious permission is remembered; the teachings of compassion, forgotten | Linked to D10 and D12 |

## 10. Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if some defenders of an idea attack religion, the idea is against religion".

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Science | It would be against religion, because some scientists attack faith | There are many religious scientists |
| Human rights | They would be against religion, because some advocates criticize churches | Many churches defend them |
| Veganism | It would be against religion | The same mistake |

## 11. What the argument gets right

It's right that some vegans treat other people's faith with contempt, use sacred texts as a weapon or mock believers. That is disrespectful, and it pushes away people who would have, in their own religion, good reasons to care about animals. It's also right that criticism of halal and kosher slaughter is sometimes used by people who only want to attack Muslims and Jews, and that animal advocates need to refuse that company.

But this describes the mistakes of people, not veganism. Not harming animals is an ancient teaching of several religions, and a conclusion reached today by believers in all of them. People of faith don't have to choose between it and the animals.
