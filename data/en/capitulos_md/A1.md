# A1. "Animals aren't rational"

**Thesis:** rationality isn't what makes someone capable of being harmed. The argument fails on three levels: it uses a criterion morally irrelevant to what's at stake (suffering), it's inaccurate about animal minds and, applied consistently, it would strip moral protection from millions of human beings.

**Family:** A. Sentience and moral status · **Part:** DIR

## 1. Formulation

**Everyday version:** "Animals aren't rational, so there's no problem using them."

**Strongest version (steelman).** In the philosophical tradition, the argument comes from Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, Descartes and Kant; today it appears, for example, in the contractualism of Peter Carruthers. Charitably reconstructed, it goes like this:

1. **P1.** Only rational beings (capable of abstract reasoning, deliberation and self-reflection) have full moral status, that is, interests that create direct duties for us.
2. **P2.** Non-human animals aren't rational in this sense.
3. **C1.** So non-human animals don't have full moral status.
4. **P3.** If a being lacks full moral status, it's permissible to use it for important human ends, provided there's no gratuitous cruelty.
5. **C2.** So it's permissible to use animals for food, clothing and research.

There's also a **moderate version**: rationality wouldn't be a condition for having some moral status, but would give human interests much greater weight. Both versions are dealt with in this chapter.

The argument is **valid**: if the premises were true, the conclusions would follow. The problem lies in its **soundness**: P1, P2 and P3 are false or lack sufficient justification.

## 3. Answer for children

Imagine your friend falls and scrapes their knee. They cry because it hurts, not because they can do sums. It's the same with a dog, a pig or a chicken: they also feel pain, fear and joy, even though they can't read. A baby can't do sums yet either, and no one thinks it's right to hurt them. To know whether we can hurt someone, the question isn't "are they clever?" but "can they feel?".

## 4. Short answer

The moral question isn't whether a being can reason, but whether it can suffer. A pig's pain doesn't hurt less because the pig can't solve equations. If rationality were the criterion, babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities could also be used, and no one accepts that. Besides, the divide "rational humans, irrational animals" is false: crows make hooks to get food, and fish work out social hierarchies just by watching other fish.

**Question to ask back:** "If there were a human being as little rational as a pig, would it be acceptable to raise them for food? If not, what really protects that human?"

## 5. Full answer

### (a) Reconstruction and diagnosis

As we've seen, the argument is formally valid. So it has to be assessed premise by premise. Three points give way: the normative criterion (P1), the empirical claim (P2) and the bridge between status and use (P3). Before them comes an earlier problem: the word "rational" is ambiguous, and the ambiguity creates a dilemma.

**The dilemma of ambiguity.** "Rational" can have two senses:

- **Strong sense:** abstract reasoning, propositional language, the ability to reflect on one's own reasons.
- **Weak sense:** learning, making inferences, solving new problems and adjusting behavior to goals.

[[ARVORE]]

There's no sense of "rational" that makes P1 and P2 true at the same time without excluding humans whom the speaker protects. Those who use the argument usually switch between the two senses without noticing: the strong one to exclude animals, the weak one to include all humans.

### (b) Where the argument breaks

**1. The criterion is irrelevant to what's at stake (P1).** A characteristic only justifies different treatment if it relates to the interest affected. Requiring political understanding in order to vote can be debated; requiring political understanding in order not to be beaten is absurd. Rationality is relevant to some interests, such as political autonomy and moral responsibility, but not to the interest in not feeling pain, fear or deprivation. Jeremy Bentham summed this up in 1789: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

From here comes the sentientist foundation. Having interests means being able to be affected, for better or worse, from the point of view of whoever lives the experience. A stone has no interests; a pig does. That's why sentience is a necessary and sufficient condition for moral inclusion, and the principle of equal consideration of interests (Singer) requires that similar pains count similarly. The criterion is also the most **parsimonious**: a single trait explains why we protect adults, babies, people with dementia and, already today, dogs and cats.

**2. Species overlap (P1 against humans themselves).** The argument is old: it appears in Porphyry, in the third century, and in Bentham. Today it's called the argument from species overlap (Horta, 2014), a name replacing the older "argument from marginal cases". In the form of *modus tollens*:

1. **Q1.** If rationality is necessary for full moral status, then humans without rationality don't have full moral status.
2. **Q2.** Humans without rationality (newborns, people with profound intellectual disabilities, people with advanced dementia) have full moral status.
3. **C.** So rationality isn't necessary for full moral status.

Q2 is accepted by the speaker. The argument doesn't propose downgrading these humans: it shows the criterion is wrong and that the moral community should be widened, not narrowed (Naconecy). The usual escape routes have their own chapters: the "species norm" (A11) judges individuals by what others have; "potential" (A12) doesn't cover those who will never have rationality; "special relationships" (A13) make protection depend on someone caring.

**3. The empirical premise is a caricature (P2).** As early as 1871, Darwin held that the mental difference between humans and other animals is one of degree, not kind. Research in animal cognition has confirmed this gradual picture (see the table in c). An honest caveat is needed here: citing animal intelligence helps undo the false "rational versus irrational" divide, but using it as the **foundation** is a trap. It would mean accepting the speaker's criterion and concluding that chickens or fish, supposedly less intelligent, matter less. The argument against exploitation doesn't depend on animals being intelligent; it depends on their being sentient.

**4. The bridge between status and use doesn't hold (P3).** Even in the moderate version, where rationality would give human interests more weight, the argument doesn't reach its conclusion. First, that extra weight would have to be enormous to outweigh intense, prolonged suffering, plus death, in exchange for taste and convenience, since there are alternatives (J11). Second, the "important human ends" of P3 are, in practice, mostly preferences, not needs. Third, in the Kantian version, duties toward animals are only indirect: cruelty would be wrong because it hardens human character. It follows that torturing a dog in secret, with no effect on anyone, wouldn't be wrong in itself, a conclusion almost no one accepts.

The overall picture is one of continuity, with capacities unevenly distributed among species. None of these data, however, is needed for the moral conclusion: the evidence of sentience, which is far more robust than the evidence of complex reasoning, is enough.

### (d) The strongest counter-reply: contractualism

The philosophically strongest version comes from contractualism, defended on this subject by Peter Carruthers (1992). Morality would be the set of rules that rational agents would accept in order to live together. Only those who can understand, accept and follow rules are party to the contract. Animals would be left out and would have, at most, indirect protection, because cruelty reveals bad character. Non-rational humans would be included for two reasons: the contractors care about them, and excluding them would create social insecurity, since anyone could come to lose their rationality. A close variant, Carl Cohen's, grants rights through membership of a species of moral agents and is dealt with in chapter A11.

### (e) Why the counter-reply also fails

1. **It confuses who makes the rules with whom the rules protect.** Moral agents are those who can have duties; moral patients are those who can be harmed. Babies are patients without being agents, and no one concludes that their protection is therefore a favor.
2. **It makes the protection of vulnerable humans contingent.** If a baby is protected only because the contractors care about them, then torturing an orphan nobody cares about wouldn't be wrong in itself. The theory fails precisely the test of our firmest moral judgments.
3. **The contract's own device points the other way.** Behind Rawls's veil of ignorance, rules are chosen without knowing what position one will occupy. Mark Rowlands (1997) observed that if we also didn't know whether we'd be human or some other sentient being, we'd choose rules protecting all sentient beings. Excluding animals only appears when the contract is designed by those who already have power, and power isn't justice (A2).
4. **Even if accepted, it doesn't justify the real practice.** Contractualism itself condemns cruelty; large-scale farming and slaughter systems, with confinement and mutilation, would hardly pass even that minimal filter.

## 7. Reasoning errors

| Level | Error | How it appears in A1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logical | Equivocation | "Rational" changes meaning between P1 and P2 |
| Logical | Non sequitur (irrelevance) | From the absence of abstract reasoning, permission to cause pain is inferred, though pain doesn't depend on that trait |
| Logical | Ad hoc criterion | The group to protect (humans) is chosen first and then the trait separating it, adjusting the criterion whenever a counterexample appears |
| Logical | Proving too much | If valid, it also authorizes using humans without rationality |
| Epistemological | False dichotomy | Treats as binary (rational or irrational) something the evidence shows to be gradual |
| Epistemological | Outdated science | Relies on the Cartesian image of the animal-machine, abandoned by neuroscience |
| Epistemological | Reversed burden of proof and misapplied parsimony | Assumes no mind until proven otherwise, when evolutionary continuity and shared neuroanatomy make the hypothesis of mind the simplest |
| Epistemological | Anthropocentric tests | Measures "rationality" with tasks designed for humans and ignores each species' own capacities |
| Moral | Criterion irrelevant to the interest | Uses a trait linked to autonomy to decide about pain and death |
| Moral | Violating equal consideration | Weighs similar pains differently because of species |
| Moral | Only indirect duties | Makes cruelty wrong only because of its effect on humans, not because of the victim |

## 8. Contradictions

Those who use A1 usually hold, at the same time, beliefs that contradict it:

1. **They protect dogs and cats**, which are no more rational than pigs, and are outraged at cruelty to them. Laws in the UK, the US, the EU and Brazil punish cruelty to animals, which presupposes that their suffering matters in itself.
2. **They grant full rights to babies** and to people with profound intellectual disabilities, without requiring rationality of them.
3. **They reject degrees of rationality among humans.** No one accepts that a Nobel Prize winner has more right not to be tortured than an ordinary person. If rationality doesn't grade protection within the species, it remains to be explained why it eliminates protection outside it.
4. **They say animals "understand nothing"**, but treat their own dog as someone who feels longing, fear and joy.

## 9. Biases

| Bias | How it sustains A1 | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivated denial of mind (meat paradox) | Those about to eat an animal tend to attribute less mind to it, which reduces moral discomfort | Bastian et al., 2012; effect replicated, somewhat smaller, by [Jacobs et al., 2024](https://rips-irsp.com/articles/10.5334/irsp.932) |
| Effect of consumption on moral status | Eating meat leads to attributing less moral status to the animal eaten | Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010 |
| Functional categorization | Classifying an animal as "food" reduces the perception that it suffers | [Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21569805/) |
| Motivated reasoning | A trait is sought that justifies a practice one already wants to keep | General literature on motivated cognition |
| Self-serving bias | The criterion chosen is exactly the one adult humans score best on | A known pattern in judgments of desert |
| Anthropocentric measurement | Judges animal minds by human tasks, not by each species' capacities | Frans de Waal, *Are We Smart Enough to Know How Smart Animals Are?* (2016) |

## 10. Extrapolations

The universalization test applies the principle "whoever isn't rational may be used" to other cases. If the person rejects the consequences, they must abandon or reformulate the principle.

| Applied to | What would follow | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newborns | They could undergo painful procedures without anesthesia. A newborn doesn't reason, speak or reflect on themselves. If protection from pain depends on rationality, they'd have no right to it, and the fact that they feel pain would be irrelevant | Not just hypothetical: until the 1980s, surgery on newborns was performed with minimal anesthesia in the belief that they didn't really feel pain, a practice revised after Anand and Hickey (1987) |
| People with profound intellectual disabilities or advanced dementia | They could be used as test subjects or organ sources. These people will never have, or have lost, the rationality the criterion demands. The criterion offers no reason to protect them, and the care we give them would stop being a duty and become a favor | A conclusion rejected by practically everyone |
| Hierarchies among humans | The appeal to capacities wouldn't only decide who deserves respect, but also how much. If what matters is rationality or intelligence, and it varies in degree, there'd be a moral scale among humans: a person with a doctorate would be worth more than someone who can barely read, and their interests would take priority in a conflict | This is exactly what happened: Aristotle attributed incomplete reason to "natural slaves" and women, and colonial discourse called whole peoples "irrational" to justify domination |
| Artificial intelligence or extraterrestrials | A being far more rational than us could use us as we use animals. If superior rationality gives the right to use the less rational, the distance between us and that being would justify the same treatment we give a pig today, and we'd have nothing to complain about | Whoever rejects this conclusion is, in practice, appealing to sentience |
| Cruelty in secret | Torturing an animal without witnesses would only be bad because of the habit it creates. In the Kantian version, the animal isn't a victim: the duty is only to humanity. If no one knew and the torturer went on treating people well, there'd be nothing wrong at all | Contradicts the common intuition that the wrong lies in what is done to the victim |

## 11. What the argument gets right

Rationality does have moral importance, but elsewhere. It's relevant to goods such as autonomy, political rights and responsibility: animals aren't moral agents, and a lion commits no crime by hunting. Cognitive differences can also change **what** harms each being; a being with long-term plans may lose more by dying (McMahan's time-relative interest account, 2002). Finally, equal consideration isn't identical treatment: a pig doesn't need the right to vote.

What the argument can't show is the decisive step: rationality can adjust the **content** of our duties, but it doesn't decide **who is in** the moral circle. That role belongs to sentience.
